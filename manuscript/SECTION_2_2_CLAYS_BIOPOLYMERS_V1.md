## 2.2. Natural clays and biopolymers

Natural clay minerals and biopolymers offer two different routes to adsorbent design. Clay studies in this corpus exploit mineral surfaces, interlayer exchange sites and the effects of pH and competing ions. Biopolymer studies use materials such as chitosan and alginate in powder or bead form, while more engineered systems combine polymers with clay, carbon or metal-containing structures. The comparison must keep the material itself separate from its physical form and processing history: raw clay powder, acid-activated bentonite, purified stevensite, a calcined clay monolith and a clay–alginate bead are not equivalent versions of one material class.

These materials span a processing continuum rather than a simple “natural equals cheap” category. Washing, sieving and drying can be modest preparation steps; acid/base activation, ion exchange, size fractionation, calcination, polymer crosslinking, composite synthesis and solvent-based regeneration add reagents, water, energy and secondary waste. The papers rarely quantify all of these inputs per unit of usable sorbent. Accordingly, a higher reported adsorption metric is evidence of a performance change under the test conditions, not by itself proof of lower treatment cost or a better life-cycle outcome.

The experimental evidence is heterogeneous. Tests often use mg/L or high µmol/L feeds, synthetic water and batch vessels; some studies add spiked water matrices or laboratory columns, but these are not the same as treatment of native, trace-contaminated wastewater. Percentage removal, observed uptake, fitted isotherm capacity, capacity retained after regeneration and dynamic column capacity are kept separate below.

### 2.2.1. Natural clay minerals

**Natural sepiolite and montmorillonite: mineral identity matters.** Zhou et al. compared natural sepiolite (Sep) and montmorillonite (Mt) for tetracycline (TC) adsorption. At 25 mg/L TC, pH 7 and a dose of 1 g/L, observed uptake at 298 K was 11.252 mg/g for Sep and 20.736 mg/g for Mt; the approximate time to equilibrium was 240 minutes for Sep and 120 minutes for Mt. These are observed kinetic-test values. In a separate isotherm series (10–120 mg/L, pH 7, 1 g/L, 24 h), the fitted Langmuir maximum at 298 K was 33.592 mg/g for Sep and 213.273 mg/g for Mt. The fitted values should not replace the lower observed uptakes from the 25 mg/L experiment. The study therefore supports a within-study distinction between these two minerals, not an across-paper capacity ranking. ([DISC-002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seppur.2024.127767); evidence EV-B04-014–EV-B04-017)

The same paper tested microwave (MW) regeneration. Under selected settings, the authors reported regeneration ratios of 81.83% for Sep after 25 minutes and 98.83% for Mt after 20 minutes. In the cycle series, the reported ratio fell to 65.21% for Sep and 66.38% for Mt by cycle 8. These are ratios of adsorption capacity after treatment to the reference capacity, not the percentage of TC desorbed. The treatment was conducted at nominal 750 W, but the paper does not provide a measured, mass-normalized energy demand, electricity cost, real-wastewater trial or life-cycle assessment. MW reuse is thus a promising laboratory route, but its process advantage remains unquantified. The paper also found stronger inhibition from Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ than from Na⁺/K⁺; a good result in simple solution should not be presumed to transfer unchanged to every water matrix.

**Raw and activated clays: activation is not a universal improvement.** Hamdi et al. compared three Tunisian clays in raw form and after HCl or Na₂CO₃ treatment. Preparation of the raw materials involved suspension, sieving and drying; the activated variants also required chemicals, heating, washing/settling and drying. In batch tests spanning 25–1000 µmol/L TC (approximately 11–444 mg/L), raw clays generally showed adsorption close to 100% at the higher additions, with a maximum observed amount of 12,672.7 µmol/kg at 1000 µmol/L. Acid/base activation did not consistently increase adsorption and could reduce it at the lower concentrations. This within-study finding is important for the processing–cost continuum: extra treatment should not be assumed beneficial just because it modifies the surface. ([CORP-008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2023.117536); evidence EV-B17-009–EV-B17-013)

The same study reported desorption below 10%, and mostly below 4%, in a single CaCl₂ release step. This indicates low release under that specific batch test; it does not demonstrate multi-cycle regeneration, antibiotic destruction or safe spent-clay disposal. The authors did not report a measured production cost, wash-water treatment, process yield or life-cycle inventory. The tested TC concentrations were also much higher than typical trace environmental levels. The result supports testing raw clay before committing to activation, but it does not establish treatment performance in native wastewater.

**Acid-activated bentonite: a stronger batch response with practical penalties.** Maged et al. compared purified natural bentonite (NB) with acid-activated bentonite (AAB) for ciprofloxacin (CIP). The starting bentonite was dried, ground and sieved, then lab-purified through repeated washing and reagent treatment; AAB underwent additional acid activation, heating and washing. BET surface area more than doubled after activation. In the paper’s synthetic-solution tests, the reported maximum capacities were approximately 126.56 mg/g for NB and 305.20 mg/g for AAB, with experimental values of 126.53 and 300.03 mg/g respectively. AAB reached its selected equilibrium response at about 0.3 g/L, compared with about 0.7 g/L for NB, and the study reported that roughly 80% of uptake occurred in the first 30 minutes, with equilibrium within about 120 minutes. These are useful within-study activation contrasts; they do not establish that acid activation is cost-effective for other clays or matrices. ([DISC-014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-020-09267-1); evidence EV-B19-006–EV-B19-010)

The matrix and deployment tests qualify the batch result. Raising NaCl from 0 to 0.5 M reduced removal from 59.8% to 16.1% for NB and from 95.3% to 75.5% for AAB. Regeneration used 0.1 M NaOH or HCl over five adsorption–desorption cycles; NaOH gave better reuse results, but the acid/base consumption, washing demand and spent eluate were not assessed in a full process inventory. In a laboratory column, AAB at 50 mg, 20 mg/L CIP and 1.5 mL/min showed 37.99% removal in the first cycle, with reported bed capacity of 97.12 mg/g. The source table's three-cycle capacity sum (187.11 mg/g) conflicts with the conclusion's 178.11 mg/g, so the multi-cycle total must remain flagged. The comparison illustrates a central design trade-off: higher batch capacity can coexist with salinity sensitivity, regeneration inputs and a more modest continuous-flow removal result.

**Purified stevensite: fast removal, but capacity figures are not ready for ranking.** García-Criado et al. evaluated a purified, size-fractionated stevensite-rich clay for tetracyclines and metabolites. The preparation used washing, HCl to remove carbonate, NaCl treatment to convert the clay to a sodium form, separation of particles below 2 µm and drying. The reported BET area was 147 m²/g, with pore volume 0.122 cm³/g and mean pore diameter 3.32 nm. The paper reports rapid uptake and high removal across several tetracyclines, including >99% removal under its selected acidic-pH conditions; it also tests tap water, surface water and wastewater matrices spiked at 2 mg/L. Those are spiked-matrix results, not proof of removal from untreated water at native trace concentrations. The paper's initial-concentration labels are inconsistent across the abstract and methods, and some pH conditions may permit tetracycline transformation as well as adsorption. ([DISC-018](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics14040395); evidence EV-B09-008–EV-B09-015)

The fitted-capacity claims must be quarantined. The narrative gives an extreme qmax value above 200,000 mg/g in one passage, while Table 2 lists values such as 1000 mg/g and the kinetic experimental uptakes are around 0.983–1.575 mg/g under the low-concentration design. The paper generally reports Freundlich as the better fit. The extreme narrative value is not physically credible as stated, and the table's fitted maxima also require clarification against the actual experiment. The study reports more than 90% capacity retention after five adsorption–desorption cycles, using a pH-4 McIlvaine-buffer/acetone mixture. That reuse result is relevant, but regenerant solvent recovery, concentrated eluate treatment, cost and life-cycle burdens were not quantified.

**Clay structured as a monolith: recovery and handling are part of performance.** Sumariva et al. selected Esquivias kerolitic montmorillonite from five natural clays and extruded it into a honeycomb monolith, followed by calcination at 400 °C for two hours. The material was designed to be recovered and handled more easily than fine powder. Tests reported up to 69% TC removal after three days of recirculation at 50 mL/min. Other configurations reported 80% without forced circulation and 82% at a higher flow, but the exposure times, air/light conditions and volumes differed; the authors explicitly treated those configurations as not directly comparable. The feed was high (about 231 mg/L in a representative run), and the recirculation test is not a once-through, trace-level wastewater column demonstration. ([DISC-003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jwpe.2024.106381); evidence EV-B07-001–EV-B07-005)

The monolith paper also considered heating the spent material to decompose retained TC. That post-use treatment is distinct from adsorption and should not be counted as additional adsorption removal. The available evidence does not establish a complete transformation-product/toxicity mass balance or a harmonized energy, yield, cost or life-cycle result for making and reusing the monolith. The broader lesson is that shaping a powder into a recoverable structure can improve handling and reactor integration, but calcination and final contaminant management must be included in the process boundary.

**Montmorillonite and ciprofloxacin: stronger mechanistic evidence does not imply deployment proof.** Wang et al. studied ciprofloxacin interaction with Ca²⁺-dominated montmorillonite at very high starting concentrations (500–4000 mg/L). They reported about 330 mg/g as the maximum/model capacity; at a stated 3000 mg/L condition, the residual concentration of 80 mg/L corresponds to approximately 292 mg/g observed uptake. Ion-release data provided relatively direct evidence for cation exchange: the relationship between total released cations and CIP uptake had a slope near 0.99 and R²=0.9935, with Ca²⁺ the main released cation. XRD basal spacing and FTIR changes were also used to support the authors’ proposed interlayer interaction and molecular-orientation model. Uptake remained around 290–300 mg/g from pH 2.8 to 8.8 and fell to 247 mg/g at pH 11. ([DISC-015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhazmat.2010.07.025); evidence EV-B03-006–EV-B03-010)

The cation-release result is stronger mechanistic evidence than assigning a mechanism solely from a good pseudo-second-order fit. Nevertheless, the experiments were batch tests at concentrations far above trace wastewater levels, with no real-water, regeneration, continuous-flow, cost or life-cycle assessment. The reported mechanism can explain the specific montmorillonite–CIP system; it is not a universal mechanism for every antibiotic–clay pair.

**Nano-treated montmorillonite: processing may change performance, but internal consistency comes first.** Shi et al. prepared a nano-montmorillonite derivative using acid/solvent and heated treatment steps. The material is therefore not an untreated clay reference. The article reports a BET area of 247 m²/g and average particle size of 364 nm, and proposes interlayer intercalation/cation exchange and electrostatic attraction. However, the methods state 0.03 g clay in 50 mL solution (0.6 g/L), while the conclusion gives an optimum dose of 0.03 g/L. The paper also gives three different maximum-related quantities: 500 mg/g in the results discussion, 526 mg/g in the abstract/conclusion and a fitted Langmuir qmax of 558.184 mg/g. If 120 mg/L is the initial concentration for a 50 mL test with 0.03 g sorbent, the mass-balance upper limit is 200 mg/g. The headline values must therefore not enter a capacity ranking until the conditions are reconciled. No real wastewater, column operation, repeated regeneration, solvent recovery, cost or life-cycle assessment was reported in the accessible main text. ([CORP-025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.108952); evidence EV-AUD-001–EV-AUD-005)

### 2.2.2. Unmodified biopolymers: adsorption chemistry and physical form

**Separate chitosan and alginate beads for amoxicillin.** Pinheiro et al. prepared chitosan and alginate as separate bead adsorbents and reported an optimum pH of 4 for both. At that pH, the published abstract confirms capacities of 80.4 ± 0.2 mg/g for chitosan and 74.2 ± 0.3 mg/g for alginate. The feed in the reported pH/capacity experiment was 300 mg/L amoxicillin, so these values are not low-concentration wastewater capacities. The material-form distinction is important: the paper prepared separate chitosan and alginate bead types, not a mixed chitosan–alginate composite. Alginate beads were reported to dissolve above pH 6, a limitation for applications that require near-neutral pH. ([CORP-023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-024-35293-4); evidence EV-B20-005, EV-AUD-006)

The full PDF currently represented in the evidence record is the preprint; only the headline pH-4 capacities have been cross-checked against the published abstract. The preprint's detailed tables and model fits should not be silently treated as fully verified published-version data. In particular, the preprint reports a fitted Weber–Morris asymptote for alginate of 136.75 mg/g while its text gives kinetic uptake of 71.3 mg/g, and the published abstract reverses the external-/internal-diffusion assignments stated in the preprint. These details must be checked against the full published article before the mechanistic claims are used. The study did not demonstrate native wastewater treatment, a fixed-bed test or repeated regeneration in the evidence reviewed here.

### 2.2.3. Modified biopolymers and biopolymer-based composites

**Halloysite/molecularly imprinted alginate composites for tetracycline.** Kurczewska and Cegłowski compared alginate beads with halloysite- and molecularly-imprinted-polymer-containing variants. BET area increased from 7.08 m²/g for plain alginate to 42.04 m²/g for the ALG_Hal@MIP formulation. The authors report higher adsorption for the composite formulations and >80% of initial efficiency remaining for composite alginate beads after five cycles, compared with about 63% for plain alginate. These values illustrate a potential trade-off: adding mineral and imprinted-polymer components can change available surface and reuse behaviour, but also increases synthesis complexity. ([CORP-013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.142000); evidence EV-B11-001–EV-B11-006)

Numerical capacity claims from this paper require restraint. At 500 mg/L TC, 10 mL solution and 20 mg beads, the simple mass-balance maximum is 250 mg/g; the reported experimental q value of 277.6 mg/g exceeds that bound. An abstract-level “observed maximum” of 281 mg/g and other mixture/spiked-water values require the same condition check. The paper generally prefers Freundlich for the composite beads, so Langmuir qmax should be labelled as a fitted model parameter rather than a measured uptake. The spiked-water tests also used 500 mg/L TC, which does not establish performance at trace-level concentrations. Its soil-spiking test at 0.2 or 0.5 mg/L is a different evidence stream: alginate generally outperformed chitosan, while halloysite-containing beads reduced TC desorption from the soil, but this is laboratory soil behaviour rather than field validation.

**Kaolin–alginate beads: the clearest bridge from powder to recoverable sorbent.** Liu et al. compared pristine kaolin (KN), calcium-crosslinked alginate–kaolin beads (KN@Alg) and activated carbon (AC) for chlortetracycline (CTC). At a dose of 4 g/L, the reported removal efficiencies were 55.04% for KN and 68.44% for KN@Alg. Under the selected isotherm condition, observed uptake was 42.76 mg/g for KN and 68.74 mg/g for KN@Alg; AC gave 102.96 mg/g in its corresponding test, but its isotherm/kinetic dose was 0.8 g/L rather than 4 g/L. These are not equal-dose material rankings. The polymer formulation improved on the pristine clay under the matched clay/bead comparison and provided a bead that was easier to handle than dispersed powder. ([DISC-007](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.250439); evidence EV-B19-001–EV-B19-005)

This study provides some of the more deployment-oriented evidence in the section. For water spiked at 5 mg/L CTC, the authors reported 94.2% removal in tap water and 93.7% in river water after the reported SPE protocol; a continuous column reported 93.6% removal over 20 bed volumes. These are useful laboratory demonstrations in spiked water, not proof of removal from native contamination at environmental trace concentrations. KN@Alg showed a 14.19% decline in CTC removal efficiency over four cycles, compared with larger declines for pristine kaolin and AC in the paper's comparison. The paper reported an estimated material cost of US$0.095/g for KN@Alg versus US$0.15/g for AC, but the electricity-price unit is unusual and needs verification; the estimate is not a full plant-scale techno-economic analysis or life-cycle assessment. The kinetic table also gives a pseudo-second-order calculated qe of 144.307 mg/g for KN@Alg, more than twice the experimental equilibrium uptake of 68.700 mg/g, while that fit has R²=0.838. That fitted value should not be used as observed capacity or as standalone proof of chemisorption.

**Magnetic Se-MOF embedded in chitosan/alginate.** Guesmi et al. reported a magnetic selenium-containing metal–organic-framework composite embedded in chitosan/alginate beads for ciprofloxacin (CIP). The synthesis includes Fe₃O₄ precipitation, Se-MOF preparation in methanol, CaCl₂ crosslinking and epichlorohydrin treatment. The article reports a fitted Langmuir qₘ of 442.4 mg/g and an experimental qₘ of 439.2 mg/g; removal exceeded 95% below 100 mg/L initial CIP but fell to about 62% at 600 mg/L. After five cycles, the reported retained capacity was 82.5%. ([CORP-007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.144274); evidence EV-B11-007–EV-B11-011)

This is a more engineered hybrid than unmodified chitosan or alginate. Its performance data should be reported with the model distinction intact and the preparation burden visible: the source does not provide yield, solvent recovery, a quantified chemical/energy inventory, Se/Fe leaching evaluation or life-cycle analysis. The Box–Behnken table also labels its response “Yield (mmol/g)” although other sections report capacity in mg/g. The wastewater experiments were described as “recovery” after spiking at 10, 50 and 100 mg/L; retain the paper’s endpoint term rather than silently relabel it as removal efficiency.

**Carbon–chitosan beads as an immobilization trade-off.** Yaqubi et al. acid-leached carbon black waste and incorporated the material into chitosan beads for tetracycline and amoxicillin. The powder had a reported surface area around 375 m²/g in the main results, while the conclusion gives about 275 m²/g; the beads had a surface area around 20 m²/g, were highly hydrated and contained about 20.5 mg leached carbon per gram of composite bead. The composite therefore improved physical handling but diluted the carbon fraction and reduced the measured surface area per gram of bead. In distilled/tap-water tests, powder TC removal was 68%/80% and bead TC removal 92%/87%; reported empirical capacities were 181/205 mg/g for powdered carbon and 39/38 mg/g for beads. For amoxicillin, powder capacities were 42/65 mg/g and bead capacities 12/15 mg/g. The paper's dose wording is ambiguous after solution volume was doubled, so these figures should not be normalized beyond the source conditions without checking the methods. ([DISC-027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jece.2020.104988); evidence EV-B05-013–EV-B05-018)

The bead study included a tap-water fixed-bed test. At 14.5 cm bed height, the estimated time to C/C₀ = 0.1 was about 60 hours at 1 mL/min versus about 25 hours at 2 mL/min. However, the Thomas and Adams–Bohart model fits were judged unsatisfactory by the authors; the estimated 5.051–8.757 mg/g values are therefore not validated dynamic service capacities. The study did not include a regeneration campaign, measured unit production cost or full leaching/life-cycle assessment. This case shows why percentage removal and ease of recovery can move differently from per-gram capacity.

**Keep mixed adsorption–transformation systems outside a simple adsorption ranking.** Gopal and Mukherjee embedded Fe/Cu nanostructures in alginate–carboxymethyl-cellulose–chitosan beads for norfloxacin. The article reports norfloxacin disappearance alongside TOC reduction and transformation products, and attributes part of the result to catalytic transformation/mineralization. Its reported 86% removal and fitted Langmuir capacity of 354.9 ± 41 mg/g cannot be treated as pure adsorption performance without separating the adsorption contribution. The source also has conflicting bead-dose descriptions and a fitted kinetic qe that exceeds a mass-balance ceiling under one stated condition. This paper may inform discussion of hybrid treatment, but its mixed-process removal should not be included in an adsorption-only numerical table without independent adsorption attribution. ([DISC-006](https://doi.org/10.1039/d4ra01971g); evidence EV-B14-001–EV-B14-005)

### 2.2.4. Cross-cutting synthesis and implications for the continuum

Across the clay studies, **mineral identity and water chemistry** can matter as much as the nominal surface area. The matched sepiolite–montmorillonite study found higher observed TC uptake and faster equilibration for montmorillonite under its conditions; the bentonite study found a substantial increase after acid activation; but the Tunisian-clay comparison did not find a consistent benefit from HCl/Na₂CO₃ treatment. These findings do not conflict: they show that activation effects depend on feed mineralogy, target compound and the experimental system. A material's treatment history and test conditions must accompany any reported capacity.

The biopolymer examples show that **bead formation and composite architecture can solve handling problems without automatically maximizing per-gram uptake**. Kaolin–alginate beads improved on pristine kaolin in matched batch conditions and supported a laboratory column test, while carbon–chitosan beads were easier to handle but displayed lower composite-normalized capacity than the powder. The distinction between the amount of active sorbent and the total mass of wet or polymer-containing composite is therefore critical. A study that reports capacity per gram of a hydrated bead is not directly comparable with a gram of dry powder unless the denominator is aligned.

Third, **regeneration is a process step, not a free bonus**. Microwave regeneration consumes electricity; acid/base cycles consume chemicals and wash-water; acetone-containing eluents require solvent recovery or spent-stream treatment. Low desorption in a single release test may imply retention but can also transfer the disposal burden to spent adsorbent. None of the cited studies supplies a complete, harmonized inventory covering yield, chemicals, water, energy, adsorbent replacement, regeneration effluent and the fate of residual antibiotic.

Fourth, **mechanistic claims require evidence beyond a fitted kinetic model**. The montmorillonite–CIP cation-release relationship provides a more direct basis for cation exchange than a pseudo-second-order fit alone. XRD, FTIR, surface-charge and speciation results can support proposed interactions, but they do not automatically prove one mechanism dominates under all conditions. Cases where the source's diffusion assignments conflict between preprint and published abstract, or where fitted uptake exceeds experimental/mass-balance limits, should remain open rather than being silently harmonized.

Finally, **the strongest reported performance is often not the strongest evidence for deployment**. Many batch values use high initial concentrations. Spiked tap/river/wastewater tests are useful matrix screens but do not equal treatment of naturally contaminated water at trace concentration. The most useful comparisons therefore preserve multiple endpoints: observed uptake at a stated condition; fitted qmax; removal at a stated dose; reuse retention; dynamic column capacity or breakthrough; and preparation/regeneration burden. None should stand in for the others.

**Interim conclusion.** The evidence does not support a universal clay-versus-biopolymer winner. It supports a conditional design principle: start with the least-processed material that meets the target and matrix requirements, and add activation, polymer immobilization or composite engineering only when the benefit is demonstrated at comparable dose, concentration and endpoint. The next quantitative step is to validate a small set of matched comparisons and their process burdens, not to rank the largest capacity values across unrelated studies.
