# Manuscript Outline: Adsorptive Removal of Antibiotics from Wastewater (Processing & Cost Continuum)

## Title Framework Options

1. **Option A:** From Raw Agricultural Waste to Atomically Doped Biochars: A Processing and Cost Continuum for Adsorptive Antibiotic Removal

## Abstract — planned content

- **Environmental drivers:** Global presence of recalcitrant antibiotics (μg/L to mg/L) in municipal, industrial, and agricultural effluents, accelerating the spread of Antibiotic Resistance Genes (ARGs).
- **Structural continuum:** Systematic evaluation of eight adsorbent classes organized by processing complexity and cost: Raw Lignocellulosic Biomass, Natural Clays, Unmodified Biopolymers, Standard Biochar, Chemically Modified Biochars, Atomically Doped Biochars, Activated Carbon (AC), and Nano-Engineered Carbons (CNTs/GO).
- **Mechanistic spectrum:** Interfacial interactions including surface complexation, cation exchange, pore filling, π–π coupling, and atomically doped active-site resonance.
- **Techno-economic perspectives:** Comparative synthesis energy demands, normalized cost-per-mass removal capacity (USD/g antibiotic removed), reusability, and continuous-flow integration.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Occurrence, Toxicity, and Risks of Antibiotics in Water
- Pathways into water bodies: municipal wastewater, livestock runoffs, and pharmaceutical manufacturing discharges.
- Ecotoxicological threats to aquatic microflora and the proliferation of superbugs/ARGs.

### 1.2 Treatment Limitations & The Adsorption Solution
- Incomplete degradation via conventional biological treatment and high capital/operational costs of Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs).
- Adsorption as a low-footprint, flexible, and scalable unit operation.

### 1.3 The Processing & Cost Continuum Paradigm
- Moving beyond binary “low-cost vs. high-cost” labels: defining material feasibility via processing energy, synthesis complexity, and capacity-normalized cost (USD/g removed).
- Scope and organization of this review.

## 2. Adsorbent Classification along the Processing & Cost Continuum

The original outline organizes materials into four processing stages:

| Stage | Processing category | Materials shown in the original outline |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Raw / unrefined sorbents | Lignocellulosic biomass, including peels and husks |
| 2 | Extracted sorbents & mineral clays | Natural clays (e.g., bentonite) and unmodified biopolymers (e.g., chitosan) |
| 3 | Thermally converted | Standard biochar and chemically modified biochars (e.g., KOH / H₂SO₄) |
| 4 | Precision engineered & nano | Atomically doped biochars (Fe/N), standard activated carbon, and nano-carbons (CNTs/GO) |

### 2.1 Stage 1: Raw / Unrefined Sorbents (Minimal Processing & Direct Waste Use)

#### 2.1.1 Raw Lignocellulosic Biomass
- **Sorbent types:** Agricultural waste feedstocks (raw sawdust, fruit peels, rice husks).
- **Structural properties:** Natural polymer matrix dominated by lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose.
- **Active surface sites:** Native functional groups including hydroxyl (−OH), carbonyl (−C=O), methoxyl (−OCH₃), and phenolic groups.

### 2.2 Stage 2: Extracted Sorbents & Natural Mineral Clays (Low-to-Moderate Processing)

#### 2.2.1 Natural Clays (Bentonite)
- Phyllosilicate interlayer geometry and high Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC).
- Exchange process: naturally inherent low-valence cations (Al³⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) exchanged with protonated/cationic antibiotic molecules.

#### 2.2.2 Unmodified Biopolymers (Chitosan)
- Extraction from chitin (crustacean waste deacetylation).
- Baseline pharmaceutical capture driven by native amine (−NH₂) and hydroxyl (−OH) functional networks prior to chemical cross-linking.

### 2.3 Stage 3: Thermally Converted & Functionalized Biochars (Moderate Processing & Energy-Saving)

#### 2.3.1 Standard Biochar (BC)
- **Thermochemical conversion:** Oxygen-free pyrolysis (300–800 °C) or hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) using manure, leaves, and sawdust feedstocks.
- Porous carbon skeleton and baseline aromaticity.

#### 2.3.2 Chemically Modified Biochars
- **Surface area amplification:** KOH activation creating dense micropores.
- **Target-specific functionalization:** Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) surface modification tailored for enhanced uptake of specific classes such as sulfadiazine.

### 2.4 Stage 4: Precision Engineered & Nano-Carbons (High-Energy / High-Tech Benchmarks)

#### 2.4.1 Atomically Doped Biochars
- **Co-doping strategies:** Introducing heteroatoms such as iron (Fe) and nitrogen (N) into the biochar lattice.
- **Interfacial resonance:** The original outline proposes synergistic active-site density and faster mass-transfer/sorption kinetics relative to pristine carbon.

#### 2.4.2 Standard Activated Carbon (AC)
- **Historical industrial benchmark:** Highly developed microporosity and surface area.
- **Processing footprint:** High-temperature requirement (700–1000 °C) and thermal/chemical activation energy burdens.

#### 2.4.3 Nano-Engineered Carbons (CNTs & Graphene Oxide)
- Carbon Nanotubes (SWCNTs/MWCNTs) and Graphene Oxide (GO).
- High-capacity, multi-layer coverage for complex antibiotic architectures.
- **Processing barrier:** High synthesis costs per gram restricting large-scale real-world deployment.

## 3. Kinetic Pathways, Isotherm Thermodynamics, and Interfacial Mechanisms

### 3.1 Adsorption Kinetics and Transport Steps
- **Mass-transfer sequence:** Bulk solution transport → film diffusion → intra-particle pore diffusion → active-site binding.
- **Kinetic-rate comparison:** The original outline proposes comparing slower mass transfer in unrefined biomass with faster profiles in Fe/N co-doped biochars and CNTs.

### 3.2 Isotherm Models & Solution Chemistry Influences
- **Equilibrium models:** Langmuir monolayer binding (listed for standard AC/pristine biochar) and Freundlich heterogeneous/multilayer adsorption (listed for CNTs/graphene oxide).
- **pH and speciation dynamics:** Impact of solution pH relative to antibiotic dissociation constants (pKₐ) and adsorbent surface zero point charge (pHₚzc).

| Processing stage | Representative sorbent | Key active surface features | Dominant mechanism(s) listed in the original outline | Primary target antibiotic classes listed |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## 4. Techno-Economic Evaluation, Energy Footprints, and Regeneration

### 4.1 Production Cost and Energy Analysis
- **Synthesis energy footprint:** The original outline proposes the qualitative sequence Raw Biomass (Direct) < Clays/Chitosan < Biochar (HTC/Pyrolysis) < Modified Biochar < AC (High Temp) ≪ CNTs / nano-carbons. This ordering is a hypothesis to verify against sources, not a result to assume.
- **Normalized cost metric:** Evaluate cost per mass pollutant removed (USD/g removed) versus bulk procurement cost (USD/kg).

### 4.2 Desorption and Recyclability
- **Regeneration methods:** Thermal reactivation (pyrolysis) versus chemical washing (NaOH, HCl, organic solvents).
- **Structural integrity across multi-cycle use:** Compare robustness of doped biochars and clays with possible degradation of unrefined raw biomass.

## 5. Practical Implementation and Real-Water Matrix Effects

### 5.1 Wastewater Matrix Interferences
- Impact of Natural Organic Matter (NOM) and competing inorganic ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻) on cation exchange (clays) and pore blocking (biochar/AC).

### 5.2 Process Integration Configurations
- Fixed-bed continuous-flow column dynamics versus batch studies.
- Hybrid application: biochar/AC fixed beds paired with Membrane Bioreactors (MBR) or polishing filters in municipal wastewater treatment plants.

## 6. Challenges and Future Perspectives
- Developing low-energy, green synthesis protocols for atomically doped (Fe/N) biochars and green-derived nano-carbons.
- Managing secondary chemical waste generated during KOH and H₂SO₄ biochar modifications.
- Pilot-scale continuous-flow validations under multi-contaminant, real-world effluent conditions.

## 7. Conclusions
- Reiteration of the processing & cost continuum: the original outline proposes that engineered biochars (KOH, H₂SO₄, and Fe/N doped) may offer a balance of processing energy, capacity, and economic viability relative to commercial AC and expensive nano-carbons; this claim must be tested against comparable evidence.
- Strategic recommendations for selecting adsorbents based on effluent composition and target discharge standards.
