## 2.3. Biochar and chemically modified biochar

Biochar occupies an intermediate position in the review's processing continuum: a biomass or waste feedstock is thermally converted, then the resulting carbonaceous material may be washed, activated or functionalized. This is not one uniform material class. Feedstock composition and pyrolysis conditions establish a baseline; chemical activation can change porosity and surface chemistry, while adding acids/bases, extensive washing, neutralisation and further heating introduces process inputs and residual-waste questions. The available studies support case-specific comparisons, not a general rule that greater processing always improves treatment or lowers overall cost.

### 2.3.1. Standard biochar: adsorption performance depends on feedstock, operating configuration and water matrix

A useful example of a relatively unactivated, waste-derived biochar is the material produced from spent pilsener-brewery grains. Sousa et al. pyrolysed dried and ground grains by microwave heating at 800 °C for 20 min under nitrogen and reported a 23% product yield. The granular form had a BET surface area of 281 m²/g; the study also reports acid washing, neutralisation of the wash liquor and drying, so “without activation” does not mean that the product was made without chemical or water inputs. The work is particularly valuable because it progresses beyond batch tests to fixed-bed operation and includes a production-stage environmental assessment (DISC-025, `EV-B07-011`, source PDF pp. 2, 4; `EV-B07-019`, pp. 4, 12).

The authors tested sulfamethoxazole (SMX), trimethoprim (TMP) and ciprofloxacin (CIP) at 20 μmol/L and pH 8. Their batch isotherms reported Langmuir-fitted capacities in buffered water of 7.1 ± 0.1 μmol/g for SMX, 18 ± 1 μmol/g for TMP and 16 ± 1 μmol/g for CIP. These are fitted `q_m` values in micromoles per gram, not observed equilibrium uptake in mg/g. The paper reports lower fitted capacities in its wastewater matrix—approximately 7% lower for SMX, 38% lower for TMP and 11% lower for CIP. The matrix was actual secondary wastewater effluent, but it was filtered at 0.45 μm and autoclaved before controlled antibiotic feeds were introduced; it should therefore be described as a filtered/autoclaved real-effluent matrix, not untreated wastewater or a native-contaminant removal trial (DISC-025, `EV-B07-012`–`EV-B07-013`, PDF pp. 3–6).

The fixed-bed results add a distinct, operationally relevant endpoint. At a reported flow of 0.042 L/h, Thomas-model `q_TH` values were 11.4 ± 0.2 μmol/g for SMX, 33 ± 1 μmol/g for TMP and 18.9 ± 0.2 μmol/g for CIP. These are model-fitted dynamic parameters. They must not be combined with the batch Langmuir values or with experimentally reported saturation loading. The study defines breakthrough at approximately `C/C₀ = 0.1`; service time and treated volume vary with target and flow. In ternary antibiotic feeds, breakthrough became steeper and fitted capacities fell, with stronger matrix effects in the filtered/autoclaved wastewater. This is evidence that single-solute batch tests can overstate performance in a mixed, real-water operating context—not that this biochar is universally better or worse than another class (DISC-025, `EV-B07-014`–`EV-B07-017`, PDF pp. 6–10).

Regeneration further complicates a simple performance ranking. TMP-loaded biochar was regenerated under nitrogen at 600 °C for 20 min and reused for two additional fixed-bed tests. BET area increased from 281 to 380 and then 462 m²/g; experimental `q_s` increased from 38 to 55 and then 68 μmol/g, while model-derived `q_Y` increased from 32.3 to 94.9 and then 114 μmol/g. The authors' regeneration-efficiency values of approximately 118% and 146% are defined as the ratio of regenerated to initial adsorption capacity; they are not desorption percentages. The increase is noteworthy but does not establish the fate of the adsorbed antibiotic or account fully for thermal emissions. The production-only LCA identifies washing/neutralisation and drying as major contribution hotspots in several impact categories, and pyrolysis contributes to others. The reported assessment covers production, not a cradle-to-grave comparison of treatment service, spent media and antibiotic fate; supplementary totals were not present in the supplied main-text extraction (DISC-025, `EV-B07-018`–`EV-B07-019`, PDF pp. 10–12).

### 2.3.2. Chemical activation: higher measured surface area is not sufficient proof of practical superiority

Nguyen et al. compared sunflower-seed-husk biochar produced at 600 °C (SF-600) with phosphoric-acid-activated material (PSF). The reported BET area rose from 6.2 m²/g for SF-600 to 378.8 m²/g for PSF. For tetracycline (TC), ciprofloxacin (CIP) and sulfamethoxazole (SMX), the reported Langmuir-fitted values increased from 228.3 to 429.3 mg/g for TC, 80.8 to 361.6 mg/g for CIP, and 129.1 to 251.3 mg/g for SMX. These are within-study fitted `q_m` comparisons, not direct observations at a shared low environmental concentration and not evidence by themselves of a lower treatment cost or superior life-cycle performance. Ibuprofen is a non-antibiotic co-contaminant in that paper and should not be included in the antibiotic-only synthesis (CORP-020, `EV-B11-012`–`EV-B11-013`, source PDF pp. 4–6; Table 2).

An important source limitation must travel with these numbers: the paper's Figure 2 caption appears to mismatch the kinetic and isotherm panel-condition descriptions. The table's fitted values can be transcribed as reported, but exact panel conditions should not be inferred from the disputed caption. The study includes controlled pH, anion and mixed-feed tests but does not report regeneration cycles, actual wastewater validation or a quantified product cost/LCA in the extracted record. Acid activation therefore offers a supported within-study performance contrast, while its additional phosphoric acid, heating, washing and neutralisation burden remains unquantified (CORP-020, `EV-AUD-007`, PDF pp. 4–6; `EV-B11-015`–`EV-B11-018`).

A second illustration is KOH-activated dyeing-sludge biochar KSC-8. The preparation used dried dyeing sludge, a 1:1 sludge:KOH mass ratio, pyrolysis at 800 °C for 3 h under nitrogen, and a 3 M HCl wash followed by rinsing and drying. The source reports a BET surface area of 1178.4 m²/g in the abstract, characterization discussion and Table 2, while one discussion sentence prints 1078.4 m²/g. The 1178.4 value should not be presented without noting the internal discrepancy. The source also has inconsistent temperature reporting: the isotherm methods state 298 K while its conclusion attaches the maximum capacity to 278 K (DISC-028, `EV-B01-012`–`EV-B01-014`, PDF pp. 2, 4–7).

For KSC-8, the paper reports a Langmuir-fitted TC `q_max` of 1081.3 mg/g and observed uptake of 305.99 mg/g at 298 K in a separate temperature test. The former comes from a 10–800 mg/L isotherm series; the latter is a reported experimental uptake at 100 mg/L. They are different endpoints and must not be placed in one capacity column. The supplied main PDF does not clearly report the batch adsorbent mass/dose for those tests, preventing a complete independent mass-balance check. The paper also reports simulated-matrix/multi-antibiotic removal results and an actual-tailwater TC result, but concentration and matrix details are incomplete; claims about the “actual tailwater” should be limited to the source's stated result and not generalised to all four antibiotics in native wastewater (DISC-028, `EV-B01-013`–`EV-B01-015`, PDF pp. 3, 6–7).

The fixed-bed and regeneration claims for KSC-8 require additional caution. Under one reported fixed-bed condition (TC influent 10 mg/L, pH 5, flow 1.5 mL/min, 0.25 g sorbent), the source reports 13.59 L treated before effluent concentration reached 1 mg/L and a threshold-specific capacity of 543.46 mg/g. Separately, it reports a maximum fixed-bed capacity of 1124.2 mg/g, exceeding the batch fitted `q_max` of 1081.3 mg/g despite describing the figures as nearly equal. The paper also reports cycle-five regeneration efficiency of 86.88% and retained adsorption capacity of 86.35%, while the abstract labels 86.35% as removal efficiency. It regenerates with Fe²⁺/H₂O₂ under acidic conditions, so reagent requirements and spent liquid matter. None of these metrics should be silently merged or corrected in the manuscript (DISC-028, `EV-B01-016`–`EV-B01-018`, PDF pp. 3, 7).

The same paper estimates preparation cost at approximately US$9/kg, versus a cited US$28/kg average for activated carbon. This is an author-reported estimate, not a harmonised techno-economic comparison. The accessible main text does not establish a clear functional unit for the stated energy figure or demonstrate whether yield, acid washing, wastewater treatment, equipment, regeneration, and spent-media disposal are all represented. It can be reported as a preliminary estimate with these limitations, but not used to conclude that dyeing-sludge biochar is cheaper overall than commercial activated carbon (DISC-028, `EV-B01-018`; source caveats in `data/source-caveats.csv`).

### 2.3.3. Cost, scale-up and sustainability: the missing comparison is a complete process boundary

Across these examples, source evidence supports an evaluation framework rather than a numerical sustainability league table. At minimum, future comparisons should record feedstock preparation; pyrolysis or hydrothermal-conversion conditions; product yield; chemicals, solvents and wash water; neutralisation and wastewater handling; drying and heating energy; adsorbent dose and usable performance in relevant water; media recovery; regeneration losses; leaching; and spent-sorbent treatment. A low cost per kilogram of prepared material does not automatically imply a low cost per volume of treated water or per mass of antibiotic removed, especially where the dose is high, performance falls in real matrices, or regeneration adds energy and chemicals.

For this subsection, the brewery-residue study provides a relatively broad—but still production-boundary—LCA and fixed-bed dataset; the sunflower-husk study offers a controlled activation contrast but no quantified lifecycle comparison; and the dyeing-sludge study reports a preliminary cost and column test alongside important internal inconsistencies. These studies should be read as complementary evidence with different strengths, not as a head-to-head ranking. Further comparisons should use primary records and their supplements where available, and report missing or inaccessible values as such.
