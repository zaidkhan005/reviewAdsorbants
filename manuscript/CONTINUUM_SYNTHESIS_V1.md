# Processing–cost–performance continuum: evidence-weighted synthesis (v1)

**Status:** working synthesis from the current source-linked corpus. Candidate IDs are internal locators; replace them with Zotero-managed author–date citations before submission. This is not a final qmax ranking or a claim that every candidate is included. The comparison matrix at `data/primary-study-comparison-matrix.csv` contains the underlying target/material groupings and evidence IDs.

## Central synthesis

The current corpus does **not** support a simple rule that more processing always produces a better or more feasible antibiotic adsorbent. Processing can increase surface area, introduce functional groups, improve selectivity or enable bead/monolith/column operation, but it also adds reagent use, heating, washing, solvent recovery, shaping, regeneration and end-of-life burdens. Several papers show performance gains after modification, while other direct comparisons show that activation adds little or can reduce uptake. Apparent maximum capacities also vary with initial concentration, dose, model choice and internal consistency; a reported fitted qmax is not interchangeable with observed uptake, percent removal or column working capacity.

An evidence-weighted comparison should therefore locate each material by **feedstock, processing route and treatment form**, then pair performance with concentration range, adsorbent dose, solution matrix, regeneration, continuous-flow evidence and the completeness of the cost/energy boundary. The continuum is a comparison framework—not a predetermined ranking.

## Cross-cutting findings that are supportable now

1. **Processing is a trade-off variable, not a proxy for quality.** The raw/activated clay comparison found activation was not necessary overall, while phosphoric-acid biochar activation and nitrogen-doping/milling examples report clear changes in textural properties or removal under their respective laboratory conditions. These outcomes support describing process–performance relations case by case rather than asserting a monotonic trend.
2. **Environmental and engineering relevance is concentrated in a minority of studies.** The GAC hospital-effluent study, waste-derived GAC municipal wastewater study, clay monolith work, and brewery-residue biochar fixed-bed/LCA study give column, matrix or end-of-life information that many batch-only papers lack. Even these examples require endpoint-specific reading: actual secondary effluent, filtered/autoclaved real effluent, spiked water and synthetic water are not equivalent.
3. **The cost evidence is too uneven for an absolute cheapest-to-most-expensive ranking.** Several studies provide no quantitative cost/LCA; some provide only lab material cost, estimated costs with unclear units/boundaries, or LCAs that omit use and spent-media disposal. A cost continuum should record reagent and energy inputs, yield, wash-water and neutralization needs, regeneration, media lifetime and end-of-life as separate dimensions.
4. **The most consequential data-quality issue is not merely unit conversion.** Several papers contain apparent mass-balance violations, inconsistent concentration ranges, contradictory fitted/observed capacities, or mismatched text/table values. Those values should be excluded from numerical rankings until reconciled, while independently supported removal, matrix or mechanism observations may still be cited with clear restrictions.
5. **Adsorption, transformation and downstream treatment must remain distinct.** Mixed catalytic/oxidative systems and membrane rejection are useful only when clearly separated from the core adsorptive-removal comparison. GAC-associated decreases in toxicity or antibiotic-resistance-gene signals are important process outcomes but do not, on their own, show that adsorption destroyed the parent antibiotic.

## Minimum normalization fields for the final comparative table

- Antibiotic and co-contaminant; single-solute versus mixture.
- Initial and equilibrium concentration; pH; temperature; adsorbent dose; contact time; solution matrix.
- Observed uptake (q_e), percentage removal and model-fitted q_max in separate columns, with units and model fit quality.
- Batch versus column; flow rate, bed mass/volume, breakthrough criterion, treated bed volumes and working capacity where available.
- Preparation route, reagent/solvent inputs, temperature/time, yield, washing/neutralization and textural properties.
- Regeneration method, cycle count, performance retained, desorption or transformation endpoint, leaching and spent-media fate.
- Cost basis and system boundary: bulk adsorbent cost, cost per mass of antibiotic removed, energy use, LCA scope and included end-of-life stages.

## Data-quality and citation caveats

- CORP-006 (timber-waste vancomycin): do not use the reported 12,179.5 mg/g fitted qmax until resolved against observed uptake.
- CORP-019 (barley husk/levofloxacin): reported fitted q_m and kinetic table conflict with mass-balance conditions.
- CORP-025 (nano-montmorillonite/tetracycline): methods dosage and conclusion dosage disagree; reported 500/526 mg/g and fitted 558.184 mg/g are not accepted for comparison pending reconciliation.
- DISC-018 (purified stevensite): extreme narrative capacity conflicts with the table; retain direct observed results only after checking exact conditions.
- DISC-026 (acid-activated coconut biochar): abstract/method concentration ranges conflict and kinetic q_e breaches the mass-balance ceiling under the stated typical setup.
- CORP-024 (sulfonated GO) and CORP-031 (N-rGO): fitted capacities need mass-balance/unit audit before ranking.
- DISC-028 (dyeing-sludge biochar): conflicting BET area, temperatures, removal equation, regeneration metrics and fixed-bed capacity are recorded.
- DISC-022 remains deferred until the user supplies its full text; no full-text table values from that review are used here.
