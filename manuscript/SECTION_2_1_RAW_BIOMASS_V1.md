## 2.1. Raw biomass and low-processed waste-derived sorbents

Raw agricultural and forestry residues occupy the low-processing end of the review’s processing–cost–performance continuum because the base material can often be prepared through washing, drying, size reduction and sieving rather than pyrolysis or deliberate surface engineering. However, “waste-derived” is not itself a cost or sustainability result. Preparation, dose, pH, residual contaminants, solid handling, regeneration and end-of-life remain part of the process boundary. The evidence reviewed here also shows why a low-processed sorbent should not be judged by removal percentage alone: a large dose can deliver high percentage removal while leaving modest uptake per unit sorbent, and high fitted capacities can be incompatible with the experiment’s material balance.

The studies in this subsection are not a single harmonized test set. They cover different antibiotics, concentration ranges, solution matrices and outcome metrics. Some investigate retention in simple batch systems rather than wastewater treatment; one uses activated carbon as a benchmark; and two studies on pine bark, oak ash and mussel shell differ in antibiotic targets and experimental design. Their values are therefore useful for illustrating trade-offs and data quality, not for creating a universal capacity league table.

### 2.1.1. Untreated and minimally processed residues

**Pine bark, oak ash and uncalcined mussel shell.** A comparative study examined sulfadiazine (SDZ), sulfamethazine (SMT) and sulfachloropyridazine (SCP) on pine bark, oak ash and mussel shell. At an initial concentration of 50 µmol/L, the reported amounts adsorbed by pine bark were 780 µmol/kg for SDZ, 890 µmol/kg for SMT and 870 µmol/kg for SCP. The authors reported more than 70% adsorption of the three sulfonamides across the pine-bark concentration range, with maxima of 90% for SDZ at the lowest tested concentration and 95% for SCP at the two highest concentrations. These values are reported in µmol/kg and must not be recast as mg/g or as fitted saturation capacities. The same paper reported substantially lower uptake for the two other residues: oak ash uptake was below 150 µmol/kg per antibiotic at 50 µmol/L, while mussel-shell uptake was below 20 µmol/kg per antibiotic at that concentration. The authors related these differences to material chemistry and pH, but the mechanisms remain interpretations rather than an isolated measurement of each interaction. ([CORP-005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2021.110814))

The practical interpretation is constrained by the experimental setup. Pine bark and oak ash were dosed at 50 g/L, whereas mussel shell was dosed at 400 g/L. The suspensions were not pH-buffered, and the reported source has a low-end concentration discrepancy between the abstract and methods (1 versus 2.5 µmol/L). The abstract and body also give different percentage thresholds for ash and shell adsorption. Consequently, this study supports a cautious comparison of waste-derived materials under the reported conditions, but not an equal-dose material ranking or a demonstrated wastewater treatment claim.

A related study assessed amoxicillin (AMX), ciprofloxacin (CIP) and trimethoprim (TMP) retention by pine bark, oak ash and mussel shell at added concentrations up to 600 µmol/L. Oak ash retained AMX at close to 100% under the tested conditions, while pine bark retained CIP and TMP at nearly 100% across the concentration range. This was primarily a retention/adsorption–desorption study, not a column treatment demonstration. Its single CaCl₂ release test showed that desorption depended on both material and antibiotic: release of AMX from oak ash was close to zero in the reported conditions, while pine bark released less than 5% of retained TMP in the main discussion. Low release in a single batch step indicates retention under that test; it does not establish multi-cycle regeneration, destruction of the antibiotic, or safe end-of-life management. ([CORP-017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2023.116360))

That second study also illustrates a recurring model-risk issue. The fitted Langmuir maximum for TMP on pine bark was reported as 76,429 µmol/kg, with a very large parameter error, while the maximum loading accessible from the highest stated concentration and batch volume was 12,000 µmol/kg if complete removal occurred. The fitted value is therefore not a validated measured capacity and should be withheld from comparative rankings. Retention curves and directly reported adsorption amounts can still be discussed with their conditions; an implausible fitted asymptote should not determine the material’s position on the continuum. ([CORP-017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2023.116360))

**Raw rice husk.** A study of unmodified rice husk as a levofloxacin (LEV) sorbent reported a surface area of 0.328 m²/g and a fitted Langmuir (q_{\max}) of 2.47 mg/g. Increasing the nominal sorbent dose from 5 to 120 g/L increased removal from 27.8% to 93.8%; the paper reported that the removal response approached a plateau at about 120 minutes. This is a useful example of the difference between percentage removal and sorbent-normalized performance: high removal at 120 g/L requires a very large mass of solid per litre treated and cannot, by itself, substantiate the claim that the overall treatment is inexpensive. The experiments used prepared LEV solutions, not actual wastewater, and the reviewed article did not report regeneration cycles, a numerical production cost or a life-cycle assessment. ([CORP-010](https://doi.org/10.36103/ijas.v54i5.1841))

The paper identifies native hydroxyl, carboxylic/C–O and related surface groups as possible contributors based on characterization and before/after adsorption observations. These are proposed interactions; the evidence extracted here does not establish that any one mechanism dominates. For the processing–cost comparison, the more robust point is that a low surface area and modest fitted capacity can coexist with high removal when the dose is increased substantially. The fitted capacity should remain labelled model-derived, not be described as the observed uptake at the optimum removal percentage.

**Untreated barley husks and eggshells compared with activated carbon.** A separate levofloxacin study reported 71% removal using barley husks and 74% using activated carbon at 5 g/L; eggshells reached 42% removal at 50 g/L. The activated-carbon comparison is useful as a laboratory benchmark, but the main tests used synthetic water with a reported LEV concentration of 150 mg/L, far above trace environmental concentrations. More importantly, the article’s fitted Langmuir (q_{\max}) of 125.735 mg/g for barley husks exceeds the 30 mg/g maximum accessible loading under the stated isotherm setup (150 mg/L feed, 100 mL solution and 0.5 g sorbent). Its kinetic table also conflicts with the separately described dose/mass-balance conditions. These fitted and kinetic values must not be used as validated capacities unless the underlying setup or calculation can be reconciled. The observed removal percentages can be retained as source-reported outcomes, with the synthetic-water, high-concentration and dose conditions attached. ([CORP-019](https://doi.org/10.31026/j.eng.2014.12.06))

### 2.1.2. Processed sawdust as a bridge from raw waste to modified biomass

Although the following papers are not strictly “untreated raw biomass” studies, they are valuable boundary cases because they compare a waste feedstock with additional chemical treatment. They should be classified as modified-biomass evidence rather than silently counted as unmodified Stage 1 sorbents.

**Chemically modified sawdust for tetracycline.** Raw *Melia azedarach* sawdust was compared with variants treated using CaCl₂, NaHCO₃, HCl and FeCl₃/HCl. The Fe-modified variant performed best in the initial screening test: raw-sawdust removal was generally below 10%, while the Fe-modified material exceeded 60% removal at pH 7.5 and 9.5 after more than 60 minutes, using an initial tetracycline concentration of 10 mg/L and sorbent dose of 3 g/L. Response-surface modelling predicted 98.40% removal at a specified optimum (12.5 mg/L initial concentration, pH 7.9, 4.8 g/L dose and 118.3 minutes), but the listed validation runs reached measured removal up to 92.37%. The predicted optimum must therefore be reported separately from measured validation. ([CORP-004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psep.2018.04.007))

This study also included a secondary-hospital-wastewater test: tetracycline decreased from 0.25 mg/L to below HPLC detection under the reported pH 8.3, dose 3.6 g/L and 53-minute condition. Because the paper did not provide the numeric detection/quantification limits in the extracted text, “non-detect” should not be rewritten as zero concentration. Its regeneration evidence was limited to one stage. The often-quoted 88% figure refers to the fraction of water volume reclaimed in the regeneration-stage calculation, not to tetracycline desorption efficiency or multi-cycle regeneration. ([CORP-004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psep.2018.04.007))

The treatment sequence—acid/base exposure, FeCl₃/HCl treatment, neutral washing and additional drying—adds chemical, wash-water and energy requirements compared with the raw feedstock. The reviewed paper does not quantify these inputs per unit product, provide a complete process mass balance, or report Fe-leaching and full cost/LCA results. The performance improvement is thus evidence of a modification effect under its conditions, but not proof that the modified material has a better overall processing–cost–performance balance.

**Modified timber waste for vancomycin.** A timber-waste study compared modified sawdust forms against unmodified material and powdered activated carbon. At 100 mg/L vancomycin in a model-water filtration test, modified mixed sawdust removed 63.0 ± 2.3%, modified ash sawdust removed 56.6 ± 5.1%, and powdered activated carbon removed 98.2 ± 2.9%. At a 2 g sorbent load, observed uptake values were reported as 3.11 mg/g for modified mixed sawdust and 2.79 mg/g for modified ash sawdust, compared with 9.33 mg/g for activated carbon under the reported comparison. These outcomes illustrate how percentage removal, observed uptake and sorbent dose should be reported together rather than substituting one for another. ([CORP-006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11270-023-06070-9))

The same article’s fitted Langmuir (q_{\max}) of 12,179.5 mg/g for modified ash is physically implausible relative to its observed uptake (approximately 2.79–4 mg/g in the reported tests) and must not be used as a valid capacity. The study’s multiple acid/base treatments, ethanol use, washing to neutral pH and repeated drying are also important process inputs; they were not quantified as a complete cost or environmental inventory. Although the article discusses thermal energy recovery from spent sawdust, contaminant fate under that proposed treatment was not demonstrated. The results therefore support discussion of modification and of a powdered activated-carbon benchmark, while the fitted capacity and sustainability claims remain qualified.

### 2.1.3. A low-concentration cross-stage comparison: untreated biosorbents versus biochar

A low-concentration study tested trimethoprim (TMP) uptake by macro-algae, oak wood chips and orchard-pruning biochar. At 500 µg/L TMP, pH 7, 20 °C and a sorbent dose of 2 g/L, observed equilibrium uptakes were 238 µg/g for macro-algae, 158 µg/g for wood chips and 220 µg/g for biochar (equivalent to 0.238, 0.158 and 0.220 mg/g). These values are especially useful because the feed concentration is lower than in many capacity-maximization studies. They show that observed uptake under a defined low-concentration test need not follow the same ordering as fitted isotherm (Q_m): the paper’s separate isotherm fits used substantially different concentration ranges, with the macro-algae TMP series extending as high as 400,000 µg/L. The fitted (Q_m) values must therefore remain separate from the 500 µg/L kinetic uptakes and should not be used as an equal-condition ranking. ([CORP-016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2019.04.081))

This comparison also demonstrates why feedstock and processing route should be separate fields in the review taxonomy. The wood chips and macro-algae represent low-processing biosorbents, while the orchard-pruning biochar had already been pyrolyzed at 500 °C; comparing them in the same study is informative, but the biochar belongs in the biochar subsection when the paper is classified by preparation stage. Only TMP should be counted in antibiotic-specific synthesis from this study; diclofenac is an NSAID comparator, not an antibiotic.

## Synthesis and implications for the continuum

Three conclusions follow from these records. First, minimal preparation may reduce the number of process steps, but it does not guarantee strong uptake or low treatment cost: raw rice husk reached high percentage removal only at very large doses, and barley-husk performance was accompanied by an invalid fitted capacity. Second, the consequences of modification should be judged against both its performance increment and the inputs needed to create and maintain the material. The sawdust studies report improved removal after chemical treatment, but they also add acids, bases, Fe salts, ethanol, washing and drying, while giving no complete process-cost inventory. Third, retention/desorption, batch removal, actual wastewater performance and fixed-bed behaviour are distinct outcomes. Pine-bark and by-product results are principally retention studies; the modified-sawdust study provides a secondary-wastewater non-detect result under a high sorbent dose; and the timber-waste study uses a high-concentration model-water filtration setup.

The defensible conclusion is not that one raw residue is universally best. Instead, low-processed materials should be screened using antibiotic-specific performance at relevant concentrations, dose-normalized mass removal, matrix and pH dependence, release/desorption, and safe end-of-life. Numerical capacities that violate the experiment’s mass balance should be excluded from the comparative table unless their underlying conditions or calculations are resolved. In this review, low processing is one dimension of the continuum; it must be read alongside actual adsorption performance, operating conditions and the completeness of the environmental/economic boundary.
