# Working manuscript outline

**Controlling source:** [Original outline transcribed from the supplied PDF](ORIGINAL_OUTLINE_AS_SUPPLIED.md)  
**Working revision:** The original framework is retained, but the comparative mechanism material is promoted to its own numbered section. Later sections are renumbered accordingly. The original PDF's claims remain hypotheses to verify, not facts to assume.

## Title framework options from the original outline

1. **Option A:** From Raw Agricultural Waste to Atomically Doped Biochars: A Processing and Cost Continuum for Adsorptive Antibiotic Removal
2. **Option B:** Adsorptive Remediation of Antibiotics across the Material Processing Spectrum: Mechanisms, Regeneration, and Economic Feasibility

## Abstract — key elements to synthesize

- **Environmental drivers:** Antibiotic residues across municipal, industrial, and agricultural effluents and their relationship to antibiotic-resistance concerns.
- **Structural continuum:** Compare the eight original classes by processing complexity and cost: raw lignocellulosic biomass; natural clays; unmodified biopolymers; standard biochar; chemically modified biochars; atomically doped biochars; activated carbon (AC); and nano-engineered carbons (CNTs/GO).
- **Mechanistic spectrum:** Evidence-supported contributions of surface complexation, cation exchange, pore filling, π–π interactions, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and other material–antibiotic interactions.
- **Techno-economic perspective:** Preparation energy and chemicals, normalized cost per mass of antibiotic removed, reusability, real-water performance, and continuous-flow integration.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Occurrence, toxicity, and risks of antibiotics in water
- Sources: municipal wastewater, livestock runoff, and pharmaceutical manufacturing discharges.
- Ecotoxicological effects and antibiotic-resistance concerns, distinguishing measured evidence from broad claims.

### 1.2 Treatment limitations and the adsorption option
- Limitations of conventional biological treatment and the capital/operational demands of advanced oxidation processes.
- Adsorption as a flexible treatment or polishing unit operation; avoid implying that adsorption itself destroys the pollutant.

### 1.3 The processing-and-cost continuum
- Move beyond a binary “low-cost versus high-cost” label.
- Define feasibility through processing energy, synthesis complexity, material yield, adsorbent dose, adsorption performance, regeneration, and capacity-normalized cost.
- State the review questions, scope boundaries, and organization.

## 2. Adsorbent classification along the processing-and-cost continuum

Use the original four-stage structure and keep the original eight material classes distinct. Record the feedstock separately from processing route and surface modification.

### 2.1 Stage 1: Raw / unrefined sorbents — minimal processing and direct waste use

#### 2.1.1 Raw lignocellulosic biomass
- Agricultural waste feedstocks such as raw sawdust, fruit peels, and rice husks.
- Lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose matrix.
- Native surface groups such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, methoxyl, and phenolic groups.

### 2.2 Stage 2: Extracted sorbents and natural mineral clays — low-to-moderate processing

#### 2.2.1 Natural clays (e.g., bentonite)
- Phyllosilicate interlayer structure and cation-exchange capacity.
- Exchangeable cations and the influence of antibiotic speciation and solution pH.

#### 2.2.2 Unmodified biopolymers (e.g., chitosan)
- Chitin-derived chitosan and other unmodified biopolymer examples where supported by the review scope.
- Native amine and hydroxyl groups before chemical cross-linking or functionalization.
- Keep modified biopolymers and biopolymer composites clearly distinguished if discussed as adjacent classes; do not silently redefine the original Stage 2 classes.

### 2.3 Stage 3: Thermally converted and functionalized biochars — moderate processing

#### 2.3.1 Standard biochar
- Pyrolysis and hydrothermal-carbonization routes, reported with the actual conditions and feedstocks.
- Porous carbon structure and baseline surface chemistry.

#### 2.3.2 Chemically modified biochars
- Physical or chemical activation and other surface-modification routes.
- Chemicals used, energy inputs, product yield, washing/neutralization needs, and the risks of secondary waste.

### 2.4 Stage 4: Precision-engineered and nano-carbons — high-processing/high-technology benchmarks

#### 2.4.1 Atomically doped biochars
- Heteroatom-doping strategies, including Fe/N where supported by individual studies.
- Separate adsorption evidence from catalytic oxidation, redox reactions, or other degradation processes.

#### 2.4.2 Standard activated carbon
- Commercial and biomass-derived AC, including PAC/GAC where relevant.
- Treat AC as an industrial benchmark, not automatically as a nanomaterial.

#### 2.4.3 Nano-engineered carbons (CNTs and graphene oxide)
- SWCNTs/MWCNTs, GO, reduced GO, and relevant functionalized forms.
- Synthesis and modification costs, recovery/separation concerns, and scale-up barriers.

## 3. Adsorption kinetics, isotherms, and solution chemistry

### 3.1 Kinetic pathways and transport steps
- Mass-transfer sequence: bulk solution → film diffusion → intra-particle/pore diffusion → adsorption at available sites.
- Compare kinetic models and rate-controlling steps only where the data support them.
- Do not interpret a pseudo-second-order fit alone as proof of chemisorption.

### 3.2 Isotherm models and equilibrium behaviour
- Compare Langmuir, Freundlich, and other fitted models where used.
- Report fitted parameter uncertainty and fitting quality where available.
- Keep experimentally observed uptake distinct from fitted maximum capacity (qmax); flag inconsistent or implausible values.

### 3.3 pH, antibiotic speciation, and surface charge
- Relate solution pH to antibiotic pKa values and adsorbent point of zero charge (pHpzc) when these data are available.
- Consider ionic strength, dissolved organic matter, and competing solutes as context for interpreting adsorption results.

## 4. Mechanisms of antibiotic adsorption across the processing continuum

**Purpose:** This is a standalone core section. Explain *why* adsorption occurs for each material class and identify which mechanisms are directly evidenced, inferred, or still uncertain. Use mechanism labels only when supported by primary-study data.

### 4.1 Non-specific and surface-functional-group interactions
- Hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions.
- Electrostatic attraction/repulsion and acid–base interactions.
- Hydrophobic interactions and contributions from native or modified functional groups.

### 4.2 Pore- and surface-associated mechanisms
- Pore filling and pore-size/accessibility effects.
- Surface complexation and interactions with mineral or carbon surfaces.
- π–π interactions and donor–acceptor interactions on aromatic/carbonaceous materials.

### 4.3 Material-specific mechanisms across the original stages
- **Raw lignocellulosic biomass:** possible hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions, and interactions with native lignocellulosic functional groups.
- **Natural clays:** cation exchange, electrostatic interactions, surface complexation, and interlayer accessibility, conditional on antibiotic speciation and clay chemistry.
- **Unmodified biopolymers:** interactions involving amine and hydroxyl groups, including pH-dependent electrostatic capture and hydrogen bonding.
- **Standard biochar:** pore filling, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions, and aromatic-surface interactions, depending on feedstock and pyrolysis conditions.
- **Chemically modified biochar:** effects attributable to the specific modification, new functional groups, pore changes, and residual chemicals.
- **Atomically doped biochar:** distinguish adsorption at modified surface sites from electron-transfer or catalytic pathways that may transform contaminants.
- **Activated carbon:** pore filling, electrostatic interactions, and π–π interactions where supported by evidence.
- **CNTs/graphene-based carbons:** accessible surface area, π–π interactions, electrostatic interactions, and pore/aggregate accessibility; consider material recovery and aggregation.

### 4.4 Evidence-weighted comparative mechanism matrix

| Continuum stage / class | Surface features to document | Candidate mechanisms to test against evidence | Required checks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Raw biomass | Native hydroxyl, carbonyl, phenolic groups | Hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions, hydrophobic/electrostatic contributions | Spectroscopy, pH behaviour, controls, and competing solutes |
| Stage 2: Natural clays | Interlayers, exchangeable cations, surface charge | Cation exchange, electrostatic interactions, surface complexation | CEC, pH/speciation, ionic strength, mineral composition |
| Stage 2: Unmodified biopolymers | Amine and hydroxyl groups | Hydrogen bonding, pH-dependent electrostatic interactions | Degree of deacetylation/functional-group evidence, pH and controls |
| Stage 3: Standard biochar | Pore structure, aromaticity, surface groups | Pore filling, hydrophobic and π–π interactions, electrostatic effects | Feedstock, pyrolysis conditions, pore-size data, surface chemistry |
| Stage 3: Modified biochar | Modification-derived pores/groups | Pore filling, acid–base and electrostatic interactions, other supported interactions | Reagent use, wash controls, before/after characterization, secondary waste |
| Stage 4: Atomically doped biochar | Dopant identity/state and surface chemistry | Adsorption at modified sites; catalytic/electron-transfer pathways only if separately demonstrated | Check for oxidants, transformation products, mass balance, and adsorption-only controls |
| Stage 4: Activated carbon | Micro-/mesoporosity and surface chemistry | Pore filling, π–π and electrostatic interactions | Pore accessibility, surface charge, competing matrix |
| Stage 4: CNTs/graphene-based carbon | Graphitic surfaces, functional groups, aggregation state | π–π, electrostatic, hydrophobic and other supported interactions | Dispersion, aggregation, recovery, and realistic-water performance |

**Scope guardrail:** This review is about adsorptive removal. Studies dominated by peroxymonosulfate activation, photocatalysis, or catalytic oxidation must not be presented as adsorption-only evidence. They may be mentioned only in a clearly separated context if scientifically relevant.

## 5. Techno-economic evaluation, energy footprints, and regeneration

### 5.1 Production cost and energy analysis
- Compare feedstock preparation, drying, extraction, pyrolysis/HTC, chemical activation/doping, washing, neutralization, yield, and recovery.
- Treat any simple cost/energy ordering as a hypothesis until supported by comparable data.
- Compare bulk procurement cost (e.g., USD/kg) with an explicit normalized metric such as cost per mass of antibiotic removed, stating the equation, assumptions, and system boundary.

### 5.2 Desorption, recyclability, and end-of-life
- Compare thermal regeneration and chemical washing (e.g., NaOH, HCl, or solvents) where reported.
- Report desorption efficiency and performance across multiple cycles, not only a single desorption test.
- Consider adsorbent degradation, leaching, spent-adsorbent disposal, and regeneration waste.

## 6. Practical implementation and real-water matrix effects

### 6.1 Wastewater matrix interference
- Natural organic matter and competing ions (e.g., Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻) may alter adsorption and block/access sites.
- Separate synthetic-water, spiked-real-water, and untreated-real-water evidence.
- Account for mixtures, low environmental concentrations, and the difference between percentage removal and absolute mass removed.

### 6.2 Process integration and operating configuration
- Batch versus fixed-bed/continuous-flow columns; include breakthrough and bed-life metrics when reported.
- Possible integration into polishing filters or treatment trains, including MBR-associated applications where supported.
- Assess hydraulic performance, adsorbent recovery, residuals, and practical operation.

## 7. Challenges and future perspectives
- Harmonized experimental reporting and comparable metrics.
- Validation at low concentrations and in real, multi-contaminant waters.
- Low-energy and lower-waste modification methods.
- Pilot-scale continuous-flow evaluation, regeneration, material safety, and end-of-life management.
- Mechanistic claims that require stronger direct evidence.

## 8. Conclusions
- Summarize trade-offs across processing complexity, cost/energy, adsorption performance, regeneration, matrix effects, and scale-up.
- Make material-selection recommendations conditional on effluent composition and treatment goal.
- Do not declare one material class universally optimal unless the evidence supports a fair, comparable basis.
