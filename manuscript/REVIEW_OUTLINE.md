# Manuscript outline

## 1. Introduction
- Occurrence and toxicity of antibiotic residues
- Limitations of conventional treatment
- Adsorption as a treatment option
- Need for a processing–cost–performance continuum

## 2. Adsorbent classification
### 2.1 Raw lignocellulosic biomass and natural waste
### 2.2 Natural clays and biopolymers
Includes natural clay minerals, unmodified and modified biopolymers, and relevant biopolymer-based composites, with clear category-level comparisons.
### 2.3 Biochar and modified biochar
Pristine, physically activated, chemically modified, and composite/loaded biochars.
### 2.4 Engineered and nano-carbon adsorbents
Activated carbon, carbon nanotubes, graphene-based materials, and atomically engineered/advanced nano-carbons.

## 3. Adsorption behaviour and interfacial mechanisms
- Kinetics and mass transfer
- Isotherms and the distinction between observed uptake and fitted qmax
- pH, antibiotic speciation and interfacial mechanisms

## 4. Techno-economic and sustainability evaluation
- Preparation chemicals, thermal/energy requirements and yield
- Regeneration, desorption and reuse
- Cost-normalized performance, energy and life-cycle boundaries

## 5. Practical implementation
- Real-water matrix effects (NOM, competing ions and mixtures)
- Batch versus fixed-bed/column operation
- Treatment integration (including MBR/polishing where evidence supports it)
- Spent adsorbent management

## 6. Challenges and future perspectives
- Comparable reporting and evidence quality
- Real-water validation and low-concentration performance
- Scale-up, regeneration, environmental risk and costs

## 7. Conclusions

The manuscript’s novelty should be the integrated comparison of processing complexity, cost/energy, adsorption performance, regeneration and scale-up—not simply “low-cost versus high-capacity” claims.
