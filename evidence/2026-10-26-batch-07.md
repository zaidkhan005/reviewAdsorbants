## 1. Sumariva et al. (2024) — clay honeycomb monoliths

### Preparation and characterization
- Five clay materials: Archidona, Blanca, Esquivias, Negra and Yuncos. Table 1 (PDF p. 2) gives BET surface areas 135, 39, 257, 21 and 58 m²/g; pore volumes 0.169, 0.079, 0.224, 0.042 and 0.102 cm³/g; reported CEC 42, n.a., 15, n.a. and 109 meq/100 g. Esquivias was selected based on adsorption response and textural properties, not BET area alone.
- Esquivias paste used water at 0.42 mL/g and was extruded into honeycomb monoliths: 1.4 cm diameter, 50 cells/cm², wall thickness 0.33 mm, 72% open frontal area. Calcination at 400 °C for 2 h was required to improve water resistance.
- After calcination, Esquivias BET surface area was about 220 m²/g, with a type-II isotherm and H3 hysteresis and pore-size distribution centered on 4 nm mesopores (PDF pp. 4–6; Fig. 5).

### Powder adsorption
- Table 4 (PDF p. 5) gives amount of tetracycline retained per g of clay powder under light/air at 25 °C:
  - pH 3: Archidona 85.4, Blanca 12.1, Esquivias 102.4, Negra 33.7, Yuncos 81.2 mg/g.
  - pH 7: Archidona 24.2, Blanca 6.1, Esquivias 80.5, Negra 23.0, Yuncos 30.0 mg/g.
  - pH 9: Archidona 42.2, Blanca 37.4, Esquivias 80.2, Negra 42.1, Yuncos 49.2 mg/g.
- A separate Esquivias discussion gives 64.2 and 64.8 mg/g at pH 3 and 9, versus 79 mg/g at pH 7, for that test protocol; do not silently merge these results with Table 4’s different setup.
- Temperature tests with Esquivias powder gave 72 mg/g at 4 °C and 116.8 mg/g at 60 °C. Authors caution that elevated temperature may degrade tetracycline.
- TC UV–Vis spectra shift with pH. Reported lambda-max was 356, 358 and 375 nm at pH 3, 7 and 9; three pH-specific calibration curves were used. Spectral changes can confound estimates of apparent removal if pH is not controlled or degradation products are not measured.

### Monolith operation and results
- Table 3 (PDF p. 4): conditions at pH 7 using calcined Esquivias honeycombs. Setup 1 used 3.14 g monolith and 500 mL solution (TC listed as 232/460 ppm), no air/light and no forced flow. Setup 2 used a 4.20 g monolith, 335 mL at 231 ppm, 50 mL/min recirculation, no air but light present. Setup 3 used 3.43 g monolith, 2,000 mL at 232 ppm, air/light present and 2,800 mL/min flow.
- At 50 mL/min and initial 231 ppm TC, up to 69% removal was observed after three days of recirculation. Authors also report 80% without forced circulation and 82% at 2,800 mL/min, but these results involved different and longer test periods and are not directly comparable. Fig. 6 (PDF p. 6) shows the spectral evolution at 50 mL/min; Fig. 7 (PDF p. 7) contrasts no forced circulation with high flow.
- This is recirculation evidence at high mg/L concentration, not a once-through pilot column validated at trace environmental levels.

### Mechanisms and regeneration
- The authors infer adsorption on the external clay surface and mesopores. Evidence is consistent with cation exchange and pH/speciation effects; potential interactions include electrostatics, hydrophobic effects, Ca/Mg coordination, and hydrogen bonding. XRD (Fig. 9, PDF p. 8) changes little after TC treatment, so interlayer intercalation appears negligible in this particular material.
- Separate post-use treatment was investigated: TPO under oxygen generated H2O/CO2 signals and TC-associated decomposition was reported over roughly 200–600 °C. Authors propose heating loaded filters around 300–350 °C for 1–2 h to decompose much of retained TC without damaging the clay.
- That thermal treatment is a separate adsorbent-regeneration/destruction step, not adsorption. The paper calls it decomposition/mineralization; avoid asserting complete mineralization solely from H2O/CO2 signals without a full product/toxicity mass balance.
- The authors call the monoliths low-cost and easier to handle than powder, avoiding sludge/separation. No detailed TEA or full LCA is included. Fabrication calcination (400 °C, 2 h) and post-use heating (300–350 °C proposed) should both be included in practical-energy discussion.

### Use in the review
Strong Stage 2 case on material architecture: moulding/extrusion improves recovery and handling without chemical functionalization. Keep powder mg/g results, monolith percentage removal and recirculation flow/residence time separate. No pilot-scale once-through validation or harmonized cost model.

## 2. Nugraha et al. (2025) — adsorption for hospital wastewater

### Scope and occurrence
- Secondary review of HWW adsorption; HWW can contain antibiotics, other pharmaceuticals, disinfectants, metals, pathogens and other micropollutants. Its composition varies substantially by hospital and region.
- The review reports cross-study ranges of about 0.04–17.9 µg/L antibiotics in HWW versus 0.03–1.75 µg/L in municipal WWTP effluents (PDF p. 3, citing previous studies). These are not new measurements in this review.
- Adsorbents discussed include PAC/GAC, waste-derived carbon, graphene oxide, zeolite/clay/silica, MOFs and composites. Table 3 (PDF pp. 8–9) gives adsorption a broad antibiotic-removal estimate around 70–90%, but this compiles heterogeneous studies and must not be used as a controlled performance benchmark.

### Selected HWW examples, as summarized by the review
- Zeolite: 60.7% azithromycin removal, 75.6% ofloxacin and 43.6% sulfamethoxazole; respective concentrations listed are 391 ng/L AZM, 378 ng/L OFL and 78.6 ng/L SMX.
- Silica aerogel: 98.5% removal from HWW spiked with 10 mg/L doxycycline. This is a spiked system at a much higher concentration than many measured HWW samples, not equivalent to unspiked HWW validation.
- A sugarcane-bagasse fixed-bed example for SMX gives 73.3% removal/0.23 mg/g in a lab column and 78.79%/0.163 mg/g in a pilot column; water matrix needs checking in its original study before treating as direct HWW evidence.
- A 30 m³ powdered activated-carbon reactor treated wastewater from a municipal WWTP connected to a hospital/clinics; a separate full-scale GAC example reported 74% erythromycin and 88% carbamazepine removal after biological treatment at a 227,000 m³/day reclamation plant. These support adsorption as a polishing step, but are not dedicated hospital-only adsorption plants.
- Review states PAC/GAC are the materials implemented at full scale, while many advanced adsorbents remain laboratory-stage. Key gaps: high organic load and matrix variability; regeneration costs and material losses; long-term real-HWW evidence; handling and hospital space constraints; end-of-life management.

### Mechanisms, regeneration and review use
- General themes include pH/speciation, adsorbent surface charge, pore structure, salts/metal-ion and organic-matter competition, and mass-transfer conditions. Chemical elution can transfer antibiotics to a secondary liquid waste; thermal regeneration may destroy adsorbates but consumes energy and requires thermally stable media.
- The review proposes combined adsorption–degradation systems, but adsorption capacity and subsequent destruction should be reported as separate operations.
- Supplementary Tables S1–S6 were not included in the uploaded main PDF. Use this review for HWW context and backward citation chasing, not as an independent source for each summarized experimental value. Distinguish direct HWW, spiked HWW, hospital-connected municipal effluent and ordinary municipal effluent in the extraction table.

## 3. Sousa et al. (2026) — spent-brewery-grain biochar in fixed beds

### Preparation, characterization and wastewater
- Target antibiotics: sulfamethoxazole (SMX), trimethoprim (TMP), ciprofloxacin (CIP), single and ternary mixtures.
- Spent brewery grain from pilsener production was dried at room temperature, ground and microwave-pyrolysed at 800 °C for 20 min under N2 (62 mL/min; heating rate 15 °C/min). Product was washed with ~1.2 M HCl, then distilled water to neutral pH; leachate was neutralized with NaOH and neutralized NaCl solution discarded. Product dried at 100 °C for 24 h. Yield 23%. No chemical/physical activation was used.
- Granular column media: 0.5–1.0 mm; batch powder: ≤180 μm. Granular BC-SBG had BET 281 m²/g, pore volume 0.12 cm³/g, micropore volume 0.11 cm³/g, apparent density 0.87 g/cm³ and PZC 4.0. Powdered form was similar (BET ~312 m²/g, pore volume 0.14, micropore 0.12).
- Effluent was collected in May 2024 from the outlet of the Ílhavo urban WWTP (mean plant flow 39,279 m³/day; primary and secondary biological treatment). It was vacuum-filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and autoclaved at 120 °C for 15 min, stored at 4 °C up to three weeks. Measured conductivity 3.3 mS/cm, pH 7.8 ± 0.1 and DOC 31 mg/L. Thus the test matrix is real secondary effluent after filtration/autoclaving, not raw untreated wastewater.

### Batch adsorption
- Single-solute feeds were 20 μmol/L, 25 °C, 80 rpm, pH 8 (0.01 M phosphate-buffered distilled water or the prepared WWTP effluent). Kinetic doses were 0.75 g/L in buffered water and 1.50 g/L in wastewater; contact times 5–240 min; equilibrium achieved within 60 min. Experiments were triplicated (n=3).
- Both PFO and PSO fit reasonably; PSO generally gave R² ≥0.97 and Syx ≤0.83. A PSO fit is not proof of a specific chemical mechanism.
- Equilibrium: 240 min; dose range 0.25–3.75 g/L in buffered water, 0.80–3.80 g/L in wastewater. Langmuir fitted q_m in buffered water: TMP 18 ± 1 μmol/g, CIP 16 ± 1 μmol/g, SMX 7.1 ± 0.1 μmol/g.
- With wastewater matrix, fitted capacities for SMX and CIP fell about 7% and 11%, while TMP fell about 38%. At pH 8 and PZC 4 the surface is negatively charged; authors interpret anionic SMX as experiencing repulsion, while TMP/CIP uptake reflects weak electrostatics, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic/π–π interactions and competition from dissolved organic matter.

### Fixed-bed conditions and findings
- Glass column: 13 cm long, 1.0 cm inner diameter, 4.4 cm bed depth, ~2.00 ± 0.01 g granular BC-SBG, 25 °C. Feed concentration 20 μmol/L, pH 8. Buffered-water flow rates: 0.042, 0.083 and 0.167 L/h. Authors select 0.083 L/h (~1057 L m−2 h−1 as reported) as a compromise between time and uptake.
- Table 2 (PDF p. 7): at 0.042 L/h, Thomas-fitted capacity q_TH was 11.4 ± 0.2 μmol/g SMX, 33 ± 1 TMP and 18.9 ± 0.2 CIP. Higher flow shortened breakthrough and reduced uptake due to lower contact time/mass-transfer limits.
- Table 3 (PDF p. 7): buffered single-solute breakthrough at 0.042 L/h: SMX tb=14.4 h, Vb=0.6 L; TMP tb=64.8 h, Vb=2.7 L; CIP tb=38.4 h, Vb=1.6 L. At 0.167 L/h, tb declined to 0.6 h SMX, 6.6 h TMP and 4.8 h CIP.
- In the prepared wastewater at the selected 0.083 L/h, Yan model q_Y decreased from 7.8 to 4.15 μmol/g for SMX, 32.3 to 7.8 for TMP and 18.1 to 3.33 for CIP compared with buffered water. Table 3’s experimental saturation q_s under lower-flow tests also decreased from 7.3/46.7/28.7 μmol/g (SMX/TMP/CIP) in buffered water to 2.2/2.8/2.5 μmol/g in wastewater. Keep q_Y, q_TH and experimental q_s distinct.
- Fig. 4 (PDF p. 8) shows steeper breakthrough in wastewater than buffered water; Fig. 5 (PDF p. 10) shows earlier breakthrough in ternary mixtures. For ternary feeds, buffered-water q_Y was 5.4 μmol/g SMX, 17.7 TMP and 17.4 CIP; in wastewater it was 3.61 SMX, 6.0 TMP and 5.80 CIP. Mixed antibiotics and the real matrix reduce effective capacity/service life.
- The authors find the Yan model fits best overall (R² ≥0.977 in flow tests and ≥0.986 in matrix comparisons); Thomas and Yoon–Nelson are also evaluated. Model-derived capacity should be labelled as such.

### Microwave regeneration
- TMP-saturated BC-SBG was treated at 600 °C for 20 min in the microwave oven under N2, then re-used for two additional fixed-bed TMP tests. This is not a multi-cycle regeneration result for all three antibiotics.
- BET area increased from 281 m²/g fresh to 380 after first regeneration and 462 after second; pore volume rose from 0.12 to 0.21 cm³/g after the second cycle; micropore volume from 0.11 to 0.19 cm³/g.
- Yan model q_Y rose from 32.3 μmol/g fresh to 94.9 after first regeneration and 114 after second. Experimental saturation q_s rose from 38 to 55 to 68 μmol/g. The paper reports regeneration efficiencies around 118% and 146%, defined as q_regenerated/q_initial × 100%, not percentage of antibiotic desorbed. Capacity increased alongside textural changes.
- Antibiotic transformation and emissions during the 600 °C treatment are not documented as a complete target-specific mass balance.

### Production LCA
- LCA functional output is 1 kg BC-SBG, using ISO 14040, Ecoinvent 3.9.1 and ReCiPe 2016 midpoint indicators: global warming, ozone formation (human health), terrestrial acidification, mineral-resource scarcity and fossil-resource scarcity. A 30 km truck distance from brewery to lab is assumed; laboratory electricity is estimated from equipment power/time/capacity; combustion flue gases from pyrolysis vapour treatment are measured/estimated with FTIR.
- Total numeric impact values are in unavailable supplementary Table S4. Main-text Fig. 7 reports:
  - washing contributes 45% global warming and 46% fossil-resource scarcity; drying electricity contributes 41% to each.
  - washing contributes 82% mineral-resource scarcity, principally from HCl/NaOH.
  - pyrolysis contributes 63% ozone formation (human health) and 39% terrestrial acidification, driven by NOx/SO2/NO2 from combustion of pyrolysis vapours. Washing adds 36% terrestrial acidification, mainly HCl.
  - collection, water use and generated wastewater each contribute <3% to the listed emissions categories.
- LCA represents lab-scale production, not a cradle-to-grave treatment process. The largest hotspots are washing chemicals, drying electricity and pyrolysis emissions, not the use of waste biomass itself. Do not claim full-life-cycle superiority or current treatment cost based on this production-only LCA.

## Cross-paper conclusions
1. The clay monolith illustrates a material-architecture path: higher ease of recovery and reduced sludge compared with powder, but flow outcomes are recirculation experiments at high concentrations.
2. HWW complexity is a major reason laboratory removal rates do not translate directly into on-site hospital treatment; direct, spiked and municipal-connected effluents must be distinguished.
3. BC-SBG shows that batch capacities can overstate dynamic performance in complex wastewater and ternary antibiotic mixtures; column flow and breakthrough are critical metrics.
4. Regeneration changes the economics/environmental burden. Clay heating is a separate destruction step; brewery biochar's 600 °C regeneration improves TMP uptake but requires energy.
5. Open flags: SI parameters/costs; lack of harmonized cost data for clay monolith; HWW-review examples require primary-source checking; BC-SBG LCA totals and some method details are SI-dependent.
