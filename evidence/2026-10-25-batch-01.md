## 1) Yao et al. (2023) — Fe/N co-doped rape-straw biochar

### Identity and scope
- Journal: Waste Management, volume 168, pages 386–395 (2023).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2023.06.014
- Study type: Original experimental adsorption study.
- Scope: Single- and binary-solute adsorption of CIP and Cu2+; adsorption and separation, not catalytic oxidation.

### Material preparation and characterization
- Precursor: rape straw collected in Changsha, Hunan, China.
- Feed preparation: washed with deionized water, dried, crushed and sieved.
- Fe/N-BC recipe reported in the main text: 3.0 g rape straw; FeSO4·7H2O 0.2 M; urea 0.5 M; ascorbic acid 0.14 M; dispersed in 100 mL deionized water; ultrasonically mixed; dried at 80 °C; pyrolyzed at 600 °C with a 10 °C/min ramp for 120 min under N2 flow.
- Controls: Fe-BC omitted urea; N-BC omitted FeSO4·7H2O; raw BC omitted both dopants.
- Fe/N-BC specific surface area: 20.35 m2/g; pore volume 0.048 cm3/g (the paper reports overall area range 2.54–93.36 m2/g across the four materials). EDX, XRD, FTIR and XPS are used to support Fe/N incorporation.
- Yield, full energy balance, chemical-waste burden and preparation cost: not reported in the supplied main PDF.

### Experimental conditions
- Typical single-system comparison: 0.2 g/L adsorbent, 50 mg/L CIP and/or 50 mg/L Cu2+, 298 K, shaker 150 rpm.
- pH tests: pH 2–6; ionic strength tests: NaCl 0–100 mM.
- Isotherm concentration ranges: CIP 0–200 mg/L; Cu2+ 0–100 mg/L; isotherm temperature series 298, 308 and 318 K. Figure 2 caption gives pH 6.0 ± 0.2 for the single-system isotherms.
- Kinetic test: 20 mg Fe/N-BC in 100 mL solution containing 50 mg/L CIP and/or Cu2+; tests at 298–318 K.
- At least three repetitions; results reported as mean ± standard deviation.
- Water-matrix experiment: 50 mg/L CIP + 50 mg/L Cu2+ in deionized water, tap water and groundwater. Groundwater was collected in Changsha. The main text does not provide enough matrix composition to isolate why the groundwater result is higher.

### Quantitative results
**Langmuir qmax in single-solute systems (Table 2, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 390):**
- CIP: 25.36 mg/g at 298 K (R2 0.92); 39.20 mg/g at 308 K (R2 0.89); 46.45 mg/g at 318 K (R2 0.90).
- Cu2+: 27.92 mg/g at 298 K (R2 0.94); 28.35 mg/g at 308 K (R2 0.97); 30.77 mg/g at 318 K (R2 0.94).
- For CIP, Freundlich fits had higher R2 values (0.98–0.99); for Cu2+, Langmuir fit was generally slightly better than Freundlich (Langmuir R2 0.94–0.97 vs Freundlich 0.91–0.96 across the reported temperatures). This model comparison is descriptive and does not itself prove a molecular mechanism.
- Under the fixed 50 mg/L conditions in Figure 1, reported uptake by Fe/N-BC was 17.30 mg/g for CIP and 20.51 mg/g for Cu2+ (PDF p. 2 / printed p. 387; Figure 1 caption specifies 0.2 g/L dose and 298 K). Do not report these as qmax.

**Binary CIP + Cu2+ system (Table 2, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 390):**
- Langmuir qmax at 318 K: CIP 18.84 mg/g (R2 0.95) and Cu2+ 17.23 mg/g (R2 0.90), both below the corresponding single-system values.
- At fixed Cu2+ concentration, increasing CIP concentration from 0 to 200 mg/L reduced Cu2+ uptake from 20.51 to 4.66 mg/g (PDF p. 6 / printed p. 391).
- In the binary pH study, at pH 2 the reported CIP uptake was 15.37 mg/g and almost no Cu2+ was removed. Between pH 3 and 6, CIP uptake was reported in the range 3.09–7.22 mg/g while Cu2+ uptake was 12.65–18.0 mg/g.
- Separation factor for CIP reached 7.42 at pH 2; the reported Cu2+ separation factor rose from 0.14 at pH 2 to 3.82 at pH 6 (PDF p. 6 / printed p. 391). This is a binary mixture selectivity result, not a demonstrated full-scale separation process.

**Ionic strength and water matrix:**
- CIP uptake fell from 17.30 to 11.33 mg/g as NaCl increased from 0 to 100 mM; Cu2+ uptake rose from 20.51 to 33.72 mg/g over the same range (PDF pp. 4–5 / printed pp. 389–390).
- Authors describe the ionic-strength effect on CIP as negligible, but the reported 17.30-to-11.33 mg/g change is about a 35% decrease. Flag the wording/performance characterization for careful interpretation rather than repeating “negligible” uncritically.
- In the binary real-water matrix test, reported performance order was groundwater > deionized water > tap water. Relative to deionized water, uptake reportedly increased 1.50-fold for CIP and 1.18-fold for Cu2+ in groundwater; tap-water uptake declined slightly (PDF p. 8 / printed p. 393). These are tap-water/groundwater tests with high spiked concentrations, not municipal or hospital wastewater validation.

### Kinetics, mechanisms and reusability
- Pseudo-second-order (PSO) fits were reported as better than pseudo-first-order fits in many tests; for single-system CIP, PSO R2 ranged 0.94–0.97, while the table reports different R2 by temperature for both models (Table 1, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 390). Do not infer chemisorption as established solely from PSO fit.
- Isotherm interpretation: authors describe CIP as better fit by Freundlich and Cu2+ by Langmuir in single systems; in binary systems, their interpretation similarly distinguishes heterogeneous/multilayer-like CIP behaviour and more Langmuir-like Cu2+ behaviour. Treat these as model-based descriptions.
- XPS changes are discussed for C–N, π–π* satellite, C–O, M–O/C=O and Fe oxidation-state features. Authors propose pore filling, π–π interaction, electrostatic interaction and hydrogen bonding for CIP; surface complexation/iron-associated interactions and electrostatics for Cu2+ (PDF pp. 7–8 / printed pp. 392–393). The claim of Cu reduction needs careful oxidation-state verification; the main figure confirms Cu 2p features but the narrative should not be paraphrased as conclusive proof of reduction without resolving the chemical-state assignment.
- Five sequential co-adsorption cycles were conducted. The text states Cu2+ adsorption declined significantly after five cycles and CIP performance declined gradually; exact numerical cycle-by-cycle values are only in Figure 3i and are not transcribed as precise values here.
- The main text provides no desorption/regeneration chemistry, material recovery yield or spent-regenerant management.

### Limitations and use in manuscript
- Useful for Stage 4 / atomically engineered or heteroatom/metal-doped biochar discussion; compare single vs competitive adsorption and pH-driven selectivity.
- Not evidence for economic feasibility: cost, yield, energy, regeneration consumables and lifecycle costs are missing.
- Matrix evidence is limited to tap water and groundwater with high added concentrations; do not call it a real-wastewater study.
- Keep fitted qmax separate from observed uptake and ensure the qmax value is attached to the temperature and model used.

## 2) Jaria et al. (2019) — waste-derived GAC in municipal-wastewater fixed beds

### Identity and scope
- Journal: Science of the Total Environment 683, 699–708 (2019).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2019.05.198
- Study type: Original experimental continuous fixed-bed study.
- PSA-PA is a granular activated carbon made from primary paper-mill sludge (precursor) and ammonium lignosulfonate (binder).
- Targets: carbamazepine (CBZ; antiepileptic), sulfamethoxazole (SMX; antibiotic), paroxetine (PAR; antidepressant). For the antibiotic-specific synthesis, only SMX should count as the antibiotic target; the other two are valuable co-contaminant comparators.

### Preparation and column setup
- Production outline in the main text: dry primary sludge mixed with 50% ammonium lignosulfonate solution at 6:5 mass ratio; dry and pyrolyze at 500 °C for 10 min under N2; impregnate the resulting material with KOH at 1:1 mass ratio; pyrolyze again at 800 °C for 150 min under N2; wash with HCl and distilled water; dry at 105 °C for 24 h; sieve to 0.5–1.0 mm. The paper refers to a separate production paper for full characterization details.
- Virgin PSA-PA BET surface area: 671 m2/g; pore volume 0.37 cm3/g; micropore volume (Dubinin–Radushkevich) 0.27 cm3/g (Table 4, PDF p. 8 / printed p. 706).
- Column: glass column 13 cm total height, 2.5 cm internal diameter; 2.6 cm packed-bed depth; adsorbent mass about 3.55–3.61 g; 25 ± 1 °C.
- Wastewater: secondary/biologically treated effluent from a local WWTP serving 159,700 population-equivalents. Five campaigns, March–June 2018. Wastewater was filtered through 0.45 μm membrane, stored in darkness at 4 °C and used within 15 days. Background target pharmaceuticals were below detection before spiking.
- CBZ flow-rate screen: feed concentration 5 mg/L, flow rates 4.3, 8.6 and 13.0 L/day in both distilled water and wastewater. Subsequent single/ternary wastewater feeds: 5 mg/L of each compound, at the selected 4.3 L/day flow rate. Wastewater pH was approximately 7.3–7.7.
- Breakthrough threshold: C/C0 ≈ 0.1 (10%) was selected for t10%. Reported qtotal is obtained by integrating the curve over the experiment; because curves did not reach full exhaustion (C/C0 = 1) within the experiment, some qtotal values are time-window capacities rather than guaranteed true saturation capacities.

### Observed fixed-bed capacities (Table 1, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 703)
**CBZ in distilled water, single-solute feed (5 mg/L):**
- At 4.3 L/day: qtotal = 39.91 mg/g; t10% = 2.92 days; volume treated at breakthrough = 12.61 L.
- At 8.6 L/day: qtotal = 31.66 mg/g; t10% = 0.83 days; volume = 7.17 L.
- At 13.0 L/day: qtotal = 19.95 mg/g; t10% = 0.21 days; volume = 2.72 L.
- Increasing flow shortened contact time and lowered observed capacity/service time.

**Single-solute feeds in municipal wastewater at 4.3 L/day (5 mg/L):**
- CBZ qtotal = 6.65 mg/g; t10% = 0.33 days; breakthrough volume = 1.41 L.
- SMX qtotal = 2.23 mg/g; t10% = 0.10 days; breakthrough volume = 0.45 L.
- PAR qtotal = 4.46 mg/g; t10% = 0.01 days; breakthrough volume = 0.04 L.
- The paper’s authors explain low SMX retention as possibly due to electrostatic repulsion at wastewater pH (~7.3–7.7) between negatively charged PSA-PA and SMX. PAR's near-immediate breakthrough is a separate pharmaceutical behaviour; its possible biodegradation in wastewater is presented as a hypothesis, not quantified.

**Ternary wastewater feed (5 mg/L each; 4.3 L/day):**
- CBZ qtotal = 5.58 mg/g; SMX qtotal = 0.67 mg/g; PAR qtotal = 8.57 mg/g.
- In competition, CBZ and especially SMX capacities declined, whereas PAR’s integrated qtotal rose. This does not mean PAR is an antibiotic or that it has the best practical removal: its breakthrough time is nearly zero and qtotal reflects the complete curve/integration window.

**Fitted values are not observed capacities:**
- For CBZ in distilled water at 4.3 L/day, the Yan fitted qY = 59.6 ± 0.6 mg/g while observed qtotal = 39.91 mg/g; Thomas qTH = 64.3 ± 1.2 mg/g (Tables 1–2, PDF pp. 5–6 / printed pp. 703–704).
- The authors note the Yan model best fit the curves overall, yet fits and experimental capacity diverged; models overestimated capacities in several conditions. Use the experimental curve-integrated values where comparison of actual operation is intended and list fitted model values separately.

### Regeneration and stability
- Saturated PSA-PA after CBZ adsorption from distilled water was regenerated at 500 °C under N2 for 90 min. The same fixed-bed conditions were used for cycle 1 and cycle 2.
- CBZ qtotal declined from 39.91 mg/g (virgin bed) to 24.95 mg/g after the first regeneration and 11.49 mg/g after the second (Table 1, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 703); corresponding losses are approximately 37.5% and 71.2% relative to the virgin value.
- BET area declined from 671 to 617 and 463 m2/g for cycle 0, cycle 1 and cycle 2, respectively; post-use cycle-2 area was 391 m2/g (Table 4, PDF p. 8 / printed p. 706).
- Authors recommend no more than one reuse cycle, considering capacity loss together with thermal-regeneration energy and the possibility of producing fresh GAC from waste feedstocks.
- No adsorption/regeneration cost estimate or full life-cycle energy balance was supplied in this paper; the regeneration temperature/time are inputs for a later process-cost comparison, not a complete cost calculation.

### Mechanisms, limitations and use in manuscript
- Main contribution: practical column evidence and matrix/competition effects, especially real biologically treated municipal effluent.
- Feed water was filtered and spiked; it was not an unmodified sample with measured background target concentrations, which were below detection. The matrix is real, but the experiments are controlled spiked-water tests.
- Only SMX contributes direct antibiotic-specific evidence. CBZ and PAR should not be merged into antibiotic-only performance averages.
- Adsorption performance is constrained by flow rate, short contact time, pH/charge effects and multi-solute competition. The paper does not offer batch kinetic/isotherm qmax for SMX in this column experiment.
- Breakthrough curves did not reach full exhaustion within the test window. Do not assume every integrated qtotal represents a true saturation capacity.
- No separate mechanistic spectroscopy in this paper's fixed-bed tests; avoid assigning molecular mechanisms beyond the authors' discussion of charge/competition.

## 3) Gao et al. (2024) — KOH-activated dyeing-sludge biochar

### Identity and scope
- Journal: Science of the Total Environment 912, 169035 (2024 issue; available online 5 Dec 2023 in the PDF metadata).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.169035
- Study type: Original experimental adsorption study with batch, simulated-matrix and fixed-bed experiments, plus a regeneration and preparation-cost estimate.
- Adsorbent: KOH-activated dyeing sludge-derived biochar. The optimized material is KSC-8, pyrolyzed at 800 °C. It is chemically activated waste-derived biochar, not atomically doped biochar.

### Preparation and characterization
- Precursor: dewatered dyeing sludge from a printing/dyeing plant in Jiaxing, Zhejiang; reported solids content 61.23%.
- Sludge dried at 105 °C for 72 h, ground and passed through 60-mesh sieve.
- Sludge and KOH mixed at 1:1 mass ratio; pyrolysis at 700, 800 or 900 °C for 3 h, ramp 5 °C/min under N2; wash with 3 M HCl for 6 h; rinse to neutral pH and dry at 75 °C overnight.
- KSC-7, KSC-8 and KSC-9 correspond to 700, 800 and 900 °C; SC-7/8/9 are unactivated controls.
- BET surface areas reported: SC-7/8/9 = 360.3, 306.8, 284.6 m2/g; KSC-7/8/9 = 791.2, 1178.4, 926.5 m2/g. KSC-8 pore volume was 0.74 cm3/g. The study reports a developed microporous structure and FTIR evidence of oxygen-containing and aromatic groups.
- Main-text characterization states pores after activation are mostly below 1 nm. The paper also refers to a tetracycline molecular size under 1.27 nm when discussing pore filling; the relationship between the pore-size distribution and access of TC to the smallest pores deserves careful interpretation, not a blanket claim that every micropore is accessible.

### Batch conditions and adsorption-performance data
- Main method: conical flasks, thermostatic shaker at 180 rpm, 100 mL solution; TC initial concentration of 20 mg/L for the kinetic screen; initial concentration 10–800 mg/L for TC isotherms at 298 K. The mass/dose used for these batch tests is not clearly stated in the supplied main-text PDF; supplementary methods are needed before exact reproduction/comparison.
- The main text reports an optimized qmax of 1081.3 mg/g for KSC-8 from the Langmuir isotherm model (Table 1, PDF p. 6). It is a fitted model parameter, not an observed uptake at trace concentration.
- Table 1 Langmuir fits: SC-7 150.5 mg/g (R2 0.986); SC-8 210.7 (0.910); SC-9 287.7 (0.999); KSC-7 531.8 (0.975); KSC-8 1081.3 (0.966); KSC-9 613.8 (0.954). KSC-8 had the greatest fitted value among the six tested materials.
- At pH 5, the paper reports a capacity of 311.85 mg/g under the pH study conditions; TC capacity declined above pH 7. It reports TC pKa values of 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7, and KSC-8 pHpzc = 4.4 (PDF p. 6 and Fig. 4c on PDF p. 7).
- In a temperature comparison where TC was 100 mg/L, reported experimental capacities were 305.99 mg/g at 298 K, 323.91 mg/g at 308 K and 351.48 mg/g at 318 K. PSO fitted R2 values were 0.997, 0.999 and 0.996, above PFO values 0.910, 0.937 and 0.913 (PDF p. 6). A PSO fit alone does not prove chemisorption.
- The intraparticle-diffusion plot was interpreted as having two stages, and neither fit passed through the origin; the authors conclude intraparticle diffusion was not the sole rate-limiting step.
- The paper’s methods give TC isotherm tests at 298 K, while the conclusion states that the 1081.3 mg/g uptake was at 278 K. This temperature conflict requires verification against SI or the authors’ record.

### Water matrix and antibiotic scope
- Simulated coexisting-solute study used glucose to represent COD (200 mg/L COD), NH4Cl to represent ammonia-N (30 mg/L) and NaCl to represent chloride (300 mg/L); authors report only slight effects on TC adsorption rate.
- Actual antibiotic tailwater: reported COD 57.19 mg/L and ammonia-N 0.40 mg/L; the paper says TC was completely removed within 30 min and reports a small kinetic change from 0.012 to 0.011. The wastewater source, full composition, antibiotic background and the units/meaning of the “kinetics” value are not sufficiently clear in the main PDF.
- Four target antibiotics were tested to assess wider applicability: TC, ciprofloxacin (CIP), sulfamethazine (SMZ) and tilmicosin. Reported removal: 100% for TC, CIP and SMZ, and 96.34% for tilmicosin (Fig. 4e, PDF p. 7). Do not imply these four percentages are all proven in the same real-water matrix unless the missing SI confirms that experimental detail.
- The main text states the TC initial concentration for the real-water fixed-bed test was 10 mg/L and pH 5. Its description of the “real antibiotic tailwater” needs clearer sourcing before using the result as strong real-effluent evidence.

### Regeneration, fixed-bed operation and economics
- Regeneration: Fenton treatment of the spent adsorbent in 100 mL solution containing Fe2+ 2 mM and H2O2 100 mM at initial pH 3 for 3 h, followed by washing and drying.
- Five cycles reported. Body text says regeneration efficiency declined to 86.88% and adsorption capacity to 86.35% of the initial value by cycle 5; first three cycles maintained 100% removal. Abstract instead says “maintained 86.35% TC removal efficiency after five cycles,” so labels (regeneration efficiency, retained capacity, removal efficiency) are inconsistent and should not be merged into one figure.
- Fixed-bed setup: cylindrical container 100 mm long × 16 mm diameter, 80 mL volume; 0.25 g KSC-8; TC influent 10 mg/L, pH 5; flow 1.5 mL/min; nominal adsorbent dose 3.13 g/L; hydraulic retention time about 50 min.
- The paper reports 13.59 L could be treated before TC reached a 1 mg/L critical concentration, with capacity 543.46 mg/g at that endpoint. It separately reports “maximum” fixed-bed capacity 1124.2 mg/g. Because the first value corresponds to an influent mass/adsorbent-mass calculation under the stated assumptions and the second exceeds the batch Langmuir qmax (1081.3 mg/g), the definitions, integration window and calculation should be rechecked before using 1124.2 as a validated experimental capacity.
- Economic estimate: reagents about $3/kg adsorbent; pyrolysis electricity around 25 kWh and cost about $5.6/kg; combined preparation cost about $9/kg, compared with the paper’s stated average activated-carbon cost of $28/kg (PDF p. 7). Functional unit/boundaries, currency year, local energy price, plant scale, drying/acid wash/wastewater treatment, yield, equipment, Fenton regeneration and spent-media disposal are not fully established. Use as the authors’ preliminary preparation-cost estimate, not a complete techno-economic analysis.
- Regeneration’s Fenton process introduces oxidant and ferrous-salt demands and may generate transformation products; residual TC/intermediate products are suggested by the authors as one reason for performance decline, but fate/toxicity and residual oxidant handling need separate assessment.

### Mechanisms and source inconsistencies
- Authors propose pore filling, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions. Support described includes surface-area/pore data, zeta potential and XPS/FTIR features. These results are consistent with proposed interactions but do not quantify each mechanism’s contribution independently.
- **Surface area discrepancy:** abstract, characterization text and Table 2 report KSC-8 SBET = 1178.4 m2/g; one discussion sentence on PDF p. 7 says 1078.4 m2/g. Prefer the repeated 1178.4 m2/g as the internally repeated table/characterization value but retain the conflict in the caveat register pending SI verification.
- **Removal-equation discrepancy:** Equation (2) on PDF p. 3 prints R% = (C0 − Ct)/Ct × 100. The conventional removal expression uses C0 in the denominator. This appears to be a typographical or calculation-reporting error; the article’s percentage-removal values should not be fully validated until the authors’ intended formula or the data calculation is confirmed.
- **Fixed-bed q discrepancy:** reported 1124.2 mg/g exceeds the batch Langmuir qmax of 1081.3 mg/g; the paper itself describes the fixed-bed value as nearly the theoretical maximum despite it being larger. Needs verification.
- **Temperature discrepancy:** isotherm method says 298 K; conclusion ties 1081.3 mg/g to 278 K. Needs verification.
- **Regeneration metric discrepancy:** abstract labels 86.35% as removal efficiency; main text reports regeneration efficiency 86.88% and retained capacity 86.35%.
- No independent metal/leachables assessment, spent-regeneration effluent assessment or full LCA is evident in the supplied main text.

## Cross-paper synthesis: what this batch supports

1. **Higher engineering does not automatically imply reliable practical performance.** Fe/N-BC has a modest reported BET surface area (20.35 m2/g) but fitted CIP qmax of 46.45 mg/g at 318 K; KSC-8 reports a much larger BET area and fitted qmax, but the extremely high qmax and fixed-bed capacity need their conditions and internal inconsistencies verified before it is compared numerically with other materials.
2. **Single-solute qmax is not sufficient for real-water claims.** Yao et al. demonstrate competition with Cu2+ and pH-dependent selectivity; Jaria et al. show sharply lower CBZ/SMX capacity in a biologically treated municipal effluent than in distilled water; Gao et al. report limited coexisting-solute effects but provide incomplete matrix metadata in the main text.
3. **Continuous-flow performance must be compared with continuous-flow performance.** Jaria et al. give breakthrough/service-life data and observed curve-integrated capacities; its fitted Thomas/Yan capacities are not interchangeable with experimentally integrated qtotal. Gao et al. give one fixed-bed design and endpoint, but the reported 1124.2 mg/g is internally inconsistent with the batch qmax and needs resolution.
4. **Regeneration imposes a process penalty.** The GAC loses capacity over repeated 500 °C regeneration; KSC-8 uses Fenton chemistry and shows lower cycle-5 metrics; Fe/N-BC’s numeric cycle data require extraction from the figure/SI before comparing reusability.
5. **No apples-to-apples ranking yet.** These papers use different target compounds, feed concentrations, temperatures, doses, water matrices and reactors. The headline capacities must not be plotted or ranked without harmonizing these factors and separating fitted qmax, observed batch qe, and fixed-bed capacities.
