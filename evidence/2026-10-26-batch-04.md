## 1. Du et al. (2025) — phosphoric-acid-modified cotton-husk biochar

### Identity, synthesis and characterization
- *Industrial Crops & Products* 226 (2025), article 120649; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.indcrop.2025.120649.
- Material: cotton-husk biochar activated by phosphoric acid (PCH); unactivated cotton-husk biochar (CH) is the control.
- Cotton husks were collected in Xinjiang, China, washed, dried at 60 °C, crushed and sieved to 80 mesh.
- Synthesis screen: 10 g cotton-husk powder immersed in 15 mL H3PO4 solution at 10%, 20%, 30% or 40%; stirred 15 min; pyrolysed for 120 min at 300, 400, 500, 600 or 700 °C, heating ramp 5 °C/min under N2 flow 0.1 L/min. Product was washed with DI water to remove excess acid, dried at 80 °C, and ground to <0.2 mm.
- Selected PCH recipe: 30% H3PO4 and 600 °C. CH control was pyrolysed at 600 °C for 120 min without H3PO4, then cleaned, dried and ground similarly.
- PCH BET surface area = 316.1 m²/g and total pore volume = 0.45 cm³/g; CH pore volume is reported as 0.19 cm³/g. PCH showed type-IV/H4 behaviour with mesopores and a hierarchical pore structure; average pore size cited in the mechanism discussion is 8.2 nm.
- FTIR/XPS support introduction of oxygen- and phosphorus-containing surface groups, especially O–P and C–P–O. Surface point of zero charge (pHpzc) reported = 2.57.
- Production yield, acid consumption per kg final product, washing-water demand, acid-containing wastewater treatment, measured pyrolysis energy, and a complete life-cycle inventory are not stated in the supplied main text; details of material characterization/cost refer to SI Tables S2–S4.

### Adsorption design and performance
- Kinetics: 10 mg PCH in 100 mL CIP solution (0.1 g/L), initial concentrations 30, 60 and 120 mg/L, pH 6, 120 rpm, samples from 5 to 720 min. The article reports equilibrium at about 4 h for the studied concentrations.
- Experimental kinetic uptake, Table 1 (PDF p. 6): 281.4 mg/g at 30 mg/L, 410.4 mg/g at 60 mg/L, and 599.8 mg/g at 120 mg/L. PSO fitted capacities are 279.2, 407.2 and 592.2 mg/g, with R² 0.997–0.999; PFO fits are weaker/less consistent. A PSO fit by itself is not proof of chemisorption.
- Intraparticle-diffusion plots/table (Table 2, PDF p. 6) resolve three stages attributed to film diffusion, interparticle diffusion and adsorption at sites. The fitted lines do not pass through the origin, so intraparticle diffusion was not the sole rate-controlling step.
- Isotherms: 100 mL CIP at initial concentrations 20–160 mg/L, dose 10 mg PCH (0.1 g/L), pH 6, 12 h, at 15, 25 and 35 °C. Table 3 (PDF p. 7) gives:
  - 288 K: experimental Qe = 524.1 mg/g; Langmuir fitted Qm = 496.4 mg/g (R² 0.973).
  - 298 K: experimental Qe = 572.8 mg/g; Langmuir fitted Qm = 523.0 mg/g (R² 0.957).
  - 308 K: experimental Qe = 621.4 mg/g; Langmuir fitted Qm = 572.8 mg/g (R² 0.982).
  - Freundlich fits were poorer or more variable (R² 0.900–0.928); Temkin fits R² 0.950–0.979.
- **Important abstract/table mismatch:** the abstract describes 572.8 mg/g as the maximum capacity at 25 °C. In Table 3, 572.8 mg/g is the experimental Qe at 298 K, while the Langmuir fitted capacity at 298 K is 523.0 mg/g and the fitted Qm reaches 572.8 mg/g at 308 K. Keep the abstract statement separate from table-defined fitted Qm and measured Qe; do not silently call all of these the same “qmax.”
- pH edge: tested pH 2–10; article reports maximum observed capacity around 295.2 mg/g at pH 6. Authors cite CIP pKa values 6.25 and 8.78; pHpzc 2.57. They propose that excess H+ competes at low pH, while negatively charged PCH above pHpzc attracts cationic CIP under the conditions and then uptake falls under alkaline conditions as CIP becomes anionic.
- Coexisting ions: Na+, K+, NO3− and SO4²− had little effect in the reported tests; Ca²+ and Mg²+ lowered CIP uptake by about 12% relative to the pure-water control. CO3²− lowered uptake by 17.8%, 22.7% and 25.9% at the three tested concentrations.
- Dose screen: authors report 94.17% CIP removal when dose increased from 0.05 to 0.1 g/L at a cited initial concentration of 30 mg/L; raising dose 0.1 to 0.2 g/L increased removal only ~5.4%, while capacity fell from 282 to 149 mg/g. The Figure 5 caption groups initial concentrations as 30–120 mg/L; the exact dose-screen concentration associated with every plotted value is not fully explicit in the main text, so do not pair all those values as if they shared one known C0 without checking the figure/SI.

### Real-water applicability and practical caveat
- Secondary effluent from pharmaceutical wastewater was tested without pH adjustment at 1 g/L PCH, 120 rpm for 2 h. EEM fluorescence peaks diminished/disappeared; CODCr decreased from 486.45 to 50.14 mg/L and TOC from 71.63 to 21.33 mg/L; the effluent became visually clearer.
- **This is evidence of bulk organic-load/color reduction in a real wastewater matrix, not a quantified CIP-specific removal test.** The main text does not state the initial CIP concentration in this effluent or report a target-specific CIP measurement before/after PCH. Do not write “complete CIP removal from real wastewater” based on EEM/COD/TOC alone.
- Separate laboratory tests report at least 85% removal for orange-yellow G, tetracycline and acetaminophen below 40 mg/L, but those are not the real pharmaceutical effluent test and should be kept separate.

### Regeneration and cost
- Three-cycle adsorption/desorption comparisons used ethanol (EtOH), 2 M HCl and 2 M NaOH (Fig. 8a, PDF p. 9). The main text reports third-cycle capacities of 150.33, 156.49 and 196.96 mg/g (54.04%, 56.25% and 70.79% of the control capacity, respectively), but the ordering/interpretation of the three desorption routes is not completely clear from the prose. It says HCl and NaOH were less desirable than EtOH even though the listed sequence can be read otherwise. Preserve the three values and flag the route-to-value mapping for checking, rather than asserting a definitive ranking.
- Authors report preparation cost PCH = US$3.98/kg versus a cited commercial activated-carbon value of US$259.5/kg, and expected treatment cost of US$7.64 per kg CIP. These estimates depend on SI Table S4 and on a cited commercial carbon comparator; the main article does not fully establish consistent geography, scale, energy price, yield, acid-wash/wastewater treatment, equipment, labor, regeneration, or spent-media boundaries. Treat them as **author-reported preliminary cost estimates**, not a harmonized TEA or current procurement quote.
- Authors propose pore filling, electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding and π–π electron-donor/acceptor interactions. XPS shifts support interaction with phosphorus/oxygen groups. DFT/ESP analysis estimates adsorption energies with magnitude: O–P 1.113 eV, C–P–O 0.635 eV, C–O 0.425 eV, –OH 0.327 eV, and CH 0.261 eV (the paper reports negative adsorption energies; O–P is most favourable). These calculations support the authors’ hypothesis that O–P sites contribute strongly via hydrogen bonding; they are not direct measurements of fractional mechanism contributions.

### Review use
This is a high-value Stage 3 processing–cost–performance study because it joins an explicit recipe, characterization, kinetic/isotherm tests, DFT mechanism analysis, a secondary-effluent experiment and a preparation-cost estimate. It also illustrates why we must not equate observed Qe with fitted qmax, and why bulk wastewater metrics do not automatically demonstrate antibiotic-specific removal.

## 2. Na et al. (2024) — pristine macadamia-nutshell biochar for four antibiotics

### Material and preparation
- *Bioresource Technology* 394 (2024), article 130281; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biortech.2023.130281.
- The paper calls its macadamia nutshell biochar **MBC**; this is their abbreviation and must not be confused with “magnetic biochar” in other papers.
- Raw shells were washed with tap water, sun-dried 48 h, and pyrolysed at 600 °C for 2 h under N2 (0.3 L/min), heating rate 10 °C/min. Biochar was crushed to 125–500 µm, washed until pH 7, vacuum-filtered and dried at 105 °C for 6 h. No chemical activation or functionalization was performed.
- BET area ≈392 m²/g; total pore volume ≈0.18 cm³/g; total micropore volume ≈0.05 cm³/g; DFT mean pore diameter ≈1.17 nm. Micropore area was around one-third of BET area. FE-SEM showed heterogeneous oval/semicircular pores; EDS: carbon 85.4 ± 0.2% and oxygen 14.5 ± 0.2%; FTIR indicates prominent –OH/oxygen groups; Raman ID/IG = 2.68 indicates a defective/disordered carbon structure.
- Yield, preparation cost, measured energy balance, regeneration, leaching, actual wastewater testing and LCA are not reported in the supplied main PDF. The authors mention carbon-sequestration potential, but no full carbon-storage/lifecycle assessment is provided.

### Experiment conditions
- Targets: ciprofloxacin (called CFX in this paper), ofloxacin (OFX), sulfamethoxazole (SMX) and trimethoprim (TMP).
- Kinetics: initial concentration 4 mg/L for each antibiotic, 8 g/L biochar, pH 7, 20 ± 1 °C, 120 rpm; samples at 2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 120, 240, 480, 720 and 1,440 min. Antibiotic concentrations were normalized to mmol/L for comparing molecules of different molecular weights.
- Isotherms: initial concentrations 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 50 and 60 mg/L; dose 8 g/L; pH 7, 20 °C, dark, 120 rpm, approximately 600 min. The authors also show isotherms at pH 3 and pH 10; exact sample setup is stated in §2.3.2.
- This is a relatively high sorbent dose (8 g/L), so do not treat its results as evidence that comparable treatment can be delivered at typical lower doses.

### Bayesian kinetic estimates (Table 1, PDF p. 6)
- The authors fit pseudo-second-order kinetic curves and report posterior means with standard deviations; units for qe are mmol/kg and k2 in kg mmol−1 min−1.
- Mean kinetic qe (mmol/kg): CFX 1.230 ± 0.046; TMP 0.641 ± 0.034; OFX 0.482 ± 0.025; SMX 0.230 ± 0.010.
- Mean k2: CFX 0.350 ± 0.084; OFX 0.068 ± 0.016; SMX 0.099 ± 0.024; TMP 0.070 ± 0.017. Reported MCMC convergence R-hat = 1.001.
- The paper reports half-saturation-time order: CFX 2.32 min < TMP 22.28 min < OFX 30.51 min < SMX 43.91 min. This is the authors’ summary from fitted parameters, not the same as equilibrium time; the experimental series extended to 1,440 min.

### Bayesian Langmuir estimates (Table 2, PDF p. 7)
Mean Qmax values are reported in **mmol/kg**, not mg/g:
- pH 3: CFX 1.530 ± 0.051; OFX 1.302 ± 0.046; SMX 1.801 ± 0.050; TMP 2.640 ± 0.094.
- pH 7: CFX 7.381 ± 0.398; OFX 1.727 ± 0.102; SMX 0.873 ± 0.048; TMP 5.827 ± 0.339.
- pH 10: CFX 0.868 ± 0.047; OFX 0.832 ± 0.023; SMX 0.370 ± 0.025; TMP 1.570 ± 0.050.
- These are model-derived posterior means, not the observed kinetic qe values. Keep the native units and model type visible. Do not mix mmol/kg numbers with mg/g data from other studies.
- Sorption tendency in the study is CFX > TMP > OFX > SMX. CFX and TMP have their largest Qmax at pH 7; all four show strong pH-dependent changes. In the authors’ interpretation, antibiotic speciation and pH-dependent functional-group charge partly explain the patterns.

### Mechanistic interpretation and limits
- The paper’s model combines Bayesian nonlinear estimation of PSO kinetics and Langmuir isotherms with FTIR chemometric analysis (hierarchical clustering and PCA). It argues that oxygen-containing functional groups and antibiotic structure drive the different uptake behaviour.
- Proposed CFX interactions at pH 7 include hydrogen bonding, π–π electron-donor/acceptor interactions, electrostatic interactions and mesopore/external-surface filling. OFX and SMX show much weaker sorption in the tested conditions; TMP has pH/speciation-dependent sorption. These are proposed interpretations supported by statistical/chemometric patterns, not independently quantified contributions.
- The text notes that molecule dimensions approach the 1.17 nm MBC pore diameter; pore entrance/access and hydration can constrain diffusion. It proposes that more severe/higher-temperature pyrolysis might improve pore connectivity, but this study itself prepared MBC at 600 °C.
- No real wastewater, continuous-flow column, regeneration, cost or toxicity/leachables experiment was conducted. The authors warn that adsorbed antibiotics could be released if biochar were used as a soil amendment; they suggest repyrolysis/reuse as a possible future option, but it was not tested in this paper.
- Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. S1 contain antibiotic/property and characterization detail not present in the supplied main PDF; data availability says data can be made available on request. These are not user dependencies; leave missing values unresolved.

### Review use
A strong unmodified-biochar comparison showing selectivity among four antibiotics and the value of reporting posterior uncertainty. It also exemplifies why low single-solute feed, high sorbent dose, model units and pH must accompany the headline values. It is not real-wastewater or economic evidence.

## 3. Zhou et al. (2024) — microwave regeneration of sepiolite and montmorillonite

### Material and experimental conditions
- *Separation and Purification Technology* 348 (2024), article 127767; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seppur.2024.127767.
- Natural reagent-grade sepiolite (Sep) and montmorillonite (Mt) were washed with DI water, centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, oven-dried at 75 °C and ground.
- Kinetic test: 0.02 g adsorbent + 20 mL TC at 25 mg/L, pH 7, 100 rpm at 25 °C. Isotherms: 0.02 g adsorbent in 20 mL, TC C0 10–120 mg/L, pH 7, 24 h, 100 rpm, temperatures 298, 308, 318 and 328 K.
- For pH/dose/ionic strength tests: 25 mg/L TC, often 24 h at 298 K; the selected optimum adsorbent dose was 1.0 g/L. pH range 3–11. K+ and Na+ had relatively little effect; Ca2+ and Mg2+ inhibited adsorption more strongly.
- MW regeneration: adsorbents saturated with TC were dried at 75 °C to constant mass, cooled, ground/transferred to a ceramic crucible, and irradiated at nominal 750 W for 5–45 min. The selected times were 25 min for Sep and 20 min for Mt.
- Nominal device-power × time equals 0.3125 kWh at 750 W for 25 min (Sep) and 0.25 kWh at 750 W for 20 min (Mt). **These are theoretical nameplate energy products, not measured electrical consumption or a scale-up energy intensity**; treated adsorbent mass was very small (the lab kinetic design used 0.02 g in 20 mL), and no measured energy meter, yield-normalized kWh/kg, or plant-scale energy balance was reported.

### Adsorption amounts and model parameters
- At 298 K and 25 mg/L feed, experimentally measured uptake (Fig. 7/Table 2, PDF pp. 7–9) was Sep 11.252 mg/g and Mt 20.736 mg/g. Approximate equilibrium times were 240 min for Sep and 120 min for Mt.
- Raising temperature to 308 and 318 K increased observed uptake: Sep 11.721 and 11.958 mg/g; Mt 21.815 and 22.564 mg/g, respectively. Table 2 shows PSO fits with R² = 0.997–0.999, better than PFO fits (R² roughly 0.789–0.897). A PSO fit alone does not establish a unique chemical mechanism.
- Langmuir isotherm Qmax parameters (Table 4, PDF p. 11; units mg/g) were:
  - Sep: 33.592 ± 1.290 (298 K); 38.057 ± 1.202 (308 K); 48.363 ± 1.347 (318 K); 65.040 ± 3.011 (328 K).
  - Mt: 213.273 ± 42.057 (298 K); 237.854 ± 45.135 (308 K); 242.557 ± 50.620 (318 K); 245.411 ± 34.842 (328 K).
- The corresponding Langmuir fits reported R² > 0.97; Freundlich fits were lower/variable (R² roughly 0.936–0.976). These Qmax values are fitted isotherm parameters based on higher isotherm concentrations, not the measured 25 mg/L kinetic-test capacities. Do not place them into one “observed capacity” column without labels.
- Reported thermodynamics (Table 5, PDF p. 11): ΔH = 28.77 kJ/mol (Sep) and 18.19 kJ/mol (Mt); ΔS = 0.151 and 0.126 kJ? The table labels entropy in J mol−1 K−1 but prints 0.151 and 0.126; keep this unit/magnitude as printed and verify before calculating derived values. ΔG is negative at all four temperatures; both processes are described as spontaneous and endothermic. The text calls activation energies 21.020 kJ/mol for Sep and 7.015 kJ/mol for Mt and interprets them as chemisorption, whereas ΔH values are later described as below 40 kJ/mol and interpreted as physisorption; treat mechanism classification from these thresholds as the authors’ interpretation, not decisive proof.

### MW regeneration and reuse
- The authors define regeneration ratio as \(Q_i/Q_0 \times 100\%\), where Qi is uptake after MW regeneration and Q0 is original uptake. Therefore the ratio can exceed 100% if regeneration alters accessible pores/surface and the measured capacity rises; it is not simply percentage of TC molecules desorbed.
- With time optimized at 750 W, Fig. 15 (PDF p. 12): Sep reaches 81.83% regeneration ratio at 25 min; Mt reaches 98.83% at 20 min. Longer exposure decreases the ratio, which the authors associate with pore/surface damage or changes to functional groups.
- With cycles (Fig. 17, PDF p. 13), Sep’s regeneration ratio increases to 89.76% at cycle 4 and declines to 65.21% by cycle 8. Mt starts at 98.56% at cycle 1 and declines to 66.38% by cycle 8. Authors describe Sep’s ratio as more stable over eight cycles, while Mt performs better in individual regeneration optimization tests.
- The abstract/conclusion also report **maximum** regeneration ratios of 89.76% (Sep) and 114.45% (Mt), with Mt’s 114.45% reached in a separate dose-optimization condition / first-cycle result. Keep this “maximum under optimized conditions” claim separate from Fig. 15’s selected-time values and Fig. 17’s cycle series. It is not necessarily a contradiction, but it is a different test context.
- Surface changes (Table 1, PDF p. 6): Sep BET area 283.17 m²/g raw, 274.38 after TC adsorption, 224.00 after MW regeneration; Mt 45.52 raw, 41.83 after TC adsorption, 53.60 after MW regeneration. The Mt basal spacing expands from 1.460 nm to 1.515 nm after TC adsorption and returns to 1.465 nm after MW regeneration (XRD, PDF p. 3), supporting interlayer uptake and partial recovery.
- Authors propose hydrogen bonding, van der Waals, electrostatic interactions, and cation exchange/bridging. XRD, FTIR and BET changes support TC uptake/removal. Their MW explanation includes thermal desorption of volatiles, selective heating/hot spots and possible non-thermal effects; these proposed mechanisms are not established as full-scale energy or emissions advantages.
- There is no real wastewater test, continuous-flow study, measured electricity consumption, regeneration-product analysis, adsorbent loss balance, cost model, or LCA. No product-level analysis identifies whether TC is fully destroyed or transformed during MW exposure; do not equate regeneration with mineralization.

### Review use
Strong Stage 2 evidence for raw natural clay performance and the regeneration/reuse dimension. Its central value is not simply that microwave regeneration works, but that regeneration must be evaluated by material, irradiation time, dose, cycle count and energy boundary. Keep kinetic uptake, fitted isotherm Qmax, optimized regeneration ratio and cycle-by-cycle regeneration ratio in separate metrics.

## Cross-paper synthesis from Batch 04

1. **Feedstock-to-process continuum:** raw macadamia shell biochar provides a pristine baseline; H3PO4 activation greatly changes pore structure/surface chemistry; natural clays supply a low-processing baseline with a distinct regeneration option. These are different material/condition regimes, not a direct rank-order experiment.
2. **Capacity numbers are not directly comparable.** The PCH paper mixes measured Qe and fitted Langmuir Qm in its abstract wording; MBC uses mmol/kg and an 8 g/L dose; Sep/Mt Qmax is fitted over 10–120 mg/L and differs sharply from measured uptake at 25 mg/L.
3. **Mechanism evidence varies in strength.** PCH adds DFT/XPS support for O–P hydrogen-bond interaction; MBC uses Bayesian parameter uncertainty plus chemometric group analysis, producing proposed rather than uniquely proven mechanisms; the clay MW paper uses FTIR/XRD/BET changes to support uptake/interlayer changes and regeneration.
4. **Real-water tests need the correct claim.** PCH tested real secondary pharmaceutical effluent but reports EEM/COD/TOC rather than target-specific CIP quantification. Macadamia MBC and the Sep/Mt MW study test prepared antibiotic solutions, not real wastewater.
5. **Practical cost/regeneration trade-off.** PCH reports low estimated production cost but incomplete cost boundaries; MBC reports no cost or regeneration; Sep/Mt demonstrate reuse in a tiny laboratory setup but do not report measured energy intensity or full-scale economics.
6. Do not accept the abstracts’ qualitative labels (“low cost”, “excellent regeneration”, “large-scale potential”) without boundary-matched evidence. These three papers remain provisionally relevant pending Q1 verification and final screening.
