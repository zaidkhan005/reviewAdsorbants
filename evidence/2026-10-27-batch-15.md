## A. Li et al. (2019) — natural waste biosorbents for pharmaceuticals at low concentrations
*Journal of Cleaner Production* 227 (2019), 88–97. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2019.04.081. Existing candidate **CORP-016**. Primary experimental study.

### Materials and methods
Three biosorbents were compared: orchard-pruning biochar made by pyrolysis at 500 °C; brown macro-algae (*Fucus vesiculosus*); and oak wood chippings. Materials were ground/sieved to 0.25–0.8 mm, washed three times with methanol and Milli-Q water, and dried at 105 °C. BET surface areas were 8.89 m²/g (biochar), 1.77 m²/g (wood chips), and 0.29 m²/g (macro-algae); pHpzc values were 9.1, 5.7 and 5.5, respectively. Tests were batch and triplicate.

### TMP (the antibiotic endpoint)
For kinetic tests at C0 = 500 µg/L, pH 7, 20 °C, 2 g/L biosorbent and up to 24 h, Table 1 reports observed equilibrium uptakes:
- Biochar: 220 µg/g = 0.220 mg/g
- Macro-algae: 238 µg/g = 0.238 mg/g
- Wood chippings: 158 µg/g = 0.158 mg/g

The pseudo-second-order fits report R² = 0.999 for all three TMP series, with calculated qe close to the experimental values. The article attributes TMP uptake particularly to macro-algae under these low-concentration kinetic conditions.

The rendered Table 3 confirms that fitted Langmuir Qm units are **µg/g** (not mg/g, a likely OCR/text-extraction error in the micro sign). TMP Qm values convert as follows:
- Biochar: 2.08 × 10³ µg/g = **2.08 mg/g** (Langmuir R² = 0.987)
- Macro-algae: 7.14 × 10⁴ µg/g = **71.4 mg/g** (R² = 0.994)
- Wood chippings: 8.33 × 10³ µg/g = **8.33 mg/g** (R² = 0.932; Freundlich fit is better, R² = 0.996)

These isotherms use concentration ranges substantially above the kinetic experiment and different plotted ranges by material; the macro-algae TMP isotherm extends to 400,000 µg/L. Thus, fitted Qm must not be compared directly with low-concentration observed uptake as if both use the same conditions.

The paper also studies diclofenac (DCF), but DCF is an NSAID, not an antibiotic. Its results are excluded from antibiotic-specific counts. Table 2/Fig. 3 indicate mass transfer to biochar was mainly limited by intraparticle diffusion, whereas the final adsorptive-attachment step was important for macro-algae and wood chips. These are the authors' fitted/interpretive conclusions, not proof of a unique molecular mechanism.

## B. Adu-Poku et al. (2024) — acid-activated coconut-husk biochar for amoxicillin
*Journal of Chemistry* (2024), Article 3648098. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/3648098. Existing candidate **DISC-026**. Primary experimental study.

### Preparation and operating conditions
Coconut husk was washed, dried at 105 °C and pyrolyzed at 400 °C for 1 h in a low-cost homemade reactor. Activation used 50 g biochar in 500 mL 0.2 M HNO3 for 2 h at 25 °C. The material was neutralized back toward its stated original pH of 10 with 2–5 mL 0.5 M NaOH, filtered and dried at 105 °C for 12 h. Reported pHpzc = 6.25. No BET area/total pore-volume/pore-diameter analysis was performed; the authors suggest these should be measured in future work.

The main Methods describe a typical batch test of 25 mg biochar in 50 mL of 50 mg/L amoxicillin at pH 7 and 25 °C for 6 h. Separate tests vary pH 1–11, C0 = 20–70 mg/L, dose 2–20 mg and time 0–24 h. Importantly, the abstract instead states initial AMX concentrations of **0.5–2.5 mg/L**; this mismatch remains unresolved.

### Reported removal and dose/concentration trends
For the reported contact-time series, removal reached around 90% by 10 min and about 99% by 40 min, remained around 98% through 8 h, then declined to about 80% by 24 h. Removal peaked around pH 3 at 98.9%, remained relatively stable from pH 5 to 7, and decreased in the alkaline range. In Fig. 10, plotted uptake rises from roughly 37 mg/g at C0 = 20 mg/L to approximately 136 mg/g at 70 mg/L; removal is about 93.5% at 20 mg/L and about 98% at 50–70 mg/L. Fig. 11 shows capacity decreasing as adsorbent mass increases, while removal remains around 97–98%.

### High-priority mass-balance inconsistency
Table 1 reports experimental kinetic qe = **245.9 ± 1.4 mg/g** and PSO-calculated qe = 243.902 mg/g (R² = 0.999); Table 2 reports Langmuir qmax = **239.230 mg/g** (R² = 0.995). Yet if the stated typical condition of 25 mg biochar plus 50 mL at 50 mg/L applies, the batch initially contains 2.5 mg AMX and uptake cannot exceed **100 mg/g** by mass balance. The 245.9 mg/g value is therefore not reconciled by the stated test setup. The fitted qmax also sits slightly below the reported experimental qe. We should not use this capacity as a verified benchmark until the exact kinetic conditions/raw data are clarified. The plotted Fig. 10 values at 20–70 mg/L are much closer to the mass-balance envelope for the stated 25 mg/50 mL condition.

### Isotherm, regeneration and limitations
Authors report Langmuir as best fitting, with qmax 239.23 mg/g, and PSO as best-fitting kinetics; model fit alone does not prove a unique chemical mechanism. Four adsorption/desorption cycles used 0.05 M NaOH regeneration and drying at 110 °C. The paper describes the fourth-cycle loss as nearly 20% but also reports removal of 72.2% from 98.6% initially, which does not align precisely with that description. The conclusion limits the work to AMX and artificially polluted water. No cost, product yield, leaching, washwater-treatment or life-cycle data are reported.

## C. Li et al. (2025) — graphene oxide-based hydrogel for fluoroquinolones and Cu²⁺
*Process Safety and Environmental Protection* 202 (2025), 107659. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psep.2025.107659. Existing candidate **CORP-015**. Primary experimental study.

### Preparation and adsorption
PGOT@GEL combines phosphorus-doped graphene oxide, triethylenetetramine (TETA) grafting, modified β-cyclodextrin and AMPS-based hydrogel polymerization. GO oxidation, phosphorus doping, amidation/grafting, organic-solvent use and polymerization make this a highly engineered material; the article does not provide a quantitative process inventory or LCA.

The authors report fitted Langmuir qmax = **821.24 mg/g for ciprofloxacin (CIP)** and **755.96 mg/g for gatifloxacin (GAT)** (R² = 0.9908 and 0.9923). Under the pH tests, reported CIP uptake was 192.24 mg/g at pH 7 with 96.12% removal; GAT uptake was 171.84 mg/g at pH 6 with 85.92% removal. Response-surface optimization used CIP C0 = 296.52 mg/L, pH 6.70, dose 5.08 mg and 298.88 K; predicted uptake was 800.60 mg/g and the reported validation uptake was 807.54 mg/g. These are high-concentration optimized tests, not evidence of the same capacity at trace environmental levels.

The reported kinetics approach equilibrium after about 120 min. Ionic interference was modest for the reported tests: retained CIP uptake was 96.63% with Na2SO4, 96.75% with NaCl and 92.27% with CaCl2. Higher temperature reduced uptake and the authors describe adsorption as exothermic.

### Co-adsorption, real-water testing and reuse
In binary CIP–Cu²⁺ experiments, the authors report capacity-ratio (Rq) values consistent with mutual promotion over some concentration ranges: Rq about 1.0061–1.1417 for CIP and 1.0070–1.2044 for Cu²⁺. They propose CIP–Cu²⁺ complex formation, coordination and bridging, while noting that site limitation weakens the effect at higher concentrations.

The paper reports more than 89% CIP removal across four water conditions (deionized, tap, farmland and lake water) and about 90.53% of initial removal retained after seven cycles. Regeneration soaked the adsorbent in methanol for 12 h after each adsorption, followed by washing and drying. The main text does not provide all matrix/challenge concentrations, which are in the supplementary information not supplied here. Do not treat this as validated trace-level untreated-wastewater performance until those conditions are checked.

A rice seedling assay showed reduced adverse effect in the CIP-plus-hydrogel group relative to CIP alone; this is a separate biological response endpoint and does not establish that spent hydrogel or every transformation product is harmless.
