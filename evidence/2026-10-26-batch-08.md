## 1. Kar et al. (2025) — wood-apple-shell activated carbon for enrofloxacin

### Preparation and material properties
- Journal of Hazardous Materials Advances 19 (2025), article 100666; DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hazadv.2025.100666.
- Wood-apple shell was collected locally, cleaned/sun-dried for five days, oven-dried at 100 °C for 1 h, and carbonized at 250 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace. The char was washed and oven-dried at 100 °C for 12 h to produce the authors' N-biochar (CNB).
- For acid activation, 33.372 g of carbonized shell material was mixed with 50 mL orthophosphoric acid, stirred 24 h at 35 °C, filtered, washed to pH 7, and dried at 100 °C for 3 h to yield ACNB. The article describes H3PO4 activation as safer/less energy intensive than some alternatives, but the main text does not supply a closed production mass balance or product yield.
- The described activation recipe uses H3PO4; it does not specify adding an external nitrogen dopant. The material label “activated N-biochar” should therefore be kept as the authors' terminology, not silently re-described as deliberately N-doped. Table 3 reports elemental N = 0.12 wt% in ACNB.
- Table 3 (PDF p. 7): BET surface area: raw wood-apple shell 69.03 m²/g, CNB 274.735 m²/g, ACNB 547.36 m²/g, commercial activated carbon (CAC) 643.97 m²/g. ACNB micropore volume is printed as 1.76 cm³/g; retain as source-reported, but it appears unusually high relative to the other entries and should be checked against the original table/SI before comparative use. Iodine value ACNB 485 mg/g versus CAC 500 mg/g.
- Table 2 (PDF p. 8): ACNB fixed carbon 73.4%, volatile matter 22.35%, moisture 7.9%, ash 4.58%; commercial AC fixed carbon 85.8%, volatile matter 2.3%, moisture 10.3%, ash 1.6%. ACNB is not unambiguously superior to commercial carbon across every characterization metric.

### Adsorption conditions and performance
- Batch experiments examined pH, dose, concentration, temperature and contact time. The methods section states C0 2–50 mg/L and dose 0.45–1.5 g/L; the abstract gives a different dose range of 0.2–1 g/L, while the results/conclusion optimum is 2 g/L. This is a material internal inconsistency; the reported optimum condition is retained as the paper's result but the dose range discrepancy stays flagged.
- The repeatedly reported high-removal condition is pH 6, initial ENF 10 mg/L, ACNB 2 g/L, 24 h, 25 °C. At this condition the paper reports 96.5% removal (PDF pp. 1, 9, 13).
- pH tests report maximum removal around pH 6. The authors relate the outcome to ACNB pHPZC ≈4 and enrofloxacin pKa values 6.09 and 7.91, with zwitterionic ENF interacting with negatively charged surface above pHPZC. Report this as the authors' interpretation.
- Dose tests in Results §6.2.2 say removal rises as dosage moves from 0.2 to 1.1 g/L and reaches a maximum at 2 g/L, although their methods/abstract list different test ranges. Use of 2 g/L in the final optimum requires clarification against the dose study setup.
- Increasing contact time from 2 to 24 h increased removal, with the reported maximum 96.5% at 24 h.
- Equilibrium tests were run over different initial concentrations with pH 6, 24 h and 2 g/L dose. The Langmuir model was the best fit (R²≈0.966); the authors report Qmax = 54.94 mg/g and RL = 0.118 (favourable fit classification). These are fitted-model results, not direct uptake at 10 mg/L.
- Kinetic data gave reported pseudo-second-order R²≈0.999, and Elovich R²≈0.991. State as model-fit observations; PSO fit alone does not prove chemisorption.
- Temperature tests spanned 15–40 °C. The paper reports highest removal around 25 °C (~95% in the temperature panel), decreasing at higher temperatures, and interprets uptake as exothermic.

### Mechanisms and serious thermodynamic inconsistency
- Characterization includes SEM/EDX, FTIR, XRD, BET and point-of-zero-charge analysis. FTIR shifts/new bands after uptake and EDX signals are interpreted by authors as evidence of ENF association with ACNB. Possible interactions discussed include electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding and interactions with surface groups/pores.
- Table 4 (PDF p. 11) prints ΔG° values of −3825, −3879, −3916, −3970, −4032 and −4089 kJ/mol from 288 to 313 K, with ΔH° = −0.735 kJ/mol and ΔS° = −0.107 kJ/(mol K). These numbers are not thermodynamically consistent with the article's own ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° relation: at 288 K the given ΔH and ΔS imply about +30.1 kJ/mol, not −3825 kJ/mol. This is a severe calculation/unit inconsistency, not a value to repeat as valid evidence of spontaneity. Do not use the paper's reported ΔG values for cross-paper comparison until the calculation is resolved.
- The paper gives activation energy Ea = 61.1 kJ/mol and treats it as supportive of chemisorptive interaction; this should also be presented as an author-reported fitted interpretation, not independent mechanism proof.

### DNA fluorescence, reuse and safe disposal
- The DNA intercalation assay mixed ethidium bromide and calf-thymus DNA with 10 ppm treated/untreated ENF solutions, incubated at 34 °C for 30 min, and measured fluorescence. It is a narrow DNA-binding/fluorescence assay; it does not directly establish that all toxicities, transformation products, or ecological risks are eliminated in real wastewater.
- Reuse: the spent ACNB was desorbed using 40% (w/v) ethanol, then dehydrated for reuse. The paper reports 81.47% removal efficiency after four cycles (PDF pp. 11–12).
- The cost breakdown in Table 5 (PDF p. 12) reports production cost INR 239.22/kg, including zero-cost local shell, 8.57 INR carbonization electricity, 102.84 INR drying electricity, 97.50 INR phosphoric acid, 8.57 INR drying, and 10% overhead. The paper compares this with approximately INR 1,000/kg for local commercial activated charcoal and claims 16.7-times lower treatment cost across four cycles. Because the batch yield/mass basis is not transparent, the acid cost/quantity scale requires reconciliation, and adsorption operating costs/regeneration and effluent handling are not fully harmonized, treat this as a laboratory costing estimate—not validated industrial treatment cost.
- Spent sorbent was immobilized with lime, water and cement and landfilled; the authors themselves note that long-term leachate implications warrant further study. Do not describe spent media as inherently safe.
- No actual wastewater test, fixed-bed validation, full LCA, or scale-up is reported.

### Review use
This paper is a candidate for the processing-cost-performance section: local agri-waste, H3PO4 activation, ENF adsorption, ethanol reuse and a stated unit production cost. It is also a case study in why calculations and economic boundaries must be independently checked. Main quantitative inclusions should retain the thermodynamic, dosage, yield-basis, and DNA-assay caveats.

## 2. Sompornpailin et al. (2024) — GAC, antibiotics, bacterial toxicity and ARG/ARB in hospital effluents

### Materials and operating conditions
- Science of the Total Environment 927 (2024), article 172095; DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2024.172095.
- Six commercial granular activated carbons were tested: four coconut-shell GACs (Co-U, Co-A, Co-E, Co-P) and two coal-based GACs (Coal-K, Coal-C). GAC particles were sieved to 1–2 mm, washed with DI water and dried at 150 °C for 12 h.
- Table 1 (PDF p. 5): BET area Co-U 847, Co-A 592, Co-E 574, Co-P 594, Coal-K 893 and Coal-C 1106 m²/g. Mean pore size 2.9, 2.9, 2.8, 2.7, 2.7 and 1.9 nm, respectively; pHPZC 6.5, 6.3, 6.3, 6.1, 6.7 and 7.4. Co-U had the highest water-contact angle (78°, most hydrophobic) and lowest quantified total acidic functional-group density (0.2 mmol/g), especially carboxyl groups.
- The six antibiotic targets were penicillin G (PCG), ampicillin (AMP), ceftazidime (CAZ), meropenem (MEM), sulfamethazine (paper label SMZ) and ciprofloxacin (CIP). Preserve the paper's label SMZ; do not silently change it to sulfamethoxazole (SMX).
- The actual hospital effluent came from a general hospital in Bangkok, Thailand, after activated-sludge treatment and sedimentation. S-HWW means the non-chlorinated secondary hospital effluent; C-HWW means the effluent after chlorination. The study characterized dissolved organic matter/electrolytes/chlorine residue and identified antibiotic intermediates with LC-MS/MS. The chlorination condition reported is free chlorine residual 0.5 mg Cl2/L.
- Initial adsorption screen: 100 μg/L single-antibiotic concentration, GAC dose 1 g/L, 10 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7, 25 °C. Adsorption kinetics and isotherms used chlorinated hospital effluent (C-HWW) as the real background matrix; concentration range for isotherms was 50–200 μg/L. Therefore this study deliberately addresses low μg/L antibiotic concentrations rather than the typical mg/L laboratory feed used in many high-capacity papers.

### Adsorption findings and competitive mixtures
- Co-U performed best overall among the six tested GACs. PCG and AMP had the lowest adsorption; CAZ, MEM, SMZ and CIP were generally adsorbed more strongly. Authors relate the superior Co-U performance to its higher hydrophobicity and lower oxygen-containing acidic group abundance.
- The paper used linear isotherms because the equilibrium range was low (0–150 μg/L). Table 2 (PDF p. 8) reports Kp in L/g for single-solute C-HWW: PCG 0.3778, AMP 0.8379, CAZ 0.3916, MEM 1.5674, SMZ 0.7876 and CIP 0.4157.
- In the six-antibiotic mixed-solute solution, Kp values (L/g) were PCG 0.2996, AMP 0.5292, CAZ 0.3770 and CIP 0.1738; MEM and SMZ were not adsorbed/did not yield a fitted Kp in the mixture. AMP and CIP adsorption capacities decreased approximately 37% and 58% at Ce = 50 μg/L; CAZ adsorption was slightly enhanced and PCG changed little. MEM and SMZ were outcompeted despite being strongly adsorbed in single-solute tests.
- Kinetic runs for PCG, AMP, CAZ and CIP at 100 μg/L (GAC 1 g/L, pH 7, 25 °C, C-HWW background) were evaluated to 12 h. CAZ and CIP increased sharply during the first 45 min and reached a plateau around 300 min; PCG reached equilibrium around 200 min and AMP around 400 min. MEM and SMZ were omitted from kinetics because they were not adsorbed from the mixed solution.
- LDF and homogeneous intraparticle-diffusion (HID) models fitted kinetic data with RRMSE around 1.35–4.37 μg/g. Both external mass transfer and intraparticle diffusion were important by the authors' Biot-number analysis. A PSO fit is not the principal kinetic result here; the paper emphasizes transport models.

### Bacterial toxicity and resistance genes
- Bacterial toxicity used E. coli ATCC 25922 growth. DI water supported log 9.4 CFU/mL; untreated S-HWW and C-HWW lowered growth to log 7.2 and 6.7 CFU/mL, respectively.
- After treatment by the six GACs, S-HWW supported only log 6.7–7.8 CFU/mL: the study found little improvement in this growth-based toxicity assay. After GAC treatment, C-HWW supported about log 9.2–9.5 CFU/mL, close to the DI-water control. Authors associate this with chlorination reducing the number/type of intermediates and making remaining intermediates more adsorbable.
- Fixed-bed study: Co-U in a glass column 2.5 cm internal diameter, 15 cm high, 15 cm bed depth, 20 g dried GAC, flow 5 mL/min, empty-bed contact time 25 min. After 45 days (~4402.5 bed volumes), column media and effluent were analysed.
- HT-qPCR evaluated 43 target genes, with 32 genes detected across samples. Passage through the Co-U column substantially reduced relative abundance of many antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs) in effluent, but some taxa/genes increased in spent GAC or related samples. The authors report possible enrichment of Firmicutes and genes associated with efflux/chlorine resistance in media in some conditions and conclude used GAC still requires appropriate disinfection before disposal as infectious waste.
- The article does not establish that GAC alone permanently destroys all antibiotic molecules or all ARGs; adsorption transfers pollutants to the solid phase. Do not conflate gene reduction in the water with complete destruction or disposal safety. Likewise, increased bacterial growth in the E. coli assay is a specific bioassay response, not a full ecotoxicological certification.

### Review use
This is a high-value primary source for the practical section because it combines real secondary hospital effluent, low-concentration antibiotic adsorption, six-GAC comparison, single/mixed competition, a 45-day fixed-bed run, toxicity testing and ARG/ARB analysis. It also supports a crucial end-of-life finding: spent adsorbent can accumulate microbial/ARG risk and requires management. Do not reduce its contribution to an adsorption-capacity ranking.

## 3. Al-sareji et al. (2024) — phosphoric-acid-activated banana peel carbon

### Material preparation and characterization
- Materials 17 (2024), article 1032; DOI https://doi.org/10.3390/ma17051032.
- Targets: amoxicillin (antibiotic) and carbamazepine (anticonvulsant/non-antibiotic pharmaceutical). The paper studies the mixture, so results must retain both analytes but only amoxicillin should count in antibiotic-only synthesis.
- Banana peels were washed, cut to 2 cm, dried at 100 °C for 24 h, ground/sieved below 0.150 mm. 25 g dried BP was soaked in 500 mL of 25% v/v phosphoric-acid solution at 80 °C for 4 h, oven-dried at 110 °C for 6 h, carbonized at 350, 450 or 550 °C for 30 min, washed with hot distilled water to pH 6–7, and dried at 105 °C. The selected BPAC used carbonization at 450 °C.
- BPAC BET area was 911.59 m²/g, pHPZC 5.005, total acidic groups 0.711 mmol/g and total basic groups 0.143 mmol/g. Raw BP BET area reported 0.650 m²/g. Fixed carbon increased from 0.67% to 74.07%; volatile matter decreased from 82.53% to 16.90%; moisture decreased from 11.37% to 4.08%; elemental carbon rose from 41.83% to 74.03% (PDF pp. 10–12).
- No numerical production cost, adsorbent yield or full LCA is reported despite the paper's use of “sustainable” and “cost-effective” descriptions.

### Batch conditions and removal
- Main test conditions: 150 mL pollutant mixture, 25 mg/L initial concentration (paper describes the mixture concentration; keep its stated basis rather than assuming each analyte has 25 mg/L unless verified), 0.18 g BPAC (1.2 g/L), pH 5, 25 ± 2 °C, 150 rpm, contact time 120 min. Runs were triplicate; coefficient of variation ≤2.64%.
- Dosage tests: 0.3–1.5 g/L. For amoxicillin, increasing dose from 0.9 to 1.5 g/L raised removal from 84.07% to 96.02%; carbamazepine rose from 87.04% to 90.62%. Authors selected 1.2 g/L because 1.5 g/L gave only marginal further improvement.
- Contact-time results reached a plateau at 120 min. Both pH and temperature were evaluated; pHPZC ~5, and the paper reports pH 5 as a practical optimum for the two-target mixture. Results in the discussion show amoxicillin removal falls substantially at alkaline pH, reaching 50.65% at pH 10; carbamazepine performs slightly better around pH 5.
- The paper reports a decrease in amoxicillin removal from 99.43% at 10 mg/L feed to 91.54% at 25 mg/L; greater concentration occupies more sites and lowers fractional removal. These are specific parameter-test results and should not be confused with equilibrium Qmax.

### Equilibrium, kinetics, mechanism and desorption
- Equilibrium isotherms used initial concentrations 5–120 mg/L, 0.18 g BPAC in 150 mL, pH 5, 12 h at 25 ± 2 °C. Langmuir had lower SSE than Freundlich. Fitted Qmax: amoxicillin 393.70 mg/g; carbamazepine 338.98 mg/g. These are model-fitted values, not directly observed capacity at the 25 mg/L optimum feed.
- Pseudo-second-order R² = 0.992 for amoxicillin and 0.984 for carbamazepine, versus pseudo-first-order 0.909 and 0.977. The authors infer chemisorption from the PSO fit and D–R apparent energy; retain this as their interpretation only. Kinetic/isotherm fit alone does not establish a unique mechanism.
- Authors propose electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic and π–π interactions, pore filling/intraparticle diffusion, and functional-group interactions. FTIR shows changes after uptake; XRD shows no tangible change after adsorption.
- Desorption tests used pH-7 water and ethanol for 6 h at 150 rpm; water desorbed poorly. Ethanol desorption rates reported were 76.83% for amoxicillin and 72.41% for carbamazepine. Desorption is not the same as full recovery of pristine adsorbent or destruction of the desorbed contaminants.

### Real waters and seven cycles
- BPAC was tested in Milli-Q water, Lake Balaton water and wastewater, at the optimized operating conditions except pH was not adjusted for real samples.
- Table 2 / Fig. 8 (PDF pp. 20–21): initial-cycle removal summarized as MQ water 91.63% amoxicillin and 90.29% carbamazepine; lake water 86.63% and 83.29%; wastewater 82.34% and 81.51%. Keep the individual analyte order as displayed in the article.
- Seven cycles were run. In cycle 4, mean removal of both pollutants was >86.06% in MQ water, 74.57% in lake water and 63.89% in wastewater. By cycle 7, removal values were:
  - MQ water: amoxicillin 41.43%, carbamazepine 37.84%.
  - Lake water: amoxicillin 25.43%, carbamazepine 15.84%.
  - Wastewater: amoxicillin 20.43%, carbamazepine 12.09%.
- This shows a strong water-matrix and reuse penalty. The wastewater and lake water matrices are incompletely characterized in the main PDF (water characterization points to an earlier paper); cite matrix source and study conditions before quantitative cross-study comparison.
- Spent BPAC handling is discussed generically (landfill/incineration/regeneration/reuse and AOP treatment of concentrated regeneration liquids), but no specific full-life-cycle assessment or validated end-of-life route is supplied for this material.

### Review use
This is useful for chemical activation, mixed-contaminant competition, water-matrix comparison and regeneration. Because the mixture includes carbamazepine, it must not be treated as a two-antibiotic study; in antibiotic-only tables extract amoxicillin values separately and label carbamazepine as a competing non-antibiotic pharmaceutical.

## Cross-paper synthesis — Batch 08

1. **Real matrices change the practical story:** Sompornpailin tests genuine secondary HWW at low μg/L concentrations and demonstrates antibiotic competition, toxicity and resistome responses; Al-sareji sees large matrix/reuse losses in lake water and wastewater. These studies add real-water context absent from many high-mg/L synthetic batches.
2. **Adsorption is transfer, not destruction:** the GAC study demonstrates that lower ARG/ARB in effluent can coexist with microbial/ARG accumulation on spent media; ENF ACNB and BPAC regeneration transfer the target to ethanol or another stream unless that stream is treated.
3. **Mechanism claims need discipline:** PSO model fit or an apparent D–R energy does not independently prove chemisorption. ENF ACNB’s reported thermodynamic parameters are mathematically inconsistent with its own equation.
4. **Cost boundaries matter:** ACNB’s INR 239.22/kg is a laboratory material-preparation estimate with unclear yield/mass basis and no full LCA; BPAC has no numerical cost/LCA; the GAC hospital study does not present a unit cost.
5. **Open verification flags:** ACNB dose-range conflict, N-biochar terminology, micropore-volume unit/value, thermodynamic inconsistency, costing basis and scope of DNA assay; hospital GAC full-text SI tables and exact identities of intermediates; BPAC mixed-solution concentration basis and full water matrix characterization.
