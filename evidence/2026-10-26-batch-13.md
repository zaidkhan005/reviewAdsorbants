## A. Li et al. (2024) — magnetic biochar for antibiotic removal
*Journal of Environmental Management* 366 (2024), 121872. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2024.121872. Existing candidate **CORP-014**. Review — supporting/background source only.

### Scope and processing continuum
The review describes impregnation-pyrolysis, hydrothermal, co-precipitation and ball-milling methods, plus element doping, acid/alkali modification, nanoparticle loading and organic-compound modification. Feedstock and preparation temperature influence textural and magnetic properties. These are preparation routes with differing chemical, energy, washing and environmental burdens; the article does not provide a common, normalized process-cost dataset.

### Reported performance and practical points (secondary)
The abstract reports magnetic-biochar antibiotic adsorption capacities spanning **5–900 mg/g** and says removal commonly exceeds **90% within 60 min**. Examples described in the review include Fe/Zn-modified sludge biochar with reported maximum capacities of 145 mg/g for tetracycline and 74 mg/g for ciprofloxacin, and microwave-hydrothermal magnetic water-hyacinth biochar with a reported maximum TC capacity of 202 mg/g and BET area of 2097 m²/g. These figures originate from different cited studies and must not be treated as directly comparable results.

The review gives a cited regeneration example where TC removal after five cycles was 19% with water washing, compared with 64% following ethanol extraction and 73% following NaOH desorption. It flags high preparation costs, possible secondary pollution and ambiguous mechanisms.

### Use in manuscript
Use for Section 2.3 on modified biochars; Sections 4–5 on adsorption/degradation separation, mechanisms and regeneration; and Section 7 on preparation costs/environmental risks. Table 2 compiles adsorption studies, while Table 3 covers catalytic-degradation systems: keep these evidence streams separate. Check numeric capacities and reuse examples against the cited primary papers.

## B. Jia et al. (2024) — biochar-based strategies for antibiotic removal
*ACS ES&T Engineering* 4 (2024), 1256–1274. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acsestengg.3c00605. Existing candidate **CORP-011**. Review — supporting/background source only.

### Key synthesis
The review reports maximum biochar adsorption capacities across cited studies in a range of **0.3–612.2 mg/g**, with reported antibiotic concentrations spanning **0.003–200 mg/L**. This is a heterogeneous literature range, not a single standardized comparison.

Mechanism synthesis highlights π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions and hydrogen bonding in cited sulfonamide, tetracycline and quinolone cases, and electrostatic interactions in cited macrolide and β-lactam cases. The review also emphasizes pH-dependent speciation, solubility, hydrophobicity and biochar surface properties.

The article discusses biochar as a microbial shelter/carrier and electron mediator, including studies where biochar promotes biodegradation. Engineering options include fixed/packed-bed columns, granular biochar, support media, sachets/bags and biochar-immobilized-cell bioreactors. The review notes practical trade-offs such as no destruction in adsorption-only beds, clogging, unstable cell immobilization, and high capital or operational costs in some configurations.

### Use in manuscript
Use primarily for Sections 3–4 (solution chemistry, mechanism hypotheses), Section 5 (regeneration and operational boundaries), and Section 6 (continuous/engineering applications and biotic roles). Keep adsorption, biodegradation, ARG-related outcomes and engineering process performance as distinct endpoints. Quantitative values should be traced to original primary studies before use in comparative tables.

## C. Conde-Cid et al. (2021) — pine bark, oak ash and mussel shell for sulfonamide adsorption/desorption
*Environmental Research* 195 (2021), 110814. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2021.110814. Existing candidate **CORP-005**. Primary experimental study.

### Methods and conditions
The paper tests sulfadiazine (SDZ), sulfamethazine (SMT) and sulfachloropyridazine (SCP) individually by batch adsorption/desorption. Methods list C0 values of **2.5, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 µmol/L**, a 24 h contact time, 50 rpm agitation, 25 ± 2 °C, dark conditions, CaCl₂ background electrolyte, triplicates and HPLC-UV analysis. The measured contact-solution pH was not adjusted or buffered; blank antibiotic losses were reported below 3%.

Pine bark (<0.63 mm) and oak ash each used 0.5 g in 10 mL (nominal adsorbent dose **50 g/L**). Uncalcined mussel shell used 2 g in 5 mL (**400 g/L**). The dose difference is important for interpretation. The uploaded PDF did not include the supplementary-information file referenced for additional material/antibiotic characteristics.

### Adsorption results
At C0 = 50 µmol/L, pine-bark observed amounts adsorbed were:
- SDZ: **780 µmol/kg**
- SMT: **890 µmol/kg**
- SCP: **870 µmol/kg**

Pine bark adsorbed >70% of each antibiotic across the tested concentration range; SDZ reached up to 90% at the lowest concentration and SCP up to 95% at the two highest concentrations.

At C0 = 50 µmol/L, uptake for each antibiotic was below **150 µmol/kg** on oak ash and below **20 µmol/kg** on mussel shell. The Results section attributes the higher retention by pine bark to its high organic-matter content (48.7%) and acidic pHw (3.99). Oak ash (13.23% organic matter; pHw 11.31) and mussel shell (11.43%; pHw 9.39) were more alkaline. The proposed interaction/speciation explanation is interpretive and is not a direct proof of one unique molecular mechanism in this experiment.

### Desorption and isotherm fitting
At C0 = 50 µmol/L, Table 2 reports the following desorbed amount and percentage of previously adsorbed antibiotic:
- Mussel shell: SDZ 5.2 µmol/kg (67%); SMT 5.1 (63%); SCP 9.8 (79%).
- Oak ash: SDZ 18.8 µmol/kg (15%); SMT 21.9 (15%); SCP 15.0 (11%).
- Pine bark: SDZ 40.2 µmol/kg (5%); SMT 33.6 (4%); SCP 32.6 (4%).

Across the tested concentrations, desorption ranged 19–81% for mussel shell, 0–49% for oak ash and was <11% for pine bark. This is a desorption test, not multi-cycle regeneration.

Linear and Freundlich models fitted the data with reported R² values of 0.98–1.00. Kd spanned 0.17–253.67 L/kg; KF spanned 0.14–202.76 in the paper's n-dependent Freundlich units; n ranged 0.81–1.28. The highest reported Kd/KF values were for pine bark with SCP (Kd = 253.67 ± 11.82 L/kg, R² = 0.989; KF = 202.76 ± 20.42, n = 1.28 ± 0.09, R² = 0.984). **No Langmuir qmax was fitted.**

### Source discrepancies and limits
1. Abstract states the starting concentration range is 1–50 µmol/L, but Methods lists seven concentrations from 2.5 to 50 µmol/L.
2. Oak-ash removal is described as <45% in the abstract versus <40% in Results text.
3. Mussel-shell removal is described as <15% in the abstract/one Results passage and <10% in another passage; Fig. 2 shows single-digit percentages.
4. Adsorbent doses differ markedly (50 versus 400 g/L), and pH was not adjusted/buffered.
5. Only Linear/Freundlich fitting is reported, not Langmuir qmax. No actual wastewater, continuous-flow/column trial, multi-cycle regeneration, numerical process cost or LCA is reported in the supplied main PDF.

### Use in manuscript
This is a potentially relevant primary comparison for the low-processing/by-product end of the continuum and for Section 3 (pH/speciation) and Section 5 (desorption/retention). Preserve µmol/kg as the reported uptake unit; do not relabel Kd/KF as qmax. Describe pine bark as showing stronger retention under these specific batch conditions, not as proven operationally superior across real wastewater applications.
