## 1. Hacıosmanoğlu et al. (2022): natural and modified clay minerals — secondary evidence

### Identity and review method
- Journal of Environmental Management 317 (2022), article 115397; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2022.115397.
- Review target: antibiotic adsorption by clay-based materials from water/wastewater, including mechanisms, modification strategies, desorption/reuse, economic analysis and life-cycle assessment.
- The stated search used Scopus and ScienceDirect; base terms were “antibiotics” and “adsorption”, combined with “clays”, “water”, “adsorbents”, “wastewater”, “modified-clays” and “bionanocomposites”. Excluded papers were published before 2000 or written in languages other than English.
- The main text reports 6,208 results for the two base terms, 250 after adding “clays”, 90 potentially eligible papers screened, and 82 finally selected. It reports that 45% concerned natural clays and 55% modified or combination-based clays.
- These are the authors’ review-method counts, not our own independent search results and not evidence that the review covers papers published after its search period. Its reported literature window extends to 2021. It should guide citation discovery, not be called the latest comprehensive map of current research.
- Supplementary Tables S1–S6 are referenced in the main text (screening details and material-by-material tables); they were not included in the uploaded main PDF. The review’s quantitative summary below is therefore secondary evidence only, and primary papers must be obtained and checked before we rely on individual values.

### Material taxonomy from the review
Natural clay/mineral classes discussed include montmorillonite (MMT), kaolinite (KAOL), bentonite (BENT), sepiolite (SEP), illite (I), halloysite (HALL), rectorite (RECT), smectite (SM), stevensite (STV) and vermiculite (VERM).
Modification and composite approaches discussed include:
- organocation/surfactant intercalation;
- biopolymer/clay composites (e.g. chitosan or carboxymethyl-chitosan with MMT);
- synthetic polymer and molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) composites;
- magnetic polymer–clay composites;
- acid activation and thermal/calcination treatment;
- pillared interlayered clays using metal oligocations;
- clay–biochar composites and hybrid silicates;
- layered double hydroxides and multi-component composites.

The review emphasizes that improvement depends on the mineral, antibiotic and processing route rather than following automatically from the word “modified”.

### Representative capacity examples quoted by the review — not yet independently re-verified
These values are transcribed from the review’s main text to seed primary-paper checks. They must not be treated as a homogeneous ranking because antibiotic, pH, feed concentration, mineral composition and model differ:
- **Ca-montmorillonite (SAz-1) / ciprofloxacin:** 330 mg/g at initial concentrations 500–4,000 mg/L under acidic/neutral conditions, attributed to Wang et al. (2010). This is the same primary paper extracted in Section 2 below.
- **Na-montmorillonite / tetracyclines:** at pH 5.5, Langmuir values reported for chlortetracycline 79.97 mg/g, oxytetracycline 33.15 mg/g and tetracycline 49.78 mg/g; initial concentrations about 3.6–110.1 mg/L, attributed to Figueroa et al. (2004).
- **Clay comparison for tetracycline:** the review cites maxima 355 mg/g for low-charge Na-MMT (SWy-2), 460 mg/g for high-charge Ca-MMT (SAz-1), and 140 mg/g for rectorite (RECT), at initial concentrations 100–3,000 mg/L, attributed to Chang et al. (2009).
- **Bentonite:** review reports 47.37 mg/g for amoxicillin at an initial concentration of 300 mg/L; 147.06 mg/g for ciprofloxacin at initial concentrations 50–500 mg/L; and 156.7 mg/g for tetracycline at 5–200 mg/L. These values come from distinct studies/conditions and require primary-source rechecking.
- **Sepiolite:** S-logistic model capacities 132 mg/g for marbofloxacin and 112 mg/g for enrofloxacin, initial concentrations 20–1,800 mg/L, attributed to Sturini et al. (2016).
- **Stevensite:** values quoted for tetracycline, oxytetracycline and chlortetracycline are 127.1, 126.1 and 139.9 mg/g, respectively, with initial concentrations 50–1,000 mg/L, attributed to Antón-Herrero et al. (2018).
- **Kaolinite:** the review gives much smaller examples: 21.2 and 25.1 mg/g for marbofloxacin and enrofloxacin; 4 mg/g for tetracycline and 6.3 mg/g for ciprofloxacin in separate studies. Do not generalize this ranking without harmonizing material source and conditions.

### Representative modification examples quoted by the review — citation discovery only
- Carboxymethyl-chitosan–MMT composite: cited capacities 178.57 mg/g for tetracycline and 271.74 mg/g for chlortetracycline over initial concentrations 5–400 mg/L (Ma et al., 2019).
- Wheat-straw biochar compared with montmorillonite–biochar (MMT-BC): norfloxacin Langmuir values 10.58 and 25.53 mg/g, respectively, at initial concentrations 0.4–15 mg/L (Zhang et al., 2018).
- Si- and Fe-pillared bentonite for ciprofloxacin: 100.6 and 122.1 mg/g, respectively, over 18–500 mg/L (Roca Jalil et al., 2017).
- Hydrothermally modified hybrid silicate from low-grade palygorskite: 329.84 mg/g for chlortetracycline and 207.47 mg/g for oxytetracycline at 50–800 mg/L; review reports increases of 218.9% and 107.9% versus raw palygorskite (Tian et al., 2016).
- Calcined Verde-lodo bentonite: 114.4 mg/g for ciprofloxacin and 116.72 mg/g for ofloxacin at 0.01–4 mM (Antonelli et al., 2020a,b).
- MgAl layered double hydroxide for minocycline: 200.5 mg/g; MgAl-LDH/γ-AlO(OH)/carbon composite: 302.5 mg/g, attributed to Li et al. (2015).
- Molecularly imprinted magnetic halloysite nanotube composite: 312.08 mg/g for norfloxacin, initial concentration 50–550 mg/L, attributed to Li et al. (2018).

These examples indicate candidate mechanisms and research threads, but the values should only enter our final evidence comparisons after we retrieve the cited original papers and capture actual model, initial concentration, pH, dose and experimental matrix.

### Mechanism and model-quality points from the review
- Proposed clay–antibiotic interactions depend on antibiotic speciation, clay charge and mineral structure, pH, coexisting ions, salinity and any pretreatment/modification.
- The review discusses electrostatic attraction/repulsion, cation exchange, cation bridging/complexation, hydrogen bonding, π interactions, London forces, hydrophobic interactions and pore/interlayer filling as possible contributors. Mechanisms should be assigned to a specific pair and conditions, not attributed generically to all clays.
- It explicitly warns that PSO/PFO kinetic fit alone cannot directly determine chemisorption or physisorption; kinetic/model results should be combined with FTIR, SEM/TEM, IEP/PZC, Raman, NMR, XRD and/or XPS evidence.
- It notes that IEP and PZC are distinct measurements and are sometimes wrongly treated as interchangeable.
- It stresses comparable experimental conditions and complete isotherms before comparing capacities. Many reported capacities use very high initial concentrations, which should be shown alongside any qmax.

### Real water, continuous flow, regeneration and economics
- In the reviewed corpus of 82 studies, the authors identify 15 studies with real wastewater experiments, only seven continuous-flow studies and no pilot-scale studies at the time of their review. These are historical review counts (search through 2021), not current 2026 counts.
- The review gives an example where ampicillin adsorption declined when switching from synthetic to pharmaceutical-company wastewater: from 89.9% to 59.3% for bentonite and from 100% to 90.6% for CTAB-bentonite, attributed to competition with other wastewater constituents.
- It reports that thermal activation may improve clay mechanical strength for fixed-bed systems, but high-temperature energy is part of the processing trade-off.
- Reuse is often underreported. Examples in the review include raw bentonite tetracycline removal declining from 50% to 30% after five cycles, while thermally activated bentonite declined from 95% to 83%; MMT–biochar norfloxacin removal remained around 86.5–88.1% across five cycles; and one HAP@MMT system recovered 99.1% ciprofloxacin with 1.0 M NaOH and retained adsorption efficiency above 78.5% after four cycles. These are selected review-reported examples and not directly comparable.
- Historical unit-price estimates quoted in the review: MMT 40–120 US$/ton, bentonite 74 US$/ton, granular activated carbon 1,000 US$/ton, CNTs 12,000–90,000 US$/ton and Ti-pillared clay 9,500 US$/ton. They are not current purchasing quotes; year, local market, grade and production scale need to be verified before use in a 2026 economic comparison.
- The review states that the literature’s clay-adsorbent cost analysis was mostly absent and that complete LCA was missing. It recommends accounting for capital equipment, materials, energy, labour, maintenance, regeneration chemicals/energy and end-of-life, along with full LCA.
- **Use in our manuscript:** a strong secondary map for taxonomy, mechanisms, critical limitations and backward/forward citation mining. It is not a substitute for original-study data; screen and extract primary papers individually.

## 2. Wang et al. (2010): cation exchange between ciprofloxacin and montmorillonite — primary evidence

### Identity and study design
- Journal of Hazardous Materials 183 (2010), 309–314; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhazmat.2010.07.025.
- Study type: original batch adsorption/mechanism experiment.
- Sorbent: SAz-1 montmorillonite from the Clay Minerals Society, reported at >98% montmorillonite content, Ca2+ as the main interlayer cation. External BET specific surface area = 65.2 m²/g; cation-exchange capacity = 123 meq/100 g (1.23 meq/g).
- Test design: 0.2 g clay + 20 mL ciprofloxacin solution in a 50 mL centrifuge tube; shaken 150 rpm for 24 h; tubes wrapped in foil to limit light-induced CIP decomposition. Isotherm feeds were 500–4,000 mg/L. pH-edge experiments used initial CIP 3,000 mg/L, adjusted to pH 2.5–11 with 1 M HCl or 1 M NaOH. Duplicate experiments were reported.
- Solids were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 min; supernatant passed through a 0.45 μm filter. Residual CIP was analysed by UV/Vis at 275 nm; a calibration range of 0–10 mg/L with R² > 0.99 is reported. The PDF does not explain in the main text how higher residual samples were diluted to fit this calibration range; flag only if methodological reconstruction requires it.

### Adsorption performance
- Fig. 2 and Section 3.1 (PDF p. 3; printed p. 311) show Langmuir fitted data with R² = 0.9997 versus Freundlich R² = 0.8744. The paper reports a maximum adsorption capacity of 330 mg/g, corresponding to 1.0 mmol/g.
- At initial CIP = 3,000 mg/L, the equilibrium concentration was 80 mg/L; this corresponds to 97.33% removal. The reported loading at this condition is approximately 292 mg/g (also shown in the FTIR figure caption for the sample prepared at C0 = 3,000 mg/L). Keep this observed loading separate from the reported 330 mg/g capacity parameter.
- The fitted/maximum 1.0 mmol/g loading is close to the clay’s CEC of 1.23 meq/g. The authors infer that exchange-site charge density, rather than external BET area alone, limits uptake under these conditions.

### pH and speciation
- Fig. 4 (PDF p. 4; printed p. 312) shows about 290–300 mg/g uptake over pH 2.8–8.8 and a decline to 247 mg/g at pH 11.
- The authors report CIP pKa1 ≈ 6.1 and pKa2 ≈ 8.7. Below pH 6.1, CIP is mainly cationic; between pKa values, zwitterionic species dominate; above pH 8.7, anionic CIP dominates.
- The clay surface remains negatively charged over the studied pH range. The authors attribute the high acidic/neutral uptake to the protonated amine participating in cation exchange/attraction, and the decline above pKa2 to repulsion between anionic CIP and negatively charged clay surfaces.

### Direct mechanism evidence
- **Cation-release stoichiometry:** ion chromatography showed Ca2+ was the main desorbed cation. The total released cation amount versus CIP uptake gave a fitted slope of about 0.99 with a negligible intercept (reported equation y = −0.05 + 0.99x, R² = 0.9935; Fig. 3, PDF p. 3 / printed p. 311). This is direct evidence supporting approximately stoichiometric exchange between CIP and interlayer cations.
- **XRD/interlayer expansion:** raw SAz-1 basal spacing d001 = 15.04 Å. At low uptake (<150 mg/g), it contracted to about 14.62 Å, consistent with partial loss of interlayer water during exchange. At high uptake (>290 mg/g), basal spacing expanded to 17.23 Å (Fig. 5, PDF p. 4 / printed p. 312), supporting interlayer incorporation of CIP as loading rose.
- **FTIR:** the ketone C=O band shifted from 1624 to 1628 cm−1; the protonated piperazine amine band shifted from 1385 to 1390 cm−1; carboxylate bands appeared at 1582 and 1379 cm−1 (Table 1, PDF p. 5 / printed p. 313). The authors interpret these shifts as consistent with interactions involving the protonated amine and hydrogen bonding of the carboxylic-acid group with basal oxygen atoms.
- **Proposed orientation:** combining XRD and FTIR, the authors propose a tilted orientation of CIP molecules in the interlayer at about 35° from the silicate layer. Treat this as the authors’ structural model inferred from spacing and spectroscopy, not a direct molecular image.

### Limitations and use in our review
- This is a strong source for the **cation-exchange mechanism** subsection because the authors measured displaced cations and paired that result with XRD and FTIR; evidence is stronger than a kinetic fit alone.
- It does not report adsorption kinetics, regeneration/reuse, competition from a real wastewater matrix, continuous-flow performance, cost, yield or LCA.
- Initial concentrations of 500–4,000 mg/L are far above the µg/L–low mg/L concentrations often encountered in wastewater. The 330 mg/g result is a high-concentration batch capacity and must not be treated as direct evidence of treatment performance at environmental trace levels.
- Duplicate testing and the absence of real-water validation should be recorded. Do not infer pilot or full-scale feasibility from this result.

- The PDF is the social-science study “The effect of proximity to protected areas on community adaptation to environmental change,” Journal of Environmental Management 301 (2022), 113805, DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2021.113805.

## Cross-paper synthesis / decisions

1. **Use the review to guide taxonomy and discover primary studies.** Its taxonomy spans raw clays through chemical, thermal, polymer, magnetic, pillared and biochar composites. Quantitative claims from it remain secondary until traced to originals.
2. **Cation exchange is mechanistically well supported in the Wang et al. CIP–SAz-1 study** by the near 1:1 relation of uptake to released cations, Ca2+ dominance, d001 change from 15.04 to 17.23 Å and compatible FTIR shifts.
3. **High capacity is not the same as practical trace-level treatment.** The primary montmorillonite study used 500–4,000 mg/L feeds, not realistic trace wastewater concentrations.
4. **Processing–cost–performance:** low raw-clay prices in the review do not imply all modified clays are low-cost. It highlights a large gap between raw-clay cost and pillared/composite processing costs, with few complete cost analyses and no full LCA in the reviewed studies.
5. **Real-water gap:** the review counted few real wastewater and continuous-flow studies, and none at pilot scale through its literature window. Do not state that as the current field-wide 2026 count; use it as the dated conclusion of this 2022 review.
6. No supplementary files were supplied. The assistant looked for them independently; Tables S1–S6 from the review were not retrieved during this pass. The main-paper evidence remains usable with clear caveats.
