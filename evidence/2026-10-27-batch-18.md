## A. Nguyen et al. (2025) — Progress in biochar-derived adsorbents
*Materials Advances* 6 (2025), 8839–8876. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1039/d5ma00872g. Existing candidate **CORP-021**. Review/supporting source.

### Processing continuum
The review covers pyrolysis, gasification, hydrothermal carbonization (HTC), torrefaction and subsequent modifications (PDF pp. 4–9). It summarizes pyrolysis as typically 300–900 °C and describes HTC as processing wet biomass at about 120–260 °C and 2–10 MPa over 30 min to 8 h, without the same pre-drying requirement as conventional pyrolysis. HTC may therefore be relevant when the feedstock is wet, but the review notes hydrochar often has lower fixed carbon, surface area and pore volume than pyrochar. Different feedstocks and conversion conditions produce different surface, pore and yield characteristics.

### Modification strategies and trade-offs
The review compares physical activation (including steam), acid/base/oxidant and metal-salt treatment, ball milling, plasma, electrochemical modification, atomic doping and hybridization with MOFs/MXenes (PDF pp. 6–9 and 24–26). It cites steam-activated sludge biochar with BET area 1583.07 m²/g and SMX uptake 204.07 mg/g in 90 min, and a corn-straw biochar example in which NH3 plasma treatment raised CIP removal from 31.8% to 85.7%. These are review-reported values from separate primary papers, not comparable on a shared condition set.
The review highlights burdens: acid oxidation can introduce acidic groups while damaging structure or lowering surface area; ball milling can produce highly water-dispersible particles; plasma/electrochemical methods may need specialist equipment or investment; chemical treatments and metal loading can produce hard-to-manage waste.

### Mechanisms, compiled capacities and gaps
The review describes electrostatic interaction, ion exchange, π–π interactions, surface complexation, capillary diffusion, hydrogen bonding and cation bridging, including use of DFT to investigate antibiotic–biochar interactions (PDF pp. 13–18). These mechanistic explanations are system-specific and should be traced to the cited primary source.
Table 3 (PDF p. 24) compiles diverse qmax values, including TC values of 1480.10 mg/g for N,S co-doped biochar and 1122.20 mg/g for porous graphitic biochar. The table also appears to include a BPA (bisphenol A, not an antibiotic) entry. Do not treat this table as a harmonized ranking.
The challenges section (PDF pp. 24–26, Fig. 14) flags feedstock/pyrolysis variability, lack of standardized material descriptors, residual PAHs/phenolics/metals, unsafe or complex modifier waste, and the need for pilot-scale testing and spent-adsorbent safety assessments. It does not provide a standardized process inventory, cost per kg, or comparative LCA.

## B. Singh, Vinayagam & Selvaraj (2026) — AC/biochar for tetracycline removal
*ACS Omega* 11 (2026), 33305–33319. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.6c00799. Existing candidate **CORP-026**. Review/supporting source; TC-specific scope.

### Search scope
The method section (PDF p. 2) says the authors searched Scopus and Google Scholar title/abstract/keyword fields using the Boolean query (“activated carbon”) AND (“biochar”) AND (“tetracycline” AND “adsorption”) AND (“biomass”). It synthesizes biomass selection, AC/BC preparation and activation, material characterization, TC adsorption mechanisms, kinetics/isotherms and operating parameters. It focuses on tetracycline, not all antibiotic classes; the inspected methodology does not show a PRISMA selection flow or included-paper count.

### Design factors and review examples
The review distinguishes activated carbon, which generally has a more developed pore structure and greater surface area, from biochar's generally lower-cost/resource-efficient preparation (PDF pp. 2 and 11–12). Sections 8.1–8.3 describe oxidation, amination, metal-oxide loading, pore-size engineering and composites; TC's molecular dimensions make pore accessibility important (PDF pp. 11–12). The review discusses π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, surface complexation and pore filling.
A cited Schizochytrium limacinum biochar example reports a Langmuir-estimated TC capacity of 1357.5 mg/g, but the same passage says the Freundlich isotherm described the equilibrium data more accurately (R²=0.96) (PDF p. 10). This is a secondary example with an internal model-interpretation caution, not a capacity we have independently verified.

### Practical implementation gaps
The conclusion and future perspectives (PDF pp. 11–12) highlight synthesis reproducibility, real-water variation in pH/ions/natural organic matter/microbial activity, repeated adsorption–desorption cycles, leaching of incorporated metals, pilot-scale validation, techno-economic analysis and LCA. The authors recommend greener scalable production, continuous or semicontinuous reactors and energy integration. The review itself does not conduct a harmonized TEA or LCA across materials.

## C. Ajala et al. (2023) — biomass/biochar-based adsorptive removal of antibiotics
*RSC Advances* 13 (2023), 4678–4712. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1039/d2ra06436g. Existing candidate **CORP-002**. Broad narrative review/supporting source.

### Scope and classification
The review spans antibiotic families including β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol and multi-antibiotic systems. Materials range from untreated agricultural biomass and biosorbents to biochar, activated carbon, bentonite, zeolites, chitosan beads and carbon nanomaterials. It discusses treatment-method comparisons (Table 2, PDF p. 6), sorption model theory, study-specific adsorption data (Table 6, PDF pp. 26–29), regeneration and fixed-bed engineering.

### Secondary performance values and metrics
Table 6 compiles primary-study conditions and results across different sorbents and antibiotics. It is useful for discovering candidate studies and checking whether the original authors reported dose, concentration, pH, time, temperature, models and mechanisms; it is not a harmonized performance comparison. Review text also makes an important endpoint distinction: high COD removal does not necessarily mean high antibiotic removal, and direct antibiotic concentration measurement is preferable (PDF p. 8).

### Regeneration, engineering and limitations
The review discusses thermal and solvent regeneration and acid–base methods (PDF pp. 29–30). Its fixed-bed section describes series/parallel arrangements and operational factors such as bed resistance, mass-transfer zones and pressure drop (PDF p. 30; Fig. 4 on p. 31). These descriptions are contextual and do not prove that each reviewed material has been validated in real wastewater.
The conclusion (PDF pp. 30–31) specifically raises sparse reporting on spent adsorbent and sequestered antibiotic fate; use of synthetic aqueous systems and possible matrix effects; environmental and economic concerns from chemical modifiers; and the need for engineering-scale and greener-modifier work. The review's stated gaps support our own matrix, regeneration, cost and spent-media evidence fields, but we will still verify material-specific claims in their primary papers.
