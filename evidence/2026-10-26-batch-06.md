## 1. Zhu et al. (2024) — in-situ N-doped porous biochar from antibiotic fermentation residue

### Identity and materials preparation
- Journal of Environmental Chemical Engineering 12 (2024), article 114433; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jece.2024.114433.
- Feedstock is oxytetracycline fermentation residue (OFR), from Gansu Qilianshan Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd. It is rich in organic matter and nitrogen from the fermentation nutrient medium. The article frames the process as a way to reuse a pharmaceutical by-product that can otherwise create residual-antibiotic/resistance-gene disposal concerns.
- OFR is dried at 105 °C to constant weight, ground and sieved to 60 mesh. It is pre-carbonized at 400 °C for 2 h under N2, using a 2 °C/min heating rate, then washed 3–4 times with DI water and dried at 80 °C to make POBC.
- In-situ N-doped porous biochar (AOBC-3–600) is prepared by mixing POBC and KOH at a 1:3 mass ratio, heating to 600 °C at 2 °C/min under N2 and holding for 1 h. After cooling, material is washed with 0.1 mol/L HCl until the wash is no longer alkaline, then with DI water to neutral, and dried at 80 °C.
- The 1:3 POBC:KOH ratio is justified by Supporting Information Table S1, which was not available in the uploaded main text. The main text supports the preparation recipe, but optimization across the other tested mass ratios is SI-dependent.
- No material yield, KOH consumption per kilogram product, wash-water volume, acid-neutralization/effluent cost, furnace energy inventory or product cost is reported in the main PDF.

### Characterization
- The BET surface areas reported (Fig. 1, PDF p. 3; §3.1, PDF p. 5) are 2.41 m²/g for POBC, 6.37 m²/g for unactivated UAOBC-600 and 1960.38 m²/g for KOH-activated AOBC-3–600.
- AOBC-3–600 is described as microporous, with 61.6% microporosity and predominant pore diameter 0–2 nm. SEM on PDF pp. 4–5 compares the relatively bulky/nonporous POBC and UAOBC-600 against the smaller, porous AOBC-3–600.
- Nitrogen content is 5.80 wt% in AOBC-3–600, compared with 0.35–3.42 wt% quoted for biochars produced with external N sources. C/H atomic ratio is reported at 31.76 and O/C below 0.6, which the authors interpret as evidence of increased aromaticity/stability.
- XPS identifies pyridinic N (N-6), pyrrolic N (N-5) and quaternary/graphitic N (N-Q); after activation, N-5 and N-Q contributions increase. FTIR/XPS also support O–H, C–O, C–N/C=N, C–C and carboxyl-related functionalities.
- Detailed elemental-analysis tables and other characterization parameters are in supplementary Table S3 and were not retrieved. Do not invent total yield/elemental closure from the graphical/text data.

### Batch adsorption conditions, dose and pH
- General batch tests: 20 mL solution in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, antibiotic C0 10–100 mg/L, AOBC-3–600 dose 0.125–2 g/L, pH 2–10, 180 rpm, 24 h at 25 °C in the dark, followed by 0.45 μm filtration and UV–Vis analysis. Target wavelengths are TC 360 nm, doxycycline (DOC) 360 nm, OTC 353 nm and CTC 365 nm.
- Dose tests at 100 mg/L, 25 °C and 24 h: raising dose from 0.125 to 2 g/L increased removal for TC, CTC, OTC and DOC from 36.87%, 41.72%, 57.43% and 36.74% to 79.06%, 98.84%, 100% and 99.55%, respectively. At the 0.125 g/L dose, reported capacities were TC 294.93, CTC 333.77, OTC 459.48 and DOC 293.89 mg/g; at 2 g/L the capacity per gram fell to around 50 mg/g because more adsorbent sites were available relative to solute quantity (Fig. 5a–b, PDF p. 6).
- pH tests (100 mg/L, 0.5 g/L adsorbent, 25 °C, 24 h; Fig. 5d, PDF p. 6): uptake maxima were reported at pH 3 for TC, pH 2 for CTC and OTC, and pH 9 for doxycycline. Authors attribute the acidic optimum for TC/CTC/OTC to protonated surface groups and electrostatic attraction; doxycycline's alkaline optimum is explained as additional interactions counteracting electrostatic repulsion.
- Contact-time tests (0.5 g/L, 100 mg/L, pH 5, 25 °C; Fig. 5e, PDF p. 6) report rapid initial adsorption during the first 240 min. In that rapid stage, the authors attribute 97.37% of total removal for TC, 91.17% for OTC, 78.84% for CTC and 53.97% for DOC to uptake. OTC reached the reported completion point at 600 min. Temperature tests (Fig. 5f) are captioned at 500 mg/L, 0.5 g/L and pH 5; this should remain distinct from the 100 mg/L time-series test.
- **Printed-equation issue:** on PDF p. 3, the paper prints qe = (Ce − C0)V/m and R = (Ce − C0)/C0 × 100%, which reverse the conventional sign for positive adsorption/removal when Ce < C0. Later reported results are positive and consistent with adsorption. Treat these as apparent typographical/sign errors in the printed equations; do not reproduce the printed formula as a correct equation without checking the publisher correction or SI.

### Adsorption capacity and isotherm interpretation
- The abstract and conclusion report **Langmuir-fitted maximum capacities at 25 °C** for AOBC-3–600: TC 473.00 mg/g, CTC 293.28 mg/g, OTC 220.61 mg/g and DOC 282.72 mg/g.
- The isotherm experiments described in Methods §2.5 use 20 mL antibiotic solutions at 100–500 mg/L with 0.005 g AOBC-3–600 (0.25 g/L), 180 rpm for 24 h at 25, 35 and 45 °C (PDF p. 3). The fitted values are referred to supplementary Table S6, which was not included. Therefore the capacities can be logged as abstract/conclusion-reported Langmuir Qmax, but full fitted parameters, uncertainty and goodness-of-fit values remain unresolved.
- The authors say CTC and DOC fit Langmuir better, while TC and OTC lean toward Freundlich; TC/OTC also show Langmuir preference at 45 °C. These model-preference details are described in §3.4, but numerical fit parameters are SI-dependent. Do not claim a model fit independently proves monolayer chemisorption or multilayer physical adsorption.
- Kinetic fits in §3.3 refer to supplementary Table S4. Main text reports pseudo-second-order R² = 0.9486–0.9970 and pseudo-first-order R² = 0.9167–0.9851. The authors interpret PSO as more consistent with chemisorption; state this only as the authors' interpretation, not as definitive mechanistic proof.
- Intraparticle-diffusion analysis (supplementary Fig. S1/Table S5) is described as three stages: rapid external-surface uptake, slower diffusion into internal micropores, and near-equilibrium saturation. Both surface adsorption and intraparticle transport contribute according to the paper.

### Mechanisms and co-existing ions
- Authors propose pore filling (supported by the increase from 2.41 to 1960.38 m²/g), electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions and Lewis acid–base interactions.
- FTIR bands for –OH, C–C and C–N/C=N shift after adsorption; XPS analysis of N-containing groups is used to support interactions with tetracycline molecules. These are convergent but mostly indirect mechanistic observations; the relative quantitative contribution of each mechanism is not separated.
- In ion tests (100 mg/L antibiotic, 0.5 g/L adsorbent, pH 5, 25 °C), CO3²−, HCO3− and PO4³− showed no significant effect; Na+ and K+ slightly promoted uptake, whereas Ca2+ and Mg2+ inhibited it. Authors ascribe divalent-ion effects to site competition and potential chelation with tetracyclines. Detailed concentrations/figures are in Supplementary Fig. S2.

### Reuse and environmental safety claims
- Desorption/regeneration used 0.1 M NaOH until no tetracycline antibiotics were detected in the solution, with five adsorption–desorption cycles.
- The text states that removal rate decreased by only about 4% after five cycles. However Table 1 lists reuse/removal figures of 96.90% (TC), 89.23% (CTC), 95.08% (OTC) and 95.44% (DOC) in a single “reusability” column without clearly mapping every number to a specific cycle in the table. Preserve both the prose claim and tabulated percentages; the exact target/cycle mapping is unresolved without Fig. S4.
- Leaching assay: varying doses (0–2 g/L) of AOBC-3–600 in 20 mL DI water, 180 rpm, 24 h at 25 °C, then 0.45 μm filtration and UV–Vis measurement at OTC wavelength. The authors report no absorbance increase relative to the DI-water control and infer that no OTC leached from AOBC-3–600 under these test conditions. This is not a broad leaching assessment for every compound, water chemistry or long-term use.
- They extracted DNA from AOBC/POBC and used PCR/qPCR for four resistance genes: tet(A), tet(C), tet(M), tet(O). No target bands were found. Authors interpret this as thermal decomposition of residual OTC and antibiotic resistance genes in the prepared biochar. This is useful risk-assessment evidence, but it is limited to those four genes and the assay/sample conditions; do not generalize to all AMR genes or all product-lifecycle risks.
- There is no real wastewater test, continuous-flow test, cost model, complete environmental assessment or scale-up study.

### Review use
This is a high-value Stage 4/engineered-biochar candidate showing the potential of using a nitrogen-rich pharmaceutical-production residue as both carbon precursor and N source, avoiding separate external N-dopant inputs. Its distinctive contribution is the very high reported surface area, four-tetracycline comparison and explicit residual-antibiotic/resistance-gene safety assays. Keep the high concentration regime, missing SI fits, formula sign issue and reuse table ambiguity visible.

## 2. Yan et al. (2024) — tetracycline adsorption by biochar review

### Identity and review scope
- Journal of Water Process Engineering 60 (2024), article 105215; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jwpe.2024.105215.
- This review focuses on tetracycline adsorption by biochar. It discusses biomass feedstocks and biochar preparation by conventional pyrolysis, hydrothermal liquefaction and microwave pyrolysis; physical, chemical and biological modification; the effects of pH, temperature, co-existing ions and organic matter; mechanisms; regeneration; and real wastewater.
- It is a secondary review, not an original adsorption experiment. Its tables summarize maxima and conditions from multiple primary papers that differ in feedstock, initial TC concentration, pH, dose, model and matrix.

### Useful material and preparation synthesis
- The review says biochar properties depend on biomass composition, especially lignin/cellulose/hemicellulose, as well as pyrolysis temperature, hold time, heating rate and atmosphere.
- Its Table 1 reports TC capacity examples: crayfish-shell biochar 39.10 mg/g; pomelo-peel biochar 14.42 mg/g; Camellia oleifera shell biochar 13.85 mg/g; rice-straw biochar 150.20 mg/g; straw 65.80 mg/g; tea-waste biochar 16.71 mg/g; Fraxinus marsh-leaf biochar 60.78 mg/g; lignin biochar 277.7 mg/g; aerobic-granular-sludge biochar 15.01 mg/g; cassava-ethanol-sludge biochar 34.04 mg/g; pharmaceutical-sludge biochar 94.69 mg/g; sewage-sludge biochar 89.30 mg/g; swine-manure biochar 127.80 mg/g; bovine-manure biochar 5.82 mg/g. These are review-transcribed maxima and must not be treated as a controlled ranking.
- Table 2 contrasts high-temperature pyrolysis, microwave pyrolysis and hydrothermal liquefaction. As summarized in the review: high-temperature methods commonly use 400–600 °C and have yield ranges depending on heating speed; microwave pyrolysis can shorten residence time but relies on suitable microwave absorption; hydrothermal liquefaction often uses 150–250 °C and can accept wet biomass but requires long residence time.
- The review emphasizes that activation/modification can change surface area, pore size, oxygen/nitrogen/phosphorus-containing groups and aromaticity. It distinguishes steam/heat/ball milling, acid–base, oxidant, metal salt/oxide, clay-mineral/organic-reagent and biological modifications.

### Mechanism and operating-factor synthesis
- The review highlights pore filling, hydrogen bonding, π–π electron-donor/acceptor interaction, electrostatic attraction/repulsion, Lewis acid–base interaction, surface complexation, cation exchange and cation–π interactions as possible contributors to TC adsorption.
- It discusses pH and TC speciation, surface charge, temperature, coexisting ions and humic acid/organic matter as relevant influences. Humic acid can inhibit adsorption through competition or, in some systems, improve apparent uptake by acting as a bridge/new-site contributor.
- The review notes that the effects of temperature and modification involve a trade-off: higher temperatures can improve pore development/aromaticity but reduce yield and oxygenated functional-group abundance; production and regeneration energy need consideration.
- These are review-level interpretations, not a universal mechanism list that can be assigned to every biochar–tetracycline system without primary evidence.

### Regeneration and real-water examples
- The review describes solvent/eluent regeneration, microwave desorption and thermal regeneration. It notes solvent regeneration can create a secondary waste stream containing desorbed pollutants, while thermal regeneration can consume energy and alter or damage biochar.
- Selected examples summarized in the real-water section include:
  - Zn-loaded biochar retaining TC adsorption in two types of sewage, with reported capacities 52.65 and 85.64 mg/g.
  - High-mesoporosity phosphorus-containing biochar reducing TOC in medical-production wastewater from 53.2 to 18.4 mg/L.
  - Banana-peel biochar used for industrial wastewater cross-contaminated with TC and Pb, with approximately 96% TC removal.
  - One study reporting TC adsorption around 180 mg/g in tap and river water.
- These examples need their cited primary sources retrieved before quantitative comparison. In particular, TOC reduction is a bulk-organic metric and must not be described as target-specific tetracycline removal.
- The review concludes that most regeneration studies report stability over approximately 2–5 cycles, but this is a cross-study generalization with differences in media, eluent, cycling protocol and performance metric.

### Review use
Useful for developing the taxonomy and subsections on synthesis, modification, operating factors, mechanisms and real-water evidence. It should be used for citation discovery and gap mapping, not as a new independent primary-study record for every number in its tables. Prioritize its real-wastewater and regeneration citations for primary-paper intake.

## 3. Zango et al. (2024) — systematic review of biomass-derived biochar and activated carbon

### Identity and review-method audit
- Journal of Water Process Engineering 67 (2024), article 106186; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jwpe.2024.106186.
- The review states its objective is to assess biochar and activated carbon derived from biomass for antibiotic adsorption in pharmaceutical wastewater, including optimization methods, mechanisms, stability/reuse, real wastewater and economics.
- Search period: 2015 through 30 April 2024. Databases/resources: Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Scopus and ResearchGate.
- Reported search strings include “Biochar and activated carbon for antibiotic adsorption” on ScienceDirect/ResearchGate and “biochar AND activated AND carbon AND antibiotic OR pharmaceutical AND adsorption” on Scopus/Web of Science. The latter needs caution because its displayed Boolean structure lacks parentheses that would make the intended logic explicit.
- Fig. 1 (PDF p. 3) reports initial records: Web of Science 451, ScienceDirect 4,995, Scopus 107, and ResearchGate 48 (total 5,601); after duplicate removal it reports 3,054; relevant-content screening 632; full-text eligibility 359; qualitative assessment 305; and 162 studies included in the SLR. The figure also gives a full-text exclusion tally of 54. These steps/counts are not fully reconciled in the figure and narrative: “305 qualitative assessment” versus “162 included” needs checking before quoting a final PRISMA count. Preserve as the authors' printed counts, flagged as an internal reporting ambiguity.
- In the narrative, 301 articles are described as considered in the SLR when discussing journal contributions, which also does not align directly with the PRISMA chart’s 162 included studies. Do not state a single verified final inclusion number without resolving this discrepancy.
- The review is a useful evidence-discovery resource, but its search window ended in April 2024 and is not a current 2026 systematic search.

### Optimization-method discussion
- The review compares one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT), design-of-experiments (DoE), response-surface methodology (RSM) and Taguchi methods.
- It argues that DoE/RSM provides a more efficient/precise multivariable view than OFAT and is generally preferable for understanding interactions; this is the review's synthesis, not a universally proven rule that applies to every adsorbent experiment.
- Common operating factors include concentration, pH, contact time, temperature and adsorbent dose. It emphasizes that multivariable optimization can reduce experiments and improve interpretability.

### Mechanisms, real-water evidence and regeneration examples
- Mechanisms summarized include hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, electrostatic interactions, π–π interaction, pore filling and complexation. These mechanisms depend on the adsorbate, the carbon's surface chemistry, solution pH and wastewater composition.
- The review highlights that performance may decline in real matrices relative to synthetic water:
  - Fe/Ti biochar composite (FeMBIOCHAR600): norfloxacin/ciprofloxacin removal was reported to decline by about 10% in Nanming River water versus synthetic water.
  - Pomegranate-peel biochar: batch ciprofloxacin removal was reported as 96.31% in synthetic wastewater versus 89.94% in real wastewater; column removal 94.39% versus 84.74%, respectively.
  - Brewery-waste-derived activated carbon in urban WWTP effluent: for sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim and ciprofloxacin, pristine AC removal was 36%, 64% and 56%, while oxygen-functionalized AC-O achieved 42%, 84% and 78%.
- Regeneration examples in the review include walnut-shell biochar regenerated with 0.1 M HCl for repeated sulfonamide use, pomegranate-peel biochar reused for ciprofloxacin with up to 81.27% removal at the fifth cycle, and PET-based AC composites reused for tetracycline over six cycles with over 58% efficiency retained. These are review-reported examples, not harmonized cycle-to-cycle metrics.
- Use these studies as priorities for citation mining and primary-PDF acquisition. Do not represent the review's removal numbers as new independent observations.

### Economics and boundary cautions
- Table 4 reports historical production-price examples: coconut-shell biochar US$0.80/kg; coconut-shell activated carbon US$1.50–3.00/kg; granular AC US$6.40/kg; virgin-wood feedstock biochar US$17.80/kg; sludge-derived biochar US$0.70/kg; HCl-modified biochar US$0.69/kg; Si-modified biochar US$1.44/kg; chitosan-AC US$15.40/kg; Filtrasorb-400 US$20–22/kg; commercial AC US$21/kg.
- These values come from different locations and referenced years, are not a current comparable price list, and do not share a single cost boundary. Keep currency, location, study year and production/operation boundary visible when source-mining the originals.
- The review notes production cost depends on feedstock, conversion technology, operating scale, energy, modification, regeneration, labor, transport and adsorption efficiency. It reports historical biochar costs ranging from US$0.69–17.80/kg and activated-carbon prices of US$1.50–22/kg in its cited table.
- The review compares biochar as generally lower-cost but more variable/lower-capacity, and AC as often higher-performing/more expensive. Treat these as high-level tendencies from the included literature, not universal comparisons.
- Real-world deployment limitations identified include complex matrices, competing dissolved organics/ions, material leaching, regenerant waste streams, long-term stability and the scarcity of detailed production/operational economics.

### Review use
Use this as the principal secondary source for systematic-review method examples, RSM/DoE versus OFAT discussion, real-water effect sizes and economic data-source discovery. Because of the count discrepancies and historical prices, independently log our own searches and verify the original studies before citing exact numerical claims.

## Cross-paper synthesis from Batch 06

1. **Circular feedstock and nitrogen doping:** Zhu et al. convert antibiotic fermentation residue into an N-rich, KOH-activated microporous carbon. It is a useful engineered-biochar case that links pharmaceutical manufacturing waste, adsorption and limited material-safety assays. High qmax values came from concentrated batch solutions; no real effluent/scale-up validation was tested.
2. **Keep primary and secondary sources separate:** both reviews offer useful taxonomies and reference lists, but do not let a review-table number be counted as another independent adsorption experiment.
3. **Real-matrix evidence deserves special treatment:** the tetracycline review and the systematic review identify real-water studies, but matrix-specific removal/TOC examples differ in targets and conditions; cite the primary papers before quantitative comparison.
4. **Optimization methods:** RSM/DoE can be discussed as methods to resolve factor interactions more efficiently than sequential OFAT in appropriate designs. Phrase the comparative advantage as the systematic review's conclusion, not a universal law.
5. **Cost comparisons require comparable system boundaries:** historical material prices across different years/locations and production methods cannot be compared directly with contemporary estimates or with whole-process treatment costs.
6. **Open verification flags:** AOBC reuse cycle/table mapping, printed sign errors in adsorption equations, SI-dependent fit statistics and cost assumptions, review PRISMA-count discrepancy, and every quantitative value taken solely from either review.
