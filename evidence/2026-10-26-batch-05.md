## 1. Usama et al. (2025) — waste-PET activated carbon

### Material preparation and characterization
- Separation and Purification Technology 364 (2025), article 132487; DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seppur.2025.132487.
- PET water bottles were cleaned, rinsed, air dried for 24 h, and cut to approximately 1 × 1 mm.
- Plastic char (PC) was made under N2 by pyrolysis at 500 °C for 2 h with a 10 °C/min ramp.
- To screen activators, PC was stirred for 2 h in 1 M NaOH, HCl, H3PO4 or KOH; the activated solids were filtered, washed to remove residual chemicals, dried at 90 °C for 2 h and calcined at 700 °C for 120 min.
- KOH was selected for the optimization stage. KOH:PET/PC mass ratio was varied between 1:1 and 2:1, and the carbonization temperature was tested at 300, 500, 700 and 900 °C while calcination remained at 700 °C.
- The selected sample is kC1170 (KOH activation, 1:1 ratio, 700 °C carbonization). Reported product yield was 25% from PET waste. The authors’ scale scenario assumes 4 kg PET waste per kg kC1170.
- The main text reports BET surface area 448.09 m²/g for kC1170 versus 337.07 m²/g for PC. KOH activation increases microporosity; SEM shows an irregular layered/porous structure. The point of zero charge is 3.4.
- Ntx is labelled an antibiotic by the paper, but nitazoxanide is more commonly classified as an antiparasitic/antiprotozoal agent. Retain the paper’s tested target but do not imply that every tested compound is a conventional antibiotic.

### Adsorption experiments and main performance
- The experiments include both individual contaminants and a Dxy + Ntx mixture (called SDN).
- Broad screening assessed Congo red dye, sulfasalazine (SFZ), dibenzothiophene (DBT), Cr(VI), Dxy and Ntx. The figure and text report that kC1170 performed well among the eight KOH-activated variants; conditions differ by target.
- The main mixture-optimization work was conducted at 25 °C. pH tests covered a wide acidic-to-alkaline range; adsorbent dose was varied from 0.3 to 1.2 g/L; mixture concentration was varied from 50 to 250 mg/L; contact time was varied from 0 to 90 min.
- Authors report about 90% removal within 15 min and equilibrium by around 35 min for Dxy, Ntx and their mixture under the selected conditions.
- The abstract reports maximum capacities at 15 °C of 138.05 mg/g for Dxy, 126.79 mg/g for Ntx and 324.75 mg/g for the mixture SDN. Detailed kinetic and isotherm parameters are in supplementary Tables S2–S3, which were not retrieved here.
- In the dose screen, the paper reports q values of 246.3 mg/g Dxy and 267.5 mg/g Ntx at 0.3 g/L with the lowest removal percentages (61.5% and 75.2%). At 1.0 g/L the text gives removal rates 89.3% and 90.83% and q values 87.21 and 89.66 mg/g, respectively.
- The pH study reports the best removal at pH 3: for Dxy, 89.58% and a reported capacity of 107.5 mg/g; Ntx is described as having similar performance. Authors report a pH shift in surface charge around PZC 3.4.
- The adsorption figures and narrative have numerical/condition inconsistencies:
  1. Fig. 3d states 120 mg/L of each antibiotic, 1 g/L adsorbent, pH 4 and 100 mL. The plotted q markers appear around 170 mg/g for each antibiotic, above the 120 mg/g maximum possible for a single target at 100% removal under those stated conditions. A combined total q could be possible, but the figure uses separate qDxy and qNtx markers and the text is ambiguous.
  2. In Fig. 4b/text, 120 mg/L is given with varying doses, but the reported q values at 1.0 g/L do not align cleanly with removal fraction × initial concentration / dose if both refer to the same target and condition.
  3. The abstract includes a “maximum adsorption capacity (q) of 250 mg L−1” phrase, although q is otherwise expressed as mass per mass of adsorbent. The detailed capacity values are reported separately in mg/g.
- These values are therefore recorded as author-reported but flagged where they do not satisfy a straightforward mass-balance check. Do not select a preferred number silently or harmonize q and removal until the SI or author clarification resolves it.

### Co-ions, natural water and regeneration
- Fig. 6e compares distilled water, a natural water sample sourced from GIK Institute in Swabi, and common ions.
- In the natural water test, authors report approximately 95% Dxy removal but 67% Ntx removal, compared with distilled-water removal around 98.7% Dxy and 96.2% Ntx.
- The controlled ion screen reports nitrate as a strong inhibitor of Ntx removal (down to 37.5%) while Dxy remained around 97.6%. Increasing ionic strength with NaCl or CaCO3 also reduced adsorption, much more strongly for Ntx than Dxy.
- Regeneration involved ethanol washing, oven drying at 90 °C and calcination at 400 °C. The main text reports regenerated performance of about 72.5% for Dxy and 70.1% for Ntx, followed by a marked decline on the next cycle to 38.2% and 35.5%, respectively. The exact figure-defined basis for every percentage should be preserved; do not call the values a desorption mass balance.
- This is natural-water evidence, but not a fully characterized pharmaceutical wastewater effluent. The natural-water composition is not comprehensively reported in the main text.

### Mechanisms
- Authors propose electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions; DFT and reduced-density-gradient analysis indicate stronger modelled binding for Dxy than Ntx.
- DFT adsorption energies are reported as −16.90 kJ/mol for Dxy, −11.75 kJ/mol for Ntx and −3.389 kJ/mol for co-adsorbed SDN. The mixed case is interpreted as involving competition/steric hindrance.
- These are theoretical interaction energies, not direct quantitative allocations of experimental uptake among mechanisms.

### LCA and cost — useful but boundary-limited
- The study reports a 25% kC1170 yield, a scenario of about 1,615 t/year, ASPEN v11 pilot modelling, capital cost around US$1.354 million, annual production costs around US$20.95 million, unit price US$13.75/kg, net profit around US$0.85 million, ROI 63%, and payback 1.6 years.
- Reported LCA value: 5.92 kg CO2-eq per kg kC1170. The authors report 24.04 MJ process energy per kg, and attribution contributions of KOH 71.72%, PET 24.5%, DI water 2.54%, HCl 1.08% and energy 0.12%.
- Figure 8 (PDF p. 14) shows the mass/energy flow and 12 midpoint-impact categories. It is helpful for understanding activation chemicals and washing water as dominant contributors.
- The LCA boundary includes PET collection, pretreatment, pyrolysis/activation and post-processing for production of 1 kg adsorbent, but excludes disposal of spent adsorbent, stage waste-management, application/use and handling/disposal of adsorbed antibiotics.
- The authors themselves call the LCA and cost comparison preliminary and acknowledge uncertainty and the need for a more comprehensive assessment. Treat these as author-reported pilot scenarios, not as a fully harmonized cradle-to-grave LCA or guaranteed current market price.

### Review use
A high-value paper for the processing–cost–performance continuum because it attempts to integrate PET waste valorization, activation optimization, antibiotic-mixture adsorption, DFT, LCA and pilot economics. Its reported headline performance needs careful source-condition checking, and its sustainability case should always be presented with the stated exclusions.

## 2. Fraiha et al. (2024) — clay/pharmaceutical adsorption review

### Scope and taxonomy
- Desalination and Water Treatment 317 (2024), article 100114; DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dwt.2024.100114.
- This is a review of pharmaceutical contaminants broadly, not an antibiotic-only review. It covers activated carbon, biochar, nanoadsorbents, synthetic materials, ion-exchange resins, agricultural wastes and low-cost adsorbents, with a special focus on clays.
- Clay types include montmorillonite, bentonite, kaolinite, palygorskite, sepiolite, illite and vermiculite, and the review includes natural and modified examples.
- Modification examples in Table 2 include CTAB/other surfactants, NaCl and thermal activation. The review describes modification as a way to tune ionic affinity/surface properties, rather than evidence that every modification always improves performance.
- Unlike a formal systematic review, the main text does not provide a reproducible database-search strategy, search strings, screening flow or explicit inclusion/exclusion counts. Treat as a narrative/critical overview unless outside bibliographic verification establishes otherwise.

### Antibiotic examples in Table 2 — secondary evidence only
The following are figures as summarized by the review. Because Table 2 often combines different feed concentrations, pH, contact times, temperatures, adsorbent types and models, these examples are suitable for source mining—not direct apples-to-apples ranking:
- Bentonite / ciprofloxacin hydrochloride: 93.4–97.8% removal over 50–500 mg/L, pH 4.5, about 30 min.
- Bentonite / amoxicillin: 97.9% removal at 50 mg/L following hexadecyl-trimethyl-ammonium modification, pH 7, 30 °C, 320 min.
- CTAB-modified bentonite: 90% levofloxacin removal over 30–250 mg/L and 96% gemifloxacin removal over 30–400 mg/L, pH 5, 25 °C, 24 h.
- Montmorillonite / ciprofloxacin: 98% removal at 250 mg/L, 60 min, pH 7, according to one cited study.
- Montmorillonite / ciprofloxacin in Wang et al. (2010): about 97% removal at 500–4,000 mg/L and 24 h, which is the same high-concentration study already extracted as a primary paper in Batch 3.
- Kaolinite / oxytetracycline: initial concentration 1–35 mg/L, pH 5.5, 25 °C, 24 h; removal percentage is not provided in the review table.
- The review also lists nalidixic acid, tetracycline, enrofloxacin, ampicillin, clarithromycin and other pharmaceuticals. Not every listed pharmaceutical is an antibiotic.

### Interpretation, limitations and manuscript use
- The review states that pH/speciation has a major influence on pharmaceutical adsorption and that real effluents remain under-studied; many studies use laboratory-prepared water.
- It summarizes PSO as a frequent best-fit kinetic model and Langmuir/Freundlich as common isotherms. These are descriptive review observations; kinetic/isotherm fit alone does not prove a specific chemical mechanism.
- Some table rows contain mixed target compounds, incomplete values and inconsistent precision/labels. For example, the initial-concentration column is “mg/L” while some studies combine several targets in a single row. Primary papers must be consulted before using numbers in the master quantitative comparison.
- The article is useful for references on antibiotic adsorption by bentonite, montmorillonite and kaolinite. Prioritize original studies with actual experimental data and mechanisms, not this review's compiled percentages.
- As a 2024 review whose own search methods are not explicitly reproducible in the main text, do not call its findings an exhaustive or up-to-date systematic map of the field.

## 3. Yaqubi et al. (2021) — leached carbon-black waste and chitosan beads

### Material preparation, safety and characterization
- Journal of Environmental Chemical Engineering 9 (2021), article 104988; DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jece.2020.104988.
- Refinery carbon-black waste (CBW) was leached in 1 M H2SO4 at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 0.1 g/mL for 1 h, vacuum-filtered, washed and dried at 105 °C to make powdered LCBW.
- LCBW elemental composition: C 93.5%, H/N below 0.5%, S 0.9%, O estimated at 4.6–5.6%. Main results report a BET area around 375 m²/g and average pore diameter around 3.629 nm.
- Acid leaching reduced V from >16,000 ppm to 400 ppm, Ni from >4,000 ppm to 1,100 ppm and Fe to 600 ppm. Cd, Pb and As were not detected; other listed metals were mostly below 100 ppm.
- **Internal surface-area inconsistency:** results section reports ~375 m²/g, while the conclusion describes the surface area as ~275 m²/g. Keep both statements recorded and do not silently choose one.
- LCBW-chitosan beads were prepared using 5 g LCBW dispersed in 190 mL of 2% acetic acid, followed by 4 g chitosan; after stirring and degassing, the gel was dripped into 1 M NaOH, then washed to pH 7.5–8.5.
- Beads: density 1.287 g/mL, diameter 3.56 ± 0.6 mm, moisture content 94.1%, estimated LCBW loading 20.5 ± 0.5 mg/g and BET area around 20 m²/g with pore diameter 3.395 nm. The authors note beads lose integrity below pH 5.
- Although soluble metal leaching is described as insignificant by citing earlier work, this article does not provide a comprehensive new leaching/toxicity evaluation for the adsorbent after use.

### Batch conditions, removal and capacities
- Temperature 22 °C, pH approximately 7.5–8.5 (except amoxicillin in distilled water, which caused a pH drop of about 2 units). Experiments in duplicate; stated maximum experimental error ±5%.
- Powdered LCBW: TC feed was 50 mg/L; removal after 24 h was 68% in distilled water and 80% in tap water. Amoxicillin feed was 25 mg/L; removal was 72% in distilled water and 91% in tap water.
- LCBW-chitosan beads: TC (25 mg/L) removal after 24 h was 92% in distilled water and 87% in tap water. Amoxicillin (25 mg/L) removal was 62% in distilled water and 74% in tap water.
- Table 6 reports empirical capacities:
  - Powdered LCBW: TC 181 mg/g (distilled water), 205 mg/g (tap water); amoxicillin 42 mg/g (distilled), 65 mg/g (tap).
  - LCBW-chitosan beads: TC 39 mg/g (distilled), 38 mg/g (tap); amoxicillin 12 mg/g (distilled), 15 mg/g (tap).
- **Powdered-TC dose ambiguity:** the methods say 0.2 g LCBW was dispersed in 500 mL water and then an equal 500 mL antibiotic solution was added, giving a final 1 L volume and 50 mg/L TC. This corresponds to 0.2 g/L final adsorbent dose, although the text also states 0.4 g/L. The reported capacities of 181/205 mg/g are closer to an approximately 0.2 g/L dose, but the dose description must remain flagged. For amoxicillin, the stated dose is 0.4 g/L.
- These tests use tap water, not actual municipal wastewater or hospital effluent. The article argues for potential use upstream of wastewater treatment but does not test a real hospital effluent.

### Kinetics and fixed-bed performance
- Most uptake occurred within the first 7 h; equilibrium was around 24 h, except for amoxicillin on powdered LCBW.
- Pseudo-second-order model fit powdered LCBW for tetracycline very well (R² > 0.999); powdered-LCBW amoxicillin PSO fits were lower (R² 0.885–0.977). Pseudo-first-order fit the composite-bead kinetics well (R² > 0.995). This is statistical fit evidence, not stand-alone proof of chemisorption.
- Intraparticle-diffusion plots had multiple linear segments and did not pass through the origin; the authors infer that intraparticle diffusion was not the only rate-limiting step.
- Continuous TC adsorption was tested in tap water using LCBW-chitosan beads, with bed heights 6.5, 10.5 and 14.5 cm and flow rates 1 or 2 mL/min. Initial TC concentration was approximately 49.2 mg/L at 1 mL/min and 51.8 mg/L at 2 mL/min.
- For a 14.5 cm bed and a breakthrough threshold C/C0 = 0.1, the paper estimates about 60 h to breakthrough at 1 mL/min and 25 h at 2 mL/min. Greater bed height increased breakthrough time; higher flow shortened it.
- Table 7 reports model-derived Thomas capacities around 4.756–8.757 mg/g. Both Adams–Bohart and Thomas models produced close/overlapping fits, with R² 0.795–0.969, but authors conclude neither model fitted the breakthrough data well. Do not treat the model values as validated service-life capacities.

### Mechanisms, regeneration and economics
- XPS/FTIR identify C–C/C=C, hydroxyl, carboxyl, carbonyl/amide and C–O–C/amine-related features. The authors propose hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, π–π/hydrophobic interactions and possible metal bridging/complexation.
- Tap-water ions improved powder adsorption, consistent with electrostatic screening/bridging: TC removal increased from 68% to 80%, amoxicillin from 72% to 91%. The effects were weaker or reversed for composite beads (TC 92% to 87%; amoxicillin 62% to 74%).
- No regeneration cycle study or treatment-specific cost estimate was reported in this paper. The authors describe the material as potentially cost-effective because it valorizes refinery waste, but do not give a unit production cost. No full LCA is supplied.
- Residual V/Ni/Fe, acid leachate treatment, bead integrity, and post-use management are relevant to a processing–cost–performance assessment.

### Review use
This is valuable primarily for comparative evidence across raw powdered waste-derived carbon and an immobilized biopolymer composite, including tap-water matrix effects and one continuous fixed-bed test. It also shows a major performance trade-off: the powder has much higher per-gram adsorption capacity, whereas beads are mechanically recoverable and can be used in a column. The fixed-bed model limitations and unresolved powder-dose description must remain visible.

## Cross-paper synthesis from Batch 05

1. **Waste-to-carbon economics need boundaries.** The PET-AC article is unusual in presenting both an LCA and a pilot economics model, but it excludes spent-media disposal, use-stage waste and desorbed antibiotic management. These reported numbers are a useful case study, not a final whole-life sustainability ranking.
2. **The PET-AC adsorption numbers need a source audit.** Figure captions and reported q values have a mass-balance inconsistency under stated conditions, so some values cannot enter a quantitative ranking until corrected/clarified.
3. **Review-derived clay values remain secondary.** Fraiha et al. provide a useful clay reference map, but individual removal rates should be captured from the original studies before synthesis.
4. **Powder vs bead is a meaningful architecture comparison.** LCBW powder gives high empirical capacities but is difficult to retain in a fixed bed; chitosan beads are recoverable and demonstrated in a TC column but have much lower per-gram capacities.
5. **Real matrices and columns are still scarce.** This batch provides tap-water and a natural-water test, but not a validated full-scale wastewater process. No current journal quartile has been assigned by this batch.
6. SI was searched for independently but not retrieved. The user is not expected to supply it; unresolved points stay flagged in the evidence and caveat registers.
