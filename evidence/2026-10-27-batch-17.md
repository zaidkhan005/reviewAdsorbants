## A. Mirizadeh, Converti & Casazza (2025) — chitosan-based adsorbents in batch and dynamic operation
*International Journal of Biological Macromolecules* 331 (2025), 148399. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.148399. Existing candidate **CORP-018**. Systematic review; use as supporting literature and a discovery source, not as primary experimental evidence.

### Review search and scope
The review searches Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, Scopus and PubMed for 2018–2025 publications. Its PRISMA figure (PDF p. 2) reports 477 records identified, 342 removed before screening, 135 title/abstract records screened, 38 removed for insufficient data, 97 full texts assessed, 23 excluded and 74 studies included. These comprise 59 batch studies and 15 dynamic/fixed-bed studies. Inclusion focuses on peer-reviewed English-language experimental studies using adsorbents combined with chitosan to adsorb antibiotics from water. One stated exclusion criterion is publication in journals with impact factor below 3; this may bias source coverage and should be noted when using the review's claimed comprehensiveness.

### Secondary batch findings
Tables 1–3 (PDF pp. 6–8) collect qmax, qe, best-fit models and test conditions from original studies. The review highlights tetracycline as the most studied antibiotic, chitosan–MOF composites among reported high-capacity formulations, and frequent use of PSO kinetic and Langmuir isotherm fits. A Table 3 example reports Fe3O4/MOF/grafted chitosan for trimethoprim with Langmuir qmax 391.90 mg/g at pH 8, Co = 0.26–0.34 mg/L, dose 0.8 g/L, 100 min and 318 K; kinetic qe is reported as 383.22 mg/g at C0=0.3 mg/L. This is only a secondary transcription, so the apparent high uptake at a low initial concentration must be checked against the cited primary paper's full mass balance.

The review says PSO often fits kinetic data best and infers chemisorption in places. A good PSO fit is not itself proof that chemisorption is the rate-limiting mechanism; do not restate it as direct mechanism verification.

### Secondary fixed-bed results
Table 6 (PDF p. 15) compiles 15 dynamic studies. Examples include:
- Chitosan–cellulose composite for tylosin: Q=5 mL/min, bed height=5 cm, C0=10 mg/L, breakthrough time=146 min, exhaustion=736 min, q0=47.2 mg/g.
- PVP–chitosan–ZnFe2O4 for amoxicillin: Q=0.5 mL/min, C0=40 mg/L, breakthrough=115 min, reported q0=218.9 mg/g.
- Aminated chitosan–cellulose microspheres for sulfamethoxazole: Q=4 mL/min, bed height=1.5 cm, C0=10 mg/L, breakthrough=102.5 min, exhaustion=797.5 min, q0=117.4 mg/g.

These values should not be blended into a batch qmax table. Units/metric definitions, flow geometry, dosage and challenge concentration differ between cited studies, and the review table's compressed/blank cells need checking against the original papers. The review also describes flow-rate sensitivity: in one cited tylosin column, increasing Q from 4 to 6 mL/min shortened saturation time from 620 to 446.67 min while treated volume to saturation increased from 2480 to 2680 mL.

### Processing–cost–performance implications
Table 7 (PDF p. 16) contrasts simple controlled batch screening with continuous fixed beds, which are closer to practical operation but more complex and sensitive to flow disturbance, channeling, pressure drop, fouling and mass-transfer-zone utilization. It explicitly identifies a relative scarcity of dynamic studies (15 of 74 selected papers). The paper does not provide a harmonized techno-economic/life-cycle comparison across adsorbents; for the review, use it to motivate the gap, not to claim costs have been established.

## B. Mohammed-Ridha & Abdul-ahad (2014) — levofloxacin on barley husks and eggshells
*Journal of Engineering*, Vol. 20, No. 12, December 2014, pp. 88–104. DOI: https://doi.org/10.31026/j.eng.2014.12.06. Existing candidate **CORP-019**. Primary experimental study.

### Source identity and materials
The uploaded filename says “(2023),” but the journal imprint on the PDF is clearly December 2014. The screening record already has the corrected year; retain 2014. The study used a commercial 500 mg levofloxacin tablet for stock solutions rather than documenting a pure active-ingredient standard. Materials were 0.6–1 mm. Table 1 (PDF p. 11) reports BET area as 0.302 m²/g for barley husks, 0.163 m²/g for eggshells and 1100 m²/g for activated carbon; ash content is 19.6%, 84% and 5%, respectively.

### Reported performance
The abstract reports 74% levofloxacin removal for activated carbon, 71% for barley husks and 42% for eggshells. Activated carbon and barley husks used 5 g/L; eggshells used 50 g/L, so the eggshell result requires a tenfold higher dosage. In pH tests at 150 mg/L and 30 °C, the reported uptake increased from pH 4 to pH 7:
- Activated carbon: 3.8 to 5.2 mg/g.
- Barley husks: 2.1 to 3.7 mg/g.
- Eggshells: 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g.

The paper reports approximate time to constant uptake as 50 min for barley husks, 110 min for activated carbon and 40 min for eggshells. These tests use prepared/high-concentration water, not actual wastewater or trace environmental concentrations.

### Isotherm and kinetic discrepancies
Isotherm tests used 25–150 mg/L, 100 mL, pH 7, 3 h at 30 °C, with 0.5 g of activated carbon/barley husks and 5 g of eggshells. Table 2 (PDF p. 11) gives Langmuir q_m values: activated carbon 34.211 mg/g; barley husks 125.735 mg/g; eggshells 5.6423 mg/g. Although Langmuir R² values are high (0.995, 0.988 and 0.995), the fitted barley q_m is not experimentally validated by the available mass range: at C0=150 mg/L in 100 mL with 0.5 g sorbent, at most 15 mg antibiotic is present, giving an observed uptake ceiling of 30 mg/g. The eggshell ceiling is 3 mg/g at its stated 5 g dose. The fitted q_m is therefore an extrapolated parameter and the barley estimate is especially questionable.

A larger internal inconsistency occurs in Table 3 (PDF p. 12). For a separate 1 L setup at C0=150 mg/L and target Ce/C0=0.05, the paper states doses of 20.745 g activated carbon, 30.722 g barley husks and 720 g eggshells. With at most 142.5 mg removed at that target, the mass-balance ceilings are approximately 6.87 mg/g, 4.64 mg/g and 0.198 mg/g, respectively. But Table 3 reports PFO qe values of 30.66, 23.81 and 17.12 mg/g, and PSO-calculated qe values of 4.71, 6.87 and 0.21 mg/g. Several values exceed the limits from the stated doses, in some cases widely. The PFO and PSO qe estimates also diverge strongly. Do not use these kinetics values as verified uptake data without reconciling the experimental setup or table errors.

## C. Hamdi et al. (2024) — tetracycline adsorption/desorption on raw and activated Tunisian clays
*Environmental Research* 242 (2024), 117536. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2023.117536. Existing candidate **CORP-008**. Primary experimental study.

### Material and activation protocol
Three raw Tunisian clays were sampled from the Maknessy–Mazzouna basin: AM, HJ1 and HJ2. Raw material was sieved at 50 µm and dried at 60 °C. Base activation mixed clay with 5% solid Na2CO3 (1.5 g per 30 g clay) in 300 mL water at 75 °C for 1 h; the settled product was dried at 60 °C without washing. Acid activation used HCl at 3 N and 75 °C for 4 h; activated samples were washed with distilled water to pH 6 and dried at 60 °C.

Table 3 (PDF p. 6) reports raw clay pH 8.39–9.08, electrical conductivity 446–495 dS/m, organic matter 14–20% and eCEC 80.65–97.45 cmolc/kg. These very high EC magnitudes and units should be preserved as published pending verification; don't silently rewrite them. Mineralogical composition varies by source: AM has 51% calcite, HJ1 30% kaolinite and 14% sepiolite, and HJ2 32% microcline (Table 1, PDF p. 3).

### Adsorption tests and observed results
Batch tests used 0.5 g clay in 10 mL of 0.005 M CaCl2 solution containing 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800 or 1000 µmol/L tetracycline; 24 h at 50 rpm and 25±2 °C in the dark, in triplicate. Figure 3 (PDF p. 7) and the Results discussion report near-100% adsorption for raw clays at higher additions and a maximum observed amount of 12,672.7 µmol/kg at C0=1000 µmol/L. Raw clay performance was generally similar. Adsorption was not significantly affected by solution pH within this experiment (ANOVA p=0.981); after contact, pH was reported in the range about 6–10. The entire initial concentration range (25–1000 µmol/L, roughly 11–444 mg/L) is much higher than typical trace environmental concentrations, so high removal is not proof of trace-level treatment performance.

### Processing–performance trade-off and reversibility
Acid/base activation generally retained high adsorption performance at the higher concentrations but could reduce adsorption at the lowest concentration, with activated-clay removal around 75% in the lowest-concentration case. The authors conclude activation was not necessary and could reduce capacity. Activated products also required additional chemicals/heat and (for HCl activation) washing/drying, yet no quantitative cost, yield, acid/base consumption per kg, wastewater treatment burden or LCA was reported.

Desorption was measured by adding fresh 10 mL 0.005 M CaCl2 after adsorption, not by repeated regeneration cycles. Figure 4 and Tables 6–7 (PDF pp. 10–12) report desorption below 10%, mostly below 4%, and average hysteresis indices around 0.956–0.973. This shows strong retention under this single lab release condition; it does not establish antibiotic degradation or safe spent-clay disposal.

### Isotherms and mechanism caveats
The Sips model was the best overall empirical fit reported by the authors, with higher R²/smaller errors than the other tested fits. Table 5 (PDF p. 9) reports fitted (q_m) in µmol/kg; this is not directly an mg/g field capacity. The authors discuss cation exchange and interactions with exchangeable Na, Ca and Mg, as well as surface charge/speciation effects. These are interpretations based on the material/adsorption results, not direct proof of a unique molecular mechanism. Supplementary Table S1 was not included in the supplied PDF.
