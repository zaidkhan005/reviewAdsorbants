## 1. Alidadi et al. (2018) — tetracycline on modified sawdust

### Feedstock and modification route
- Melia azedarach sawdust was obtained from a local carpentry in Mashhad, Iran; washed, oven-dried at 80 °C for 3 h, and sieved to 40–60 mesh.
- Raw sawdust (RS) was modified four ways:
  - MS(I): 5 g RS in 100 mL 200 mg/L CaCl2 solution, shaken 2 h at room temperature; washed and dried 80 °C/3 h.
  - MS(II): 5 g RS in 100 mL 0.7 M NaHCO3, shaken 4 h at room temperature; washed and dried 80 °C/3 h.
  - MS(III): 5 g RS in 100 mL 10% v/v HCl at 80 °C/1 h; washed to neutral pH and dried 80 °C/3 h.
  - MS(IV): 5 g MS(III) in 100 mL 0.75 M FeCl3 in 3 M HCl, shaken 4 h; settled 30 min; heated at 100 °C/12 h to stabilize the iron coating, then washed to neutral pH and dried 80 °C/3 h.
- Characterization: SEM, FTIR and pHPZC for Fe-modified sawdust. SEM showed a coarser porous fibrous surface after modification. FTIR bands assigned to Fe–O–H bending at 902/897 cm−1. Reported pHPZC of MS(IV) = 4.15. No BET surface area or iron-leaching analysis is reported in the uploaded manuscript.

### Screening comparison and conditions
- Initial modification comparison: TC concentration 10 mg/L, dose 3.0 g/L, pH values 4, 7.5 and 9.5, contact time 0–120 min, mixing 100 rpm, 22±2 °C. Fig. 1 (PDF p. 10) compares RS, CaCl2, NaHCO3, HCl and Fe-modified sawdust.
- RS removal was generally below 10%. MS(I) usually achieved about 20–40%; MS(II) generally below 20%; MS(III) about 20–40% with a maximum reported 41.50% under one condition. MS(IV) performed best; at pH 7.5 and 9.5 with contact time >60 min, removal was above 60%.
- Response surface methodology (central composite design, 30 runs) studied C0 5–25 mg/L, pH 3–12, dose 1–5 g/L and time 15–120 min. Table 2's measured response range was 18.60–93.52% (PDF pp. 12–15).
- RSM predicted an optimum of 98.40% removal at C0 12.5 mg/L, pH 7.9, dose 4.8 g/L and time 118.3 min (abstract/Table 4, PDF pp. 2/18). In Table 5's separate model-validation scenarios, measured removal ranged up to 92.37%; the presented validation cases do not reproduce the exact 12.5 mg/L / pH 7.9 / 4.8 g/L / 118.3 min point. Therefore keep **model-predicted optimum** distinct from experimentally validated values.
- In synthetic tests, contact-time increase from 15 to 120 min increased TC removal from 50.97% to 67.66% at C0 15 mg/L, pH 7.5 and dose 3 g/L (results section). The paper notes removal was still rising at 120 min, so equilibrium may occur later.

### Isotherms and kinetics
- Isotherms: initial TC 5–30 mg/L, dose 4.0 g/L, 90 min, pH 7.8. Freundlich had the better fit (adjusted R² = 0.9891, SSE 0.16, AIC −12.62); Langmuir adjusted R² = 0.9802, SSE 0.28, AIC −8.42, fitted Qm = 5.41 mg/g, RL = 0.19 (Table 6, PDF p. 20).
- The 5.41 mg/g value is the reported Langmuir fit capacity under these conditions; the paper itself says Freundlich is the preferred isotherm fit. Do not treat the Langmuir Qm as a directly measured saturation loading.
- Kinetics: C0 20 mg/L, pH 7.8, dose 1.5–4.5 g/L, time 0–120 min. PFO R² values ranged 0.991–0.997 and PSO 0.989–0.996. AIC was −18.74 (PFO) vs −18.32 (PSO), so the authors concluded both described the data well and PFO was slightly better by AIC. This is not a unique mechanistic diagnosis.

### Regeneration and real hospital wastewater
- TC-loaded MS(IV) was prepared using 5 g adsorbent in 100 mg/L TC for 24 h, then dried at 35 °C/4 h. Ten regenerating solutions were compared, including distilled water at pH 4, 7 or 10, ethanol, methanol, acetone, chloroform, dichloromethane, trichloromethane and acetonitrile.
- Acidic distilled water (pH 4) and methanol were best, with methanol slightly better but more costly/toxic; the authors preferred acidic water on those grounds. Table 7 compares subsequent TC removal at 10–25 mg/L. At the optimal regeneration-solvent adsorbent loading (25 g/L in the desorption step), the calculated fraction of water reclaimed was 88%, meaning the remaining 12% of the treated water was used during regeneration. This 88% is **water reclamation**, not an 88% antibiotic-desorption efficiency.
- Regeneration was only a single stage; it is not evidence for repeated multicycle life.
- Real sample: secondary hospital wastewater from Imam Reza Hospital, Mashhad, initially had TC at 0.25 mg/L and fell to “not detectable by HPLC” after treatment at pH 8.3, dose 3.6 g/L and 53 min (PDF p. 24). The paper does not give a numerical HPLC detection/quantification limit in this report, so do not state zero concentration or complete removal.
- The authors identify limitations: only two isotherm and two kinetic models, single-stage regeneration, and non-conformability between synthetic and real-wastewater TC concentrations (PDF p. 25).

### Review use
A useful Stage 1 processing–performance study: mild precursor, multiple chemical modifications, Fe-based enhancement, RSM and one hospital-effluent test. Avoid treating the 98.4% predicted optimum as a verified operating result, and do not turn 88% water reclamation into desorption efficiency. Cost and environmental burden of FeCl3/HCl modification, neutralization water and iron leaching are not quantified.

## 2. García-Criado et al. (2025) — purified stevensite for tetracyclines and metabolites

### Feedstock and purification
- Source clay was Moroccan Rhassoul/lava clay, predominantly stevensite, from the Jebel Rhassoul region. Starting oxide composition in Methods (PDF p. 17) was primarily SiO2 (69.048%) and MgO (18.878%) with smaller mineral oxides.
- Preparation: wash raw clay, dry/mill/sieve; disperse 30 g in 1 L distilled water, stir 30 min; add 1 M HCl gradually until CO2 release stops (carbonate removal); disperse in 1 M NaCl for 12 h to obtain Na+-homoionic clay; separate particles <2 μm by Stokes-law sedimentation; dry at 50 °C/24 h and grind.
- This is purification/particle-size and exchange-form treatment of a natural clay, not a synthetic functionalization. The purification creates water/acid/salt handling and waste-stream burdens not quantified in a TEA/LCA.
- BET surface area 147 m²/g, pore volume 0.122 cm³/g, mean pore diameter 3.32 nm, type-II N2 isotherm (PDF pp. 3–4; full numerical characterization table is SI Table S1). SEM/XRD/FTIR/zeta potential/EDS were used before/after adsorption.

### Targets and batch conditions
- Thirteen target compounds: eight tetracycline antibiotics (TC, chlortetracycline CTC, minocycline MNC, demeclocycline DMCC, doxycycline DC, oxytetracycline OTC, tigecycline TGC, metacycline MTC) plus five metabolites (4-epitetracycline ETC, anhydrotetracycline ATC, 4-epianhydrotetracycline EATC, 4-epichlortetracycline ECTC, 4-epioxytetracycline EOTC).
- Main methods state 10 mL target solution, 20 mg purified ST (2 g/L), 350 rpm, room temperature, with initial concentration 2 mg/L for the general batch protocol (PDF p. 18). The abstract reports dose 2 g/L and concentration 5 mg/L; a kinetics passage appears to print “2 mg mL−1,” which conflicts with the 2 mg/L methods and capacity in the kinetic table. This concentration/unit discrepancy must stay flagged. Real-water tests explicitly used samples spiked at 2 mg/L.
- pH effects: pH 2–9; salinity 1–4% (described as % NaCl), humic acid/DOM. Isotherm series 0.25–100 mg/L, higher than trace environmental levels. Kinetics measured from 5 seconds to 24 h, but most compounds were already near equilibrium rapidly.
- Most compounds showed >80% adsorption within the first 0.5 min and equilibrium in under 5 min; 30 min was selected for follow-on tests. Abstract/conclusion phrase equilibrium within 30 min, but main kinetic results say under 5 min. Keep the conditions and reporting distinction attached.

### Removal and environmental effects
- Abstract reports >99% removal of tetracyclines and metabolites at an acidic pH, 2 g/L, 5 mg/L. Main results state >99% for most compounds over broad pH ranges except extreme conditions. pH 3–6 generally gave the highest adsorption; at pH 2, some tetracyclines likely underwent degradation and/or repulsion, while pH 9 also showed some compound-dependent degradation/reduced adsorption. The article itself notes degradation can confound apparent adsorption at extreme pH.
- DOM/humic acid had little effect for most compounds. At humic acid up to 25 mg/L in the relevant effect test, OTC and its metabolite EOTC decreased only slightly (about 0–3% and 0–1.5%, respectively); most other compounds remained above 99%.
- NaCl/salinity had a stronger negative effect. As salinity rose from 0% to 4%, OTC removal fell by about 50%, EOTC by 30–40%, TC/ETC/ATC/ECTC by roughly 20%, and several other analytes by around 10%. This shows good removal under DOM but vulnerability to saline water, especially OTC.
- Real water: tap water, surface/river water, influent wastewater and effluent wastewater samples were filtered through 1.2 μm glass microfiber filters, spiked at 2 mg/L, and analysed with matrix-matched LC-MS/MS calibration. Removal remained >99% for most compounds, with a stated matrix complexity trend: distilled water > tap water > surface water > effluent wastewater > influent wastewater. Example changes were small (OTC 99.98→99.82%, MTC 99.98→99.9%). These are spiked matrix experiments rather than trials on unspiked native antibiotic concentrations.

### Isotherms and a major source inconsistency
- PFO and PSO models were fitted. Table 1 (PDF p. 8) shows PSO fit R² = 1.0000 for every compound and calculated equilibrium capacities almost exactly matching the experimental values (~0.98–1.58 mg/g); PFO fits varied and were generally weaker.
- Freundlich fit was better overall than Langmuir, with reported R² 0.817–0.968 vs 0.755–0.908 for Langmuir. Freundlich n values were 0.288–0.389, indicating removal efficiency/capacity response changes with concentration.
- **Do not use the article's Langmuir qmax claims as reliable cross-paper capacities.** The narrative states values “over 200,000 mg/g” for tetracycline (one passage instead calls this TGC), which is physically implausible and internally contradictory. Yet Table 2 (PDF p. 9) lists values of 86.1 mg/g for TGC, several values exactly 1000 mg/g (including TC) and other values 221.6–883.6 mg/g. This mismatch between text and tabulated parameter values is a major source-quality issue. The direct kinetic q_exp values of ~1.0–1.6 mg/g under the low-concentration batch protocol are much better grounded than the extreme narrative qmax claim.
- Mechanisms discussed: cation exchange, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, coordination with interlayer cations and pore filling. Characterization changes support interaction with the mineral, but these are inferred mechanisms, not proof of any single pathway.

### Regeneration and limits
- Five adsorption–desorption cycles. Regeneration used 5 mL McIlvaine buffer/acetone (7:3 v/v) at pH 4, followed by DI-water rinse and drying at 40 °C. Text reports tetracycline desorption efficiency >99% after four cycles and retention of >90% adsorption capacity after the fifth cycle; confirm cycle-resolved details against SI Fig. S9 before detailed cycle-by-cycle numeric comparison.
- The high desorption/reuse result depends on an acetone-containing eluent; handling/recovery/treatment of the solvent and concentrated antibiotic stream is not costed. No unit treatment cost, full LCA, column test or pilot-scale validation is reported.
- The authors explicitly recommend further pilot-scale work and dynamic column adsorption. The article's statement “large-scale” potential is aspirational, not a demonstrated pilot result.

### Review use
A strong natural-clay study for breadth of targets (antibiotics plus metabolites), rapid batch kinetics, DOM tolerance, salinity vulnerability and real-water spiking. It also demonstrates why the review must scrutinize model-derived qmax values against experimental uptake and internal units. Include capacities only with clear labels and avoid citing the >200,000 mg/g narrative value as real.

## 3. Li et al. (2026) — ammonia-assisted ball-milled N-doped biochar for tetracycline

### Preparation and characterization
- Apple-tree branches from local orchards/campus were dried at 80 °C and pyrolysed under N2 at 700 °C for 2 h, then ground/sieved to 100 mesh to make raw biochar (BC).
- Modified biochar: 2 g BC mixed with 15 mL of 25 wt% ammonia solution plus zirconia balls of 15, 10 and 6 mm in the reported mass ratio 1:10:11, then milled at 300 rpm for 24 h, reversing direction every 30 min with a 5-min pause. The selected product was QN7.5BC; QBC is the same ball-milled control without ammonia. Other ammonia-to-biochar ratios were tested for optimization.
- BET/textural results in the main text (PDF p. 5): QN7.5BC area 206.8825 m²/g, total pore volume 0.2951 cm³/g, micropore volume 0.03822 cm³/g (13% total), mean pore diameter 11.8834 nm. After TC adsorption, area fell to 144.4029 m²/g, total pore volume 0.2532 cm³/g and micropore volume 0.01082 cm³/g, consistent with pore occupation/blocking. The abstract instead prints total pore volume “276 cm³/g,” inconsistent with the main text; use the detailed main-text value and flag the abstract as a unit/value error.
- FTIR/XPS/Raman support the introduction of pyrrolic-N, graphitic-N and pyridinic-N, plus oxygen-containing groups. Raman ID/IG decreased from 1.131 to 0.924 after N modification (reported as higher structural order/stability). No production yield, electricity measurement, unit material cost or full LCA is reported.

### Adsorption performance, conditions and isotherms
- Figure 3 compares BC, ball-milled QBC and N-doped QN7.5BC: reported TC removal 6.6%, 43.8% and 95.7%, respectively. This comparison is valuable because it partially isolates the effect of ball milling vs the additional ammonia/N-doping step.
- Under the reported selected condition, dose 0.5 g/L and initial TC 30 mg/L at native pH, QN7.5BC achieved 95.7% removal (PDF pp. 7, 10). Raising adsorbent dose from 0.2 to 0.5 g/L raises removal from ~65% to >95%, then plateaus. At 10–30 mg/L C0 removal was >95%; at 40 and 50 mg/L it decreased to 91.2% and 89.5%.
- pH effect: pH 3–9 removal >90%, peak 95.1% at pH 8; removal fell to 83.5% at pH 10–11 and recovered to 89.9% at pH 12. The authors report an isoelectric point pHIEP ≈6.
- Langmuir fitted Qm for QN7.5BC: 155.7405 mg/g at 298 K, 219.8073 mg/g at 308 K and 400.0180 mg/g at 318 K (Table 1, PDF p. 7); R² 0.9983, 0.9931 and 0.9793. Ball-milled but undoped QBC Qm was only 29.9875, 32.1855 and 41.7961 mg/g at the same temperatures (with a weak 298 K fit, R² 0.7781). Label these as fitted Langmuir capacities, not observed field uptake.
- Kinetics: PFO R² 0.8576 with calculated qe 65.16 mg/g vs experimental qe 64.49 mg/g; PSO R² = 0.9998 with calculated qe 64.94 mg/g. Intraparticle diffusion plots show three stages (surface diffusion, mesopore diffusion, slower micropore diffusion/equilibration). The authors call the PSO fit chemisorption-controlled, but their own FTIR/thermodynamics propose combined noncovalent interactions. Do not treat PSO fit as proof of a unique chemical mechanism.
- Thermodynamics: ΔG −1.77, −2.91, −4.40 kJ/mol at 298, 308, 318 K; ΔH +37.24 kJ/mol and ΔS +130.69 J/(mol K) as reported. The authors interpret adsorption as endothermic and more favorable at higher temperatures.

### Matrix, co-contaminants and reuse
- Real-water tests: TC removal 95.7% in deionized water, 89.2% in tap water and 92.5% in river water (PDF p. 9). These are spiked/specified tests, not a continuous real-wastewater column study.
- Most common ions had limited effect. Cl−, NO3−, SO42− and HCO3− had little impact across 20–80 mmol/L, with removal >86%; H2PO4− and CO32− decreased removal to 83.9% and 75% at higher concentrations. Mg²⁺ was the strongest reported inhibitory cation: efficiency decreased 69.8→56.3% as Mg²⁺ rose from 20 to 80 mmol/L. Humic acid had moderate impacts. The article also tests OTC and dyes (methylene blue, malachite green, rhodamine B, Congo red); these should be kept distinct from the TC-specific experiment.
- Six consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles were reported. Cycle 1 removal was 95.6%; cycle 2 fell sharply to 54.6%, and cycle 6 fell to 40.6%. This is a major limitation on claimed sustainable reuse despite strong first-cycle performance.
- Regeneration washed spent adsorbent sequentially with DI water, 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH (3–5 times) then dried. This procedure did not maintain capacity across cycles. No spent-sorbent antibiotic mass balance, column run, or full post-regeneration waste treatment is reported.

### Review use
This is a useful Stage 4 example showing how an explicit N-doping and mechanical processing step can transform raw biochar into a much stronger first-cycle adsorbent. However, include process intensification costs and regenerateability in the continuum: 700 °C/2 h pyrolysis plus 24 h ball milling are substantial processing inputs, and capacity falls rapidly over six cycles. It is not demonstrated as cost-effective by a quantitative TEA/LCA in this paper.

## Cross-paper synthesis — Batch 09

1. **Modification can improve first-cycle performance, but not uniformly across the life cycle.** FeCl3-modified sawdust substantially outperforms raw/other-modified sawdust; N-doped, ball-milled biochar strongly outperforms the ball-milled control; purified stevensite gives rapid high removal across many analytes.
2. **Real-water evidence needs matrix labels.** Alidadi reports TC below HPLC detection in secondary hospital wastewater after high sorbent dosing. Stevensite tests are spiked real-water samples; QN7.5BC tests use deionized/tap/river matrices. None of these is a full continuous-flow pilot trial.
3. **Report observed and fitted values separately.** Alidadi's Qm is 5.41 mg/g and its preferred isotherm is Freundlich. Stevensite's narrative qmax claims conflict badly with its own Table 2 and experimental q_e. QN7.5BC's Qm 155.74–400.02 mg/g is Langmuir-fitted and temperature-dependent.
4. **Regeneration is a first-order process/economic issue.** Sawdust study demonstrates one-stage regeneration and 88% reclaimed water; stevensite relies on buffer/acetone and retains >90% capacity by cycle five as reported; QN7.5BC falls to 40.6% removal by cycle six.
5. **No papers in this batch supply a complete unit-treatment TEA/LCA.** Sawdust has a water-reclamation calculation only; stevensite describes low cost but no quantified cost; ball-milled biochar does not quantify mill/pyrolysis electricity or cost.
6. **Important source flags:** Alidadi predicted optimum vs validation cases and below-detection wording; stevensite concentration/unit contradictions and implausible/internal inconsistent Langmuir qmax; QN7.5BC abstract pore-volume typo and steep regeneration loss.
