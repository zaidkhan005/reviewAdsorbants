## 1. Jiao et al. (2024) — oxytetracycline-imprinted magnetic biochar

### Identity and preparation
- Bioresource Technology 395 (2024), article 130394; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biortech.2024.130394.
- A magnetic biochar (MBC) acts as carrier; OTC is the template; methacrylic acid (MAA) is the functional monomer; ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA) is the crosslinker; AIBN is the initiator. A non-imprinted control (MBC@NIPs) is made without OTC template.
- Preparation described in the main PDF: preassemble OTC and MAA in ultrapure water; add MBC and EGDMA; deoxygenate; heat to 60 °C; initiate with AIBN and react for 24 h; use magnetic separation, wash/purify and vacuum-dry at 60 °C. The article cites an earlier method for the base magnetic biochar and refers to supplementary Text S2 for further details. The precursor identity, full pyrolysis recipe, material yield, modifier consumption and energy/cost are therefore not completely reproducible from the uploaded main PDF alone.
- Figure 1 (PDF p. 2) compares SEM, FTIR, XRD, XPS, magnetic hysteresis and TGA for MBC and MBC@MIPs; authors interpret the rounded-particle shell and spectroscopy as evidence of an imprinted polymer layer. Magnetic hysteresis suggests superparamagnetic behaviour, and the authors report magnetic separation in under 10 s.

### Adsorption conditions and performance
- General equilibrium tests: 250 rpm, 25 °C, 6 h; samples filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and OTC measured by HPLC.
- Dosage study: 5 mg/L OTC, 25 °C, pH 6.50 ± 0.1; MBC@MIPs dose 0.1–1.0 g/L. Removal rose from 17.0% at 0.1 g/L to 89.37% at 0.5 g/L; 1.0 g/L improved removal only marginally, so the authors selected 0.5 g/L.
- pH study: 0.5 g/L, 5 mg/L OTC at 25 °C. Removal was highest at pH 6.34 (about 89.4%), decreased to 41.6% at pH 3 and to 26.8% at pH 10. Figure 2 covers the pH/dosage tests (PDF p. 3). Authors attribute the low-pH/high-pH decline partly to electrostatic repulsion involving MAA-containing surface groups.
- Kinetics (Table 1, PDF p. 4): 0.5 g/L adsorbent, 2 mg/L OTC, pH 6.50 ± 0.1 and 25 °C. Fast early uptake was reported (about 70–90% within 4 h); equilibrium was approached over 6 h. For MBC@MIPs, PFO fitted capacity 10.84 mg/g (R² 0.9805); PSO fitted capacity 12.55 mg/g (R² 0.9995); the narrative gives experimental uptake about 12.07 mg/g.
- Isotherm range: 1–200 mg/L OTC. Table 2 (PDF p. 5) gives MBC@MIPs Langmuir capacity 67.89 mg/g (R² 0.9821), Freundlich R² 0.9034, and a reported experimental uptake of 60.19 mg/g at the stated condition; the paper describes an 11.3% deviation between calculated and experimental values. This 67.89 mg/g is model-fitted and measured at much higher concentrations than the ng–µg/L range typical of trace monitoring.
- **Internal capacity discrepancy:** Table 2 lists MBC (non-imprinted magnetic biochar) Langmuir capacity as 38.34 mg/g, while the abstract and narrative state 38.84 mg/g. Keep this unresolved rather than silently choosing one. The table also reports BC 8.96 mg/g and MBC@NIPs 41.09 mg/g.
- The abstract says 299 K; the experiments generally describe 25 °C (~298 K). Preserve the paper’s stated values and flag this small temperature-reporting difference if an exact temperature comparison is needed.

### Selectivity, interference, mechanism and reuse
- Selectivity tests: 0.5 g/L MBC@MIPs, individual antibiotic solutions at 4 mg/L, 6 h, 25 °C, with competing antibiotic mixtures also tested (Fig. 4, PDF p. 5).
- Single-solute capacities reported: OTC 11.97 mg/g; tetracycline (TC) 4.01 mg/g; doxycycline (DOX) 7.28 mg/g. **Mass-balance caveat:** Fig. 4a caption states 4 mg/L antibiotic and 0.5 g/L adsorbent, so the absolute uptake ceiling is 8 mg/g at 100% removal; reported OTC uptake 11.97 mg/g exceeds this ceiling. Treat it as unresolved and do not use it in quantitative comparisons without clarification. Reported imprinting factors (IF): OTC 5.64; DOX 2.93; TC 2.95; chlortetracycline (CTC) 4.14; norfloxacin (NOR) 1.05; metronidazole (MNZ) 1.34.
- In the mixed-antibiotic test, OTC capacity was 6.10 mg/g and IF was 6.70; values also reported for NOR, LCM and MNZ were 2.30, 1.14 and 1.21 mg/g, respectively. These are controlled laboratory mixtures, not real wastewater or urine validation.
- FTIR/XPS and imprinted-vs-non-imprinted comparisons support the presence of a polymer recognition layer and functional-group interactions. The authors infer chemisorption primarily from the PSO fit; this alone is not proof of a unique molecular mechanism. State this as the authors’ interpretation, not settled mechanistic evidence.
- Authors tested iron leaching: 100 mg MBC@MIPs in 10 mL distilled water for 24 h at pH 2–9, with iron analysed by graphite-furnace AAS. The supplied main PDF does not provide the numerical results, which are referred to supplement/context; do not claim low leaching is demonstrated from the methods alone.
- Seven reuse cycles were tested. The authors report a 9.36% reduction in OTC binding across seven cycles (Fig. 4c, PDF p. 5). The cycle procedure used 0.5 g/L in 4 mg/L OTC for 1.5 h followed by magnetic separation. Exact cycle-by-cycle data are graphical.
- No real wastewater, continuous-flow column, cost estimate, regeneration solvent balance or environmental-life-cycle assessment is supplied in the uploaded main text.

### Review use
Use in the engineered/advanced carbon section as a case study in **selectivity engineering** and magnetic recovery. Do not describe the study as demonstrating real-wastewater removal or low-cost production. Keep qmax, observed uptake, removal percentage and imprinting factor in separate fields.

## 2. Mosaffa et al. (2024) — bacterial-cellulose-char / chitosan–PEI beads

### Material and preparation
- International Journal of Biological Macromolecules 273 (2024), 132953; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2024.132953.
- The material is a composite bead: bacterial-cellulose-microfilament char (BCM char) embedded in chitosan (CS)/branched polyethyleneimine (PEI), crosslinked using glutaraldehyde (GA). This is a biopolymer-based composite with a char component, not a standalone biochar powder.
- BCM was cultured from bacteria in black-tea/sucrose solution for 14 days. For BCM char, 20 g crushed cellulose membrane was hydrolysed with 100 mL cold 64% H2SO4 and washed; 4 g never-dried filaments were dispersed in 50 mL water, sonicated 1 h, adjusted to pH 2 with 1 M HCl and hydrothermally treated at 200 °C for 18 h (10 °C/min ramp); material was collected and washed (Methods, PDF p. 2 / printed p. 2).
- Bead synthesis: 100 mL of 1% chitosan in 2% acetic acid; add PEI at a reported 1:1 molar ratio; stir 1 h and filter; add 0.2 g BCM char; sonicate 1 h; add 3 mL of 1% GA dropwise; drip the semi-gel into 1 M NaOH through a 1.5 mm needle; collect, wash and freeze-dry. The authors attribute bead formation to Schiff-base reaction between GA aldehydes and CS/PEI amines.
- Reported BET surface area: PEI/CS beads 5.98 m²/g; BCM char 112.42 m²/g; final BCM-char/CS/PEI beads 89.81 m²/g. Final bead BJH pore volume 0.0991 cm³/g. Bead diameters were mostly 1.5–1.6 mm, over a reported measured range of 1.5–2 mm. EDX composition of bead: C 44.95%, O 27.05%, N 28.00%. The main paper reports a pore-size figure around 68.4 Å; it calls this “sub-micropores,” a terminology/size interpretation to check before reproducing.
- No material yield, cost, chemical-recovery balance, energy inventory or end-of-life assessment is supplied.

### Experimental conditions and performance
- Main batch tests: 4 mg adsorbent in 15 mL solution (~0.267 g/L), 298 K. The time series uses 50 mg/L TET or MET, pH 8 for TET and pH 6 for MET; 90 min was the reported optimum contact time (Fig. 7, PDF p. 7).
- pH tests cover pH 3–9 using 0.1 M HCl/NaOH. TET uptake/removal peaked at pH 8; MET at pH 6. Authors associate the TET pH response with its changing ionization state and a strongly positive composite-bead surface (bead isoelectric point reported at pH 9.3; BCM char alone at pH 2.6).
- At 10 mg/L initial concentration and 90 min, the abstract reports 99.13% removal TET and 90% MET; the main body reports a maximum TET removal of 99.52% under the 10 mg/L test. Flag the TET percentage discrepancy (99.13% vs 99.52%). 
- Increasing initial concentration from 10 to 400 mg/L decreased removal percentage but increased uptake. At 400 mg/L and the stated optimal conditions, reported empirical capacities were 960.778 mg/g for TET and 691.325 mg/g for MET (abstract and conclusion). These figures are measured at high initial concentrations and low adsorbent dose (~0.267 g/L); they are not low-concentration wastewater performance.
- At 400 mg/L, the isotherm table (Table 3, PDF p. 9) gives Langmuir fitted qmax 990.099 mg/g for TET (R² 0.996) and 666.66 mg/g for MET (R² 0.997), compared with empirical capacities 960.778 and 691.325 mg/g. The fitted model underestimates the MET empirical value while overestimating TET. Other models also gave high fits: Temkin R² 0.997 for TET; Redlich–Peterson 0.996 TET; Sips 0.987 TET/0.985 MET; Toth 0.963 TET/0.991 MET. Do not claim one unique model/mechanism solely from R².
- Surface-area comparisons and swelling/stability visuals appear in Table 1 and Fig. 4 (PDF pp. 4–5): swelling reportedly peaked at pH 3 at 12,633.33% vs 3,600% for unmodified CS/PEI beads; the beads had modest pH-related mass/recovery-rate shifts over 10 days (the text reports about 2.76% for composite beads and 2.5% for CS/PEI beads).
- Kinetic Table 2 (PDF p. 8): PSO qcal 204.081 mg/g (TET, R² 0.998) and 188.67 mg/g (MET, R² 0.992). PFO fits were weaker (R² 0.937 and 0.942). The article also reports high Avrami fits (overall R² 0.939 TET and 0.990 MET; staged Avrami fits up to 0.999) and diffusion-model evidence for multiple rate steps. Its kinetic experimental conditions are 50 mg/L, not the 400 mg/L isotherm maximum-capacity experiment; the ~186 mg/g TET/~158 mg/g MET practical uptake values in the kinetics discussion therefore need to remain separate from the 960.778/691.325 mg/g high-concentration figures.
- No real-water/wastewater matrix experiment was reported in the supplied main text.

### Mechanism, reuse and limitations
- Authors propose electrostatic interactions, π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions, hydrogen bonding and Lewis acid–base interactions, based on pH/zeta-potential response and functional groups from FTIR/EDX. These are plausible proposed mechanisms; individual contributions were not isolated quantitatively.
- Regeneration section 3.16 (PDF p. 11) is internally inconsistent with other parts of the article: it refers to 11 cycles, states TET removal declined from 96.7% to 89.81% “over the two cycles,” and reports 92.86% total effectiveness; elsewhere in the same section the text says TET removal reached 63.22% after eleven cycles (65.37% of the starting value). Preserve this conflict; do not present a single settled regeneration-retention value until resolved.
- Regeneration is described as repeated water/methanol washing; exact solvent proportions and mass balance are referred to the SI. The paper itself notes solvent/post-treatment cost, active-site saturation, bead dissociation and spent-adsorbent disposal as practical limitations.
- The article’s capacity/model tables and the prose mix empirical values, fitted qmax, and kinetic fitted capacities at different initial concentrations. All need clear labels in any comparison table.

### Review use
Useful for the biopolymer-based composite category and for discussing recoverable bead architecture. The exceptional capacities should be presented alongside the 400 mg/L initial concentration, dose, pH and batch conditions; the paper does not establish equivalent performance in real wastewater or economics at scale.

## 3. Masrura et al. (2024) — solvent-modified sewage-sludge biochar for human urine

### Material / process framing
- Environmental Pollution 360 (2024), article 124588; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envpol.2024.124588.
- Sewage-sludge biochar (SS) supplied by the UK Biochar Research Centre, pyrolysed at 550 or 700 °C. The main paper refers to Supplementary Table S1 for pyrolysis details.
- Surface modifications screened include dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ionic liquids and deep eutectic solvents (DES). The four selected variants are CAS-modified SS550/SS700 and DMSO-modified SS550/SS700. The paper identifies CAS as CA:sucrose in one passage; its complete composition and preparation details are referred to SI.
- Specific surface area of pristine SS700 was 32.21 m²/g. Following modification it decreased to 16.99 m²/g (CAS) and 20.27 m²/g (DMSO), while average pore diameters rose from 5.03 nm to 5.67 nm (CAS) and 7.37 nm (DMSO). Modified biochars had lower water contact angles (all reported biochar contact angles 14.12–66.41°), consistent with increased hydrophilicity; FTIR showed stronger oxygen-containing functional groups and DMSO-derived sulfur signal. These data imply functionalization changes pore accessibility and wettability rather than a simple increase in BET area.
- Proposed process has two stages: modified biochar preferentially removes antibiotics from urine; a separate pristine biochar then captures nutrients. This study’s matrix is human urine, not municipal/hospital wastewater effluent.

### Conditions and targets
- Five antibiotic targets: azithromycin (AZ), ciprofloxacin (CPX), sulfamethoxazole (SMX), trimethoprim (TMP) and tetracycline (TC). Natural nutrients were present in the fresh urine; antibiotics were spiked.
- Fresh human urine was diluted with DI water at 1:1 to imitate toilet-flushed urine. Experiments were run in the dark to avoid possible photodegradation.
- Isotherms: 20 mL diluted urine; biochar dose range 0.1–20 g/L; all five antibiotics at 100 or 1,000 µg/L each; 30 ± 1 °C, 180 rpm, 24 h equilibrium.
- Kinetics: 5 g/L biochar in 20 mL diluted urine; antibiotic concentrations 100–1,000 µg/L; 30 ± 1 °C and 180 rpm. Figure 2 (PDF p. 6) plots kinetics for SS700 CAS with pH 7.
- pH tests: 40 mg biochar in 40 mL diluted urine (1 g/L), each antibiotic at 25 µg/L; pH 3–11, 1 M HCl/NaOH, 30 ± 1 °C, 24 h.
- Regeneration loading: pH 7, 24 h, 1,000 µg/L antibiotic, 5 g/L biochar and 30 ± 1 °C. Loaded biochar was vacuum-dried 24 h at 40 °C and shaken with isopropanol at 5–20 g biochar/L isopropanol for 24 h at 180 rpm; rinsed with distilled water and dried overnight before reuse. Five adsorption–desorption cycles were evaluated. Each experiment was in triplicate.

### Adsorption capacities and quality caveats
- Authors report approximate maximum model capacities in the **µg/g** range, consistent with the low µg/L starting concentrations rather than the mg/L-scale tests common in many high-qmax biochar papers:
  - AZ: 196.08 µg/g, SS700 CAS.
  - TMP: 370.37 µg/g, SS700 CAS.
  - TC: 833.33 µg/g, SS550 DMSO.
  - SMX: about 81 µg/g, SS700 DMSO.
  - CPX: about 263 µg/g, SS550 CAS.
- Table 1 (PDF p. 5) reports the SS700 CAS isotherm parameters in detail: AZ Qm 196.08 µg/g (R² 0.99), TMP 370.37 µg/g (R² 0.91), SMX 80.00 µg/g (R² 0.89), CPX 120.48 µg/g (R² 0.81), and **negative Langmuir Qm for TC (−151.52 µg/g; R² 0.58)**. The pristine SS700 comparator has negative fitted Langmuir Qm for all five antibiotics. Negative fitted capacities are physically unusable; do not quote them as true capacities. The maximum TC value from SS550 DMSO and the other cross-material values are given in the body/abstract but detailed variants are in SI.
- Langmuir and Freundlich both vary widely in fit quality across conditions: paper reports R² ranges 0.42–0.99 for Langmuir and 0.51–0.99 for Freundlich. Several negative isotherm constants/capacities show that a model does not describe all material–antibiotic pairs adequately.
- Authors say about 90% of antibiotics were removed in stage 1 under selected conditions, leaving the second stage for nutrient uptake. At pH 7, modified biochar generally showed strong performance with antibiotic speciation and surface interactions proposed as drivers. The abstract gives all five maximum values, while Table 1 specifically shows some fits for SS700 CAS are negative—retain the distinction between the best material for a particular antibiotic and results from one specific material.
- For nutrients, the paper reports the highest ammonia adsorption capacity as 60 mg N/g on SS550 biochar during stage 2; phosphate adsorption was low and some Langmuir phosphate capacities were negative. Nutrient results are process context, not antibiotic capacity values.
- The study explicitly notes its µg/L antibiotic spikes were selected to reflect urine concentrations, so capacities in µg/g are lower than earlier studies that used initial concentrations of 120–200 mg/L. Do not rank these µg/g capacities against high-concentration mg/g qmax values without a clear concentration/matrix caveat.

### Mechanisms, regeneration, economics and applicability
- Authors propose hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions associated with introduced –OH/–COOH groups, plus π–π interactions between antibiotic aromatic structures and biochar domains. Surface charge was negative across the tested pH range and became more negative as pH increased; the reported pH response is consistent with changing antibiotic speciation and surface functional groups. These are proposed interactions, not independently quantified contributions.
- Solvent regeneration used isopropanol at 5–20 g biochar/L isopropanol. The article reports reduced reuse performance over successive cycles; after cycle 5, CPX and TMP regeneration efficiencies were below 20%, while AZ, SMX and TC were around 40–50%. Authors do not recommend use of regenerated biochar beyond three cycles. The abstract’s phrasing “<30% removal capacity after three cycles” does not cleanly match the body’s cycle-five antibiotic-specific figures; flag the different metrics/time points.
- Regeneration efficiencies for nutrients were poorer: phosphate was not extracted after cycle 2 under the tested procedure. The paper proposes solvent accumulation and unidentified byproduct formation on biochar as causes of declining regeneration.
- The study has no full production cost, solvent recovery/economic balance, energy inventory or life-cycle assessment. Isopropanol regeneration and modifier synthesis/handling need inclusion in any future techno-economic/environmental comparison.
- A real urine matrix was used, but it was diluted 1:1 and spiked with known antibiotics; this is stronger matrix relevance than DI water but not an untreated sample with known native antibiotic concentrations and is not evidence of full wastewater-effluent performance.

### Review use
Useful as a special application in selective removal before nutrient recovery in source-separated urine. Use the low µg/L starting conditions and µg/g capacities as a distinct concentration/matrix regime. Do not mix the urine nutrient-recovery system directly into a conventional wastewater-effluent benchmark table without a visible matrix qualifier.

## Cross-paper synthesis

1. **Selectivity and maximum capacity are distinct objectives.** Jiao et al. show an OTC-specific imprinting approach and report a fitted maximum capacity of 67.89 mg/g; the strongest reason to include it is the imprinting factor/interference evidence and magnetic recovery, not a standalone capacity ranking.
2. **Architecture and high reported capacities require context.** Mosaffa et al. report empirical capacities of 960.778 mg/g TET and 691.325 mg/g MET at 400 mg/L initial concentration and ~0.267 g/L adsorbent. This cannot be represented as trace-wastewater removal; kinetic capacities at 50 mg/L are much lower.
3. **Concentration and matrix must be preserved.** Masrura et al. deliberately test 100–1,000 µg/L in diluted, spiked urine, producing capacities in µg/g. That study should not be dismissed because the numbers are smaller, nor directly compared with mg/L-scale isotherm tests.
4. **Reusability claims are not yet settled.** Jiao’s main text reports 9.36% binding reduction over seven cycles. Mosaffa’s main paper conflicts on cycle count/retained performance; Masrura reports weak regeneration after repeated isopropanol cycles and discourages use beyond three cycles.
5. **No apples-to-apples ranking yet.** Target antibiotics, initial concentration, pH, adsorbent dose, matrix, model, and metric differ. Keep fitted qmax, experimental uptake, percentage removal, selectivity/imprinting factor, regeneration efficiency and nutrient uptake in distinct fields.
