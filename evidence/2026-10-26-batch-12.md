## A. Oliveira et al. (2024) — green-functionalized MWCNTs for CIP and OFL adsorption
*Journal of Cleaner Production* 456, 142414. DOI: 10.1016/j.jclepro.2024.142414. Existing candidate CORP-022. Primary experimental paper.

### Preparation
Commercial multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs; initially reported as containing ~9% hydroxyl/carboxyl groups) were functionalized using iron sulfate heptahydrate (1.5% w/v) and eucalyptus-leaf extract as a reducing agent. Extract was prepared from 30 g eucalyptus leaves in ultrapure water, stirred for 1 h at 80 °C. The CNTs, extract and metal solution were stirred at 160 rpm and 25 °C for 24 h; the product was washed, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and dried at 50 °C. The resulting material is MWCNT-E (CNTs with eucalyptus extract and iron particles on their surface). The paper's “green” label applies to the functionalization route, not to manufacture of the original CNTs.

### Adsorption design and single-component data
Equilibrium batch tests used 10 mL antibiotic solution, initial concentration 0.05–1.5 mmol/L, adsorbent concentration 1.5 g/L, agitation 200 rpm for 6 h, and 25, 35 or 50 °C. CIP and ofloxacin (OFL) were quantified by HPLC. The paper does not report actual wastewater or a regeneration/column test in the main article.

Table 3 (PDF p. 6) reports experimental q_exp (mmol/g):
- CIP: 0.361 at 25 °C; 0.360 at 35 °C; 0.433 at 50 °C.
- OFL: 0.413 at 25 °C; 0.416 at 35 °C; 0.457 at 50 °C.
Corresponding fitted Langmuir q_max (mmol/g): CIP 0.397, 0.358, 0.440; OFL 0.407, 0.419, 0.439. The authors identify Sips as the best fit based on R²/AICc, while Langmuir q_max is closest to q_exp. Do not treat Sips q_S, fitted Langmuir q_max and observed q_exp as interchangeable.

Temperature had only a modest effect, with slightly higher experimental capacities at 50 °C. The reported single-component thermodynamic results (Table 5, PDF p. 7) were ΔH = 16.75 kJ/mol (CIP) and 4.30 kJ/mol (OFL), with negative ΔG values across the tested temperatures. The authors interpret this as favorable/endothermic adsorption and suggest possible chemisorption; a thermodynamic fit by itself is not proof of a unique chemical mechanism.

### Binary adsorption and machine learning
CIP/OFL mixtures were tested at molar fractions 1:1, 1:3 and 3:1, with total initial concentrations 0.1–3.0 mmol/L at 25, 35 and 50 °C. At 50 °C the reported mixture uptakes were 0.958 mmol/g CIP and 0.872 mmol/g OFL (PDF p. 7); at 25 °C they were 0.909 and 0.850 mmol/g. The paper calculates q_e'/q_e ratios of 2.2 for CIP and 1.9 for OFL and interprets the outcome as net synergistic behavior, while also discussing competing access to sites and stronger affinity for CIP. Preserve both reported observations without implying that the mixture is purely synergistic at all compositions and concentrations. The Langmuir–Freundlich model was reported as the best overall binary equilibrium fit (Table 6, PDF pp. 8–9).

The paper compared feedforward ANN and ANFIS predictions with fitted equilibrium data. ANFIS showed R values around 0.9999 (CIP) and 0.9998 (OFL) and low reported prediction error against the dataset. This is internal model validation on a relatively small experimental dataset, not independent external validation or proof of generalization to real wastewater.

### Mechanism and evidence boundaries
FTIR/XPS changes after adsorption, including changes in C–N/C–OH-associated signals and elemental composition, were interpreted as supporting surface interaction with CIP/OFL. SEM/EDS showed changes in dispersion and the relative iron signal after adsorption. The article suggests intermolecular interactions involving functional groups on the antibiotics and CNT-E. These measurements support association but do not isolate a single exclusive mechanism. No actual wastewater, product-yield, CNT/iron leaching, regeneration cycle, spent-adsorbent disposal, or quantified life-cycle cost/impact is presented in this paper.

## B. Yang et al. (2025) — review of iron-modified biochar for antibiotic adsorption and degradation
*Journal of Environmental Management* 380, 124875. DOI: 10.1016/j.jenvman.2025.124875. Existing candidate CORP-029. Review article, not primary experimental evidence.

### Scope and usable synthesis
The review covers zero-valent-iron biochar (nZVI@BC), iron (hydro)oxide-modified biochar and mixed-metal/iron-oxide biochar; it summarizes preparation routes and adsorption studies, then separately reviews Fenton-like and photocatalytic degradation. Table 1 (PDF p. 3) compares synthesis routes and surface area/pore volume across studies; Table 2 (PDF pp. 6–7) compiles antibiotic adsorption capacities, conditions, proposed mechanisms and models.

For the adsorption-mechanism narrative, the authors group proposed interactions into π–π electron-donor–acceptor interaction, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction/repulsion, hydrophobic interactions and pore filling (Section 3.3, PDF pp. 5–6). pH influences both antibiotic speciation and adsorbent surface charge. The review also notes that iron particles can block biochar pores, and that modification can lower surface area for some materials, so increased performance is not universal. These are synthesis-level claims and should be checked against the cited primary paper before using any specific mechanism or capacity as a quantitative observation.

### Environmental risk and regeneration
Section 5 (PDF p. 9) says magnetic separation is commonly used to recover iron-modified biochar before elution and reuse, and cites a report of <5% decrease in doxycycline and ciprofloxacin adsorption after 10 adsorption–desorption cycles. It also warns that desorption can cause secondary pollution and says environmental safety of different iron-modified biochars should be assessed before environmental deployment. Treat the 10-cycle figure as a secondary citation and verify the underlying primary paper before entering it as primary quantitative evidence. Used-media disposal and possible environmental impacts should remain explicit in the processing–cost–performance analysis.

### Strict scope boundary
The article covers both adsorption and oxidative/photocatalytic degradation. Only adsorption-only data belong in the review's adsorption evidence matrix. Results that use persulfate/peroxymonosulfate, H2O2, photo-generated radicals or catalytic degradation must not be labelled adsorption capacity or used as adsorption-only removal data. The review's Table 3 (PDF p. 9) is degradation literature and should be excluded from adsorption-performance extraction. As a review, this paper's tables also must not be counted as additional independent experimental studies alongside their cited primary papers.

## C. Yin et al. (2024) — electroactive UiO-66/CNT nanofiltration membrane
*Journal of Hazardous Materials* 475, 134944. DOI: 10.1016/j.jhazmat.2024.134944. Existing candidate CORP-030. Primary experiment, but primarily a membrane-separation study and out of scope for the core adsorption-capacity matrix.

### Fabrication and membrane result
UiO-66 was grown with activated CNTs in DMF using ZrCl4 and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid at 120 °C for 48 h; powders were washed six times with DMF/ethanol and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The MOF/CNT nanofiller was embedded in PVDF by nanobubbles-mediated non-solvent-induced phase separation (NIPS). The selected UiO-66/CNTs-5 membrane used 48.7 wt% nanofiller loading and a 400 μm active layer. The process uses significant organic solvent and energy for synthesis/washing; “green”/low impact needs a life-cycle boundary if compared against adsorbents.

The membrane had reported mean effective pore size 0.32 nm and molecular weight cut-off 375 Da. TC rejection reached 99.3%, with permeability 101.3–105.7 L h−1 m−2 bar−1 (PDF pp. 3–5). These are membrane rejection/permeance metrics—not mg/g adsorption capacity. Rejection was linked mainly to size exclusion plus Donnan effects, rather than adsorbent uptake alone (Fig. 4, PDF pp. 5–6).

### Self-cleaning, water tests and economics
Simulated municipal secondary effluent contained five common antibiotics at 5 μg/L according to Fig. 7 caption (PDF p. 10); the selected membrane was operated for 260 h. Antibiotic rejection remained stable and bacteria were rejected; the paper also tested natural Yangtze River surface water for about seven days. Electricity-driven self-cleaning was tested at 2 mA/cm² for 5 min and was attributed to ROS-mediated degradation of accumulated organic/bacterial foulants (Figs. 5–7, PDF pp. 8–10). The authors report energy consumption 77% lower and cleaning cost 72% lower than commercial NF270 on their membrane-process comparison (Fig. 7c, PDF p. 9), and negligible Zr in permeate. This is an economic comparison of a membrane treatment process, not a direct unit-cost comparison with adsorption-only processes.

### Review decision
Keep this as an adjacent membrane-technology comparator/background citation, not a core adsorbent-class primary adsorption result. Do not translate 99.3% rejection into adsorption capacity or use electrically driven foulant degradation as antibiotic adsorption. It may support a brief boundary/alternative-technology discussion if the review includes such context.

## Batch 12 synthesis
1. MWCNT-E is directly relevant to Stage 4 engineered carbon adsorption, especially because it tests binary antibiotic mixtures and machine-learning model fit. It reports mmol/g and lacks regeneration or real-water validation.
2. Iron-modified biochar review helps structure the mechanism and environmental-risk narrative, but its quantitative tables must not duplicate primary studies; separate adsorption from catalytic degradation.
3. The UiO-66/CNT membrane paper is technically interesting and has long-run/spiked-water results, but its core metric is membrane rejection/permeance and its cleaning step is ROS-mediated degradation. Exclude it from the adsorption-capacity evidence matrix.
4. Across all three papers, no new harmonized adsorption TEA/LCA was available. Process intensification, solvent/chemical consumption, recovery, leaching and end-of-life should remain visible gaps.
