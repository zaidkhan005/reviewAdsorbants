## A. Liu et al. (2025) — sustainable CTC removal using kaolin–alginate beads
*Royal Society Open Science* 12, 250439. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.250439. Existing candidate **DISC-007**. Primary study.

### Preparation and processing continuum
Kaolin (KN) was used as received. For KN@Alg, 2 g sodium alginate was dispersed in 100 mL distilled water for 2 h, then 2 g KN added, homogenized, and the suspension extruded into 0.5% CaCl2. The gelled beads were thoroughly rinsed; beads were about 1 mm diameter (PDF pp. 3–4, Fig. 1). This is a processed clay–biopolymer composite, not raw clay; it improves powder recovery and handling but adds polymer, crosslinking salt, mixing, bead formation and rinse water.

### Batch adsorption and models
Tests spanned 20–100 mg/L CTC, pH 2–12, 298–328 K, 0.4–4 g/L dose and 5 min–7 h contact time (PDF pp. 4–5). Isotherm/kinetic tests were at pH 6 and 298 K with 0.8 g/L AC versus 4 g/L KN or KN@Alg. Observed isotherm capacities were AC 102.96 mg/g, KN 42.76 mg/g and KN@Alg 68.74 mg/g (PDF pp. 10–12, Table 2). At 4 g/L, KN and KN@Alg removal efficiencies were 55.04% and 68.44%; AC reached 79.45% removal at lower dose in the reported parameter tests (PDF p. 8). Langmuir fitted qmax values were 176.151, 55.178 and 79.679 mg/g for AC, KN and KN@Alg respectively. Keep these fitted values separate from the observed capacities.
The kinetic table (PDF p. 10, Table 1) reports experimental qe of 104.720 mg/g AC, 45.300 mg/g KN and 68.700 mg/g KN@Alg. The KN@Alg PSO-calculated qe is 144.307 mg/g with R²=0.838, whereas PFO qe is 70.616 mg/g and is much closer to observation. This is a model fit discrepancy; do not quote the PSO number as observed uptake or claim PSO by itself proves chemisorption. Thermodynamic Table 3 (PDF p. 13) gives KN@Alg ΔG° negative at 298 K but positive at 308 and 318 K, so spontaneous adsorption is temperature-limited by the authors' own values.

### Reuse, practical water matrices and cost
The regeneration tests ran four cycles. KN@Alg showed a 14.19% reduction in removal efficiency, compared with reported larger declines for KN and AC (PDF pp. 13–14, Fig. 9).
For the SPE test, 40 beads were packed in a 6 mL cartridge and 5 mg/L CTC solutions in distilled water, tap water and river water were used. After three SPE cycles, reported removal was 96.5%, 94.2% and 93.7%. A continuous column (1.5 cm ID × 15 cm height; Q=1 mL/min) treated 5 mg/L CTC-contaminated river water and maintained 93.6% removal over 20 bed volumes (PDF pp. 6, 14–15, Fig. 10). These are spiked water matrices, not proof of equivalent removal at naturally occurring trace concentrations.
The article estimates cost at 0.095 US$/g for KN@Alg versus 0.15 US$/g for AC, assuming four reuse cycles. However, the electricity price is printed as 0.0175 US$/g, an unusual unit that needs checking before citing the calculation. This is not a full plant-level techno-economic analysis or LCA. Proposed mechanisms include hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interaction, cation exchange/bridging, n–π interactions, surface complexation and pore filling, supported by XPS and other characterization (PDF pp. 15–17); keep individual mechanisms as the authors' interpretations.

## B. Maged et al. (2020) — ciprofloxacin sorption onto natural and acid-activated bentonite
*Environmental Science and Pollution Research* 27, 32980–32997. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-020-09267-1. Existing candidate **DISC-014**. Primary study.

### Preparation and characterisation
Bentonite from Matrouh, Egypt, was dried, crushed, ground and sieved. The sample labelled natural bentonite (NB) was then laboratory-purified using 0.1 N sodium acetate/acetic acid at pH about 5, H2O2, repeated NaCl washes and Milli-Q water washing, so it should not be described as wholly untreated raw clay. Acid-activated bentonite (AAB) was made using 0.5 M HCl, clay:solution ratio 1:2, at 70±1 °C for 4 h, followed by repeated washing to remove chloride and drying at 60 °C for 24 h (PDF pp. 2–3). Acid activation changed oxide composition and morphology and increased BET area by more than twofold, while XRD/FTIR indicated partial structural alteration rather than complete destruction of the layered clay (PDF pp. 5–8).

### Batch capacities, pH and model results
Optimization started from 30 mg/L CIP, with pH 3–9 and dosage 0.1–0.8 g/L; isotherms covered 5–200 mg/L. A dose of 0.2 g/L and pH around 5.5 were used for selected kinetic work. Langmuir capacities were 126.56 mg/g NB and 305.20 mg/g AAB; Table 4 gives experimental qmax values of 126.53 and 300.03 mg/g (PDF pp. 10–12, Tables 3–5). Sorption rose rapidly during the first 30 min (about 80% of total uptake) and equilibrium was reached within 120 min. Experimental kinetic qe was 85.56 mg/g NB and 134.74 mg/g AAB. PSO fits had R²=0.999 and 0.994, but the model does not by itself prove chemisorption.
The paper interprets the pH response as consistent with ciprofloxacin speciation and negative bentonite surface charge, with cation exchange/interlayer complexation, intercalation, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, surface complexation and pore filling supported by XRD, FTIR, SEM and pH evidence.

### Salt, regeneration and fixed-bed column
Increasing NaCl from 0 to 0.5 M reduced removal from 59.8% to 16.1% for NB and 95.3% to 75.5% for AAB (PDF pp. 9–10, Fig. 5), showing significant matrix sensitivity. NaOH (0.1 M) regenerated sorbents better than HCl in five batch cycles: reported performance declined from 59.9% to 48.1% for NB and 97.3% to 88.5% for AAB with NaOH; AAB declined from 96.9% to 58.3% using HCl (PDF pp. 12, Fig. 5).
Fixed-bed column conditions were 5 mm internal diameter × 130 mm glass column, 25 or 50 mg AAB, 10 or 20 mg/L influent, 1.5 or 3 mL/min and pH about 5.5. At 50 mg AAB, 20 mg/L CIP and 1.5 mL/min, first cycle qbed was 97.12 mg/g, total adsorbed amount 4.86 mg, column removal 37.99%, breakthrough time 40 min and total run 400 min (PDF pp. 12–14, Table 6). Increased flow to 3 mL/min reduced qbed to 66.81 mg/g and removal to 36.05%. These fixed-bed figures must not be blended with batch Langmuir qmax.
**Internal table/conclusion discrepancy:** Table 6 gives cycle qbed values 97.12, 61.85 and 28.14 mg/g, summing to 187.11 mg/g; the conclusion states the total capacity across three sequential column cycles was 178.11 mg/g. This remains unresolved and has been recorded as a source caveat. The study's synthetic mg/L tests and 37.99% first-cycle column removal should be retained with their conditions; do not generalize directly to trace-level real wastewater.

## C. Hamid et al. (2025) — waste-based biochar adsorbents for antibiotic pollution
*Environmental Science and Pollution Research* 32, 8643–8669. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-025-36253-2. Existing candidate **CORP-009**. Review/supporting source.

### Scope and reported examples
The review says an in-depth Google Scholar assessment found 88 research articles from 2004–2024 on biochar from waste biomass for antibiotic remediation (PDF pp. 3–4, Fig. 1). It discusses antibiotic toxicity, biomass precursors, biochar preparation, kinetic and isotherm models, thermodynamics and regeneration across multiple antibiotic classes.
Table 1 compiles different adsorbents and reported capacities/removal efficiencies; examples include modified Chinese-herbal-medicine-residue biochar with 80% TC removal at 0.8 g/L and fish-scale-derived carbon with reported Langmuir capacities of 80.826, 86.394 and 108.021 mg/g at 30, 40 and 50 °C (PDF pp. 11–13). These are values transcribed from cited primary studies, not measured by this review; table units/conditions vary and require primary-source checking. The review also emphasizes regeneration and kinetic/isotherm trends in Table 3 (PDF p. 20).

### Practical limitations
The review notes that many experiments are batch studies using high initial antibiotic concentrations, unlike low concentrations in ambient water; it recommends actual wastewater and pilot-plant research. It also discusses decreasing capacity during reuse, energy/cost of regeneration and secondary waste from exhausted adsorbents (PDF pp. 20–23). These support our practical-gap framing but do not constitute a quantified full-cost or lifecycle assessment. The inspected method does not describe a PRISMA-style reproducible systematic-review flow, so describe it as a narrative review/literature survey rather than assume systematic coverage.
