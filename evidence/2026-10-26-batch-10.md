## 1. Atta et al. (2023) — raw rice husk for levofloxacin

### Preparation and characterization
- Rice husk collected in Al-Mishkhab district, Najaf Governorate, Iraq; washed repeatedly with deionized water, room-temperature dried for 24 h, sieved to 1–2 mm and stored.
- No pyrolysis or chemical functionalization: this is raw agricultural biomass.
- Table 1 reports specific surface area 0.328 m²/g, real density 1.48 g/cm³ and bulk density 0.11 g/cm³. SEM shows a coarse surface with grooves/ravines and non-uniform aggregates. FTIR bands shift after LEV loading, and the authors assign contributions to OH, carboxylic/C–O, aliphatic C–H and aromatic groups.
- The pHPZC was determined by salt-addition method over pH 2–8. No leaching, spent-sorbent toxicity or material yield is reported.

### Operating conditions and performance
- General conditions included pH 2–8, initial LEV 25–100 mg/L, rice-husk dose 0.5–12 g per 100 mL (5–120 g/L), particles of 0.25–2 mm, 150 rpm mixing, and room temperature except for temperature experiments. Tests were performed in duplicate.
- Particle-size experiment: 50 mg/L LEV, pH 5, 3 g husk/100 mL, 3 h, 150 rpm. Authors report removal increase of approximately 56% to 87% as particle size is reduced (PDF pp. 5–6, Fig. 4).
- Dose effect: increasing rice-husk dose from 5 to 120 g/L raised removal from 27.8% to 93.8% (PDF p. 6, Fig. 5). **120 g/L is an exceptionally high dose** and is crucial to the practical processing–cost–performance comparison.
- Contact time: fast initial uptake in the first ~10 min; results describe equilibrium as approached around 120 min, with insignificant subsequent fluctuations. The paper also reports that more than 80% removal of all concentrations was reached in the first hour.
- Initial concentration: as initial LEV increased from 25 to 100 mg/L, reported removal decreased from ~99% to ~90% (PDF pp. 6–7, Fig. 7). The abstract's “99%” should not be generalized across all conditions.
- pH: removal changes little from pH 5 to 8. Under pH below 5, the paper reports removal increases with pH and attributes it partly to reduced electrostatic repulsion between protonated LEV and positively charged husk.
- Temperature: reported removal rises from 52% to 70% as temperature rises from 25 to 40 °C (PDF p. 8, Fig. 9). This is presented as consistent with endothermic adsorption. Keep this temperature comparison attached to its individual experiment conditions rather than treating it as the universal optimum.

### Isotherm, kinetics and thermodynamics
- Isotherms: 25, 50, 75 and 100 mg/L LEV, pH 5, 9 g rice husk per 100 mL (90 g/L), 150 rpm and 3 h. Langmuir was selected over Freundlich by reported R² (0.95 vs 0.93). Langmuir qmax = 2.47 mg/g and b = 0.073; Freundlich K = 0.18 and 1/n = 0.763 (PDF p. 8, Table 1). This is a fitted qmax, not direct observed loading.
- Kinetic table (PDF p. 10, Table 3): intraparticle diffusion had the strongest reported fit (R² = 0.96, ERRSQ = 0.01, ARE = 0.25, χ² approximately 0.00). The paper reports weaker PFO fit (R² ≈ 0.19), PSO R² ≈ 0.89, and Elovich R² ≈ 0.92. Table layout/parameter labels are imperfectly rendered and should be checked before reproducing individual rate constants. A best fit to the intraparticle model does not alone establish that intraparticle diffusion is the sole rate-limiting step.
- Thermodynamics, Table 2 (PDF p. 9): ΔH° = +37.117 kJ/mol, ΔS° = +125.134 J/(mol·K), ΔG° = −0.197, −0.823, −1.312 and −2.188 kJ/mol at 298, 303, 308 and 313 K, respectively. Authors interpret this as spontaneous/endothermic adsorption favoured at higher temperature.

### Scope and practical gap
- Batch experiments used prepared aqueous LEV solutions. No real wastewater sample, column/pilot validation, regeneration cycle, numerical material cost, full TEA/LCA or spent-sorbent management route is reported in this article.
- The paper describes RRH as sustainable/low-cost, but that is not an empirical cost comparison. The high dose requirement and low qmax are central to judging its position at the raw-material end of the continuum.

### Review use
Useful Stage 1 evidence because the material is raw and minimally processed. Present the 2.47 mg/g fitted capacity alongside the exceptionally high dose and the dose-dependent removal curve. Do not call the sorbent cost-effective based on the paper's claim alone.

## 2. Zhang & Wu (2022) — N-doped reduced graphene oxide for trace antibiotics

### Material and synthesis
- Targets: sulfamethoxazole (SMZ), levofloxacin (LVX), clindamycin (CLI), tetracycline (TC), penicillin G sodium (PEN), chloramphenicol (CHL).
- Graphite was oxidized with concentrated HNO3, K2S2O8 and P2O5 at 80 °C for 6 h; then further treated using concentrated H2SO4/H3PO4 and KMnO4, raised through 35–60 °C to 98 °C, quenched with H2O2 and washed with HCl. GO was ultrasonically exfoliated (100 W/30 min). GO dispersion was mixed with ammonia, autoclaved at 180 °C for 24 h, then centrifuged, washed, dried at 60 °C/12 h, ground and sieved to make N-rGO (PDF pp. 2–3).
- XPS reports pyridinic N 50.91%, pyrrolic N 26.49%, graphitic N 22.60% of the N 1s components; FTIR reports OH/COOH and other oxygen-bearing signals. Fig. 1 (PDF p. 4) shows SEM/TEM morphology and spectra.
- This is a graphene-derived N-doped nanosorbent, **not a biochar**. The synthesis uses strong acids/oxidants, extended washing, sonication, and autoclaving; no material yield, chemical recovery, energy input, unit cost or LCA is reported.

### Trace-feed setup and kinetics
- Methods state that 80 mL of each antibiotic solution was combined with 20 mL N-rGO dispersion reported at 0.5 mg/L; shaking at 120 rpm, initial temperature 25±1 °C. Initial concentration series 0.1, 1, 10 and 20 μg/L; pH 3–12; reported dosage range 40–400 μg/L (PDF p. 3).
- Fig. 2 (PDF p. 5) states kinetic comparison at C0 = 10 μg/L and 288.15 K. Authors report adsorption >80%; TC was the fastest (within 30 min) and clindamycin the slowest (within 90 min). The article's broader summary says the process completes within 60 min.
- Table 1 lists experimental qe (mg/g): SMZ 87.30, LVX 88.10, CLI 82.39, TC 90.21, PEN 85.86, CHL 80.58. Calculated PFO qe values are 82–106 mg/g and PFO R² ranges 0.9579–0.9892; second-order R² ranges 0.9291–0.9834. The authors prefer the first-order fit.
- **Mass-balance check needed:** if the representative setup is 80 mL of 10 μg/L feed plus 20 mL at 0.5 mg/L N-rGO, the absolute qe ceiling at complete removal is about 80 mg/g; some experimental qe entries exceed this, so the stated feed/dosage units or calculation basis need clarification. Do not silently reconcile this.

### Isotherms and fitted capacities
- Table 2 (PDF p. 6) reports Langmuir fitted q_m: SMZ 574.71, LVX 561.80, CLI 1265.82, TC 662.25, PEN 917.43 and CHL 662.25 mg/g; Langmuir R² 0.9989–0.9996. Authors prefer Langmuir over Freundlich.
- The concentration range is stated as 0.1–20 μg/L and adsorbent dose as 40–400 μg/L. These fitted q_m values are far above the maximum loading implied by the stated concentration-to-dose ratios, especially the 1265.82 mg/g for CLI. R² alone cannot validate a saturation-capacity extrapolation when the equilibrium loading range is so limited.
- Therefore, retain the capacities as *author-reported, fitted and flagged*; do not use them for rank ordering or as reliable directly measured working capacities unless the source's concentration/dose units are verified.

### pH, competing solutes and regeneration
- pH 3–12 was examined; authors state the response depends on antibiotic structure/speciation. Temperature tests covered 288.15, 298.15, 308.15 and 318.15 K; they interpret adsorption as exothermic and generally declining with higher temperature.
- Ions included Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, HCO3−, CO3²− and NO3−; the methods report a concentration of 100 mmol/L for coexistence tests. The article states inorganic ions had little inhibitory effect. Humic acid was varied from 0.5–10 mg/L; at 10 mg/L, adsorption capacity reportedly fell by up to 30% (PDF p. 8, Fig. 3d).
- Six antibiotics were also tested in mixed systems (Fig. 3f): competitive adsorption is relevant, but the precise per-analyte changes are figure-based and need visual extraction before quoting values.
- Regeneration: 0.1 mol/L HCl used to elute saturated N-rGO; 0.1 mol/L NaOH used for regeneration, followed by DI-water washing. Authors report the adsorption capacity decreases by no more than 25% after eight cycles (PDF p. 8, Fig. 3e). Regenerant volumes, sorbent loss, concentrated eluate management and associated cost are not reported.

### Review use
This paper is useful for a Stage 4 adjacent class: N-doped graphene-derived carbon, trace-concentration tests, natural organic matter interference and reuse. It is also an example of why very high fitted capacities need a mass-balance check, and why strong oxidants, acids, washing, and autoclave energy belong in the processing-cost analysis. Do not merge N-rGO into the biochar category.

## 3. Jiao et al. (2025) — review of carbon nanomaterials for antibiotic/heavy-metal co-removal

### Scope and source type
- This is a literature review in *Journal of Hazardous Materials* 489 (2025), 137566, DOI 10.1016/j.jhazmat.2025.137566. It considers carbon nanotubes, graphene, resins and other nanocomposites for combined antibiotic and heavy-metal removal in water–soil systems; its literature trend analysis covers 2010–2023.
- It is relevant to the manuscript's multicomponent chemistry, engineered carbon, regeneration and techno-economic sections, but should remain a supporting review, not a primary adsorption-study record.

### Mechanism synthesis for combined systems
- Section 4.2/Fig. 4 (PDF pp. 10–11) classifies major interactions as bridging, electrostatic shielding, competition and spatial site blocking.
- The review describes bridging/electrostatic shielding as potential promoters of co-adsorption, while competition, displacement and pore/site blocking often inhibit uptake. The actual outcome depends on sorbent, antibiotic, metal, pH and binding-site chemistry; do not generalize a binary-system mechanism to a single-solute test.

### Regeneration and cost framework
- Section 6.1 (PDF pp. 13–14) discusses regeneration and reuse; it highlights that desorption efficiencies can differ between antibiotic and metal in the same system and that sequential recovery may be useful. It warns that poorly managed spent sorbents and regenerant liquids may create secondary contamination.
- The review defines total adsorption cost as:
  **Adsorption cost = Energy cost + Chemical cost + Transportation cost + Capital cost.**
  It further describes “adsorbent cost-performance” as the dollar cost of producing/using sorbent to remove a unit mass of contaminant, at the stated theoretical adsorption-capacity point.
- It reports that chemical and energy costs can represent over 80% of laboratory-scale carbon nanosorbent production cost in the literature it reviewed. This is not a universal percentage or a harmonized cost benchmark.
- Section 6.2 and conclusions (PDF pp. 14–15) state that an industrial-scale assessment should also include factory operation, carbon separation from water, regenerant/wastewater treatment and disposal. The review also notes the lack of standardized cost estimation and lifecycle/techno-economic analyses.

### Review use
Use as secondary support for the need to quantify co-contaminant effects, regeneration waste streams, and full process boundaries. Trace any numerical capacity/cost comparison back to its cited primary study and supplementary data. This review does not replace primary papers in our evidence base.

## Batch 10 cross-paper synthesis

1. **Processing continuum:** raw rice husk is minimally processed but gives a modest fitted qmax (2.47 mg/g) and relies on very high doses; N-rGO is substantially more engineered and has high fitted capacities, but uses strong oxidants/acids and long thermal/mechanical processing without a cost/LCA.
2. **Do not rank model capacities blindly:** the N-rGO q_m values are not consistent with the stated very low feed concentrations and adsorbent-dose range. The mass balance needs clarification. The raw rice husk qmax is explicitly model-fitted and should stay separate from percent removal.
3. **Real-world relevance:** the N-rGO study's trace-level feeds are useful, but its ion tests and secondary matrix results do not constitute a real-wastewater pilot; the rice-husk study uses prepared aqueous solutions only. The review gives cross-study context, not new primary data.
4. **Regeneration:** N-rGO reports <=25% loss by eight cycles with acid/base regeneration, but regeneration reagent and waste-stream costs are absent; the rice-husk study has no regeneration evidence.
5. **Techno-economics:** none of the two primary papers in this batch provides a quantitative unit treatment cost or LCA. The Jiao review offers a useful cost-boundary framework, not paper-specific verified costing.
6. **Decision:** retain the two primary papers provisionally with explicit caveats; retain Jiao et al. as supporting background only rather than a new primary-study entry.
