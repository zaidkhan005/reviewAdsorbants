## A. Liang et al. (2024) — NiFe2O4/biochar composite for tetracycline
*Carbon Research* 3, 2. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44246-023-00094-w. Existing candidate **DISC-030**. Primary study.

### Preparation and processing intensity
Poplar sawdust was washed, dried and carbonized at 700 °C for 1 h to form biochar (BC). For NFO/BC, 0.1 g BC was dispersed in 50 mL water by sonication for 30 min; 0.1 mmol Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.2 mmol Fe(NO3)3·9H2O were added, ammonia was added to pH≈11, then the mixture was hydrothermally treated in a PTFE-lined autoclave at 180 °C for 12 h. The product was centrifuged and washed at least three times with deionized water and ethanol, then vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h (PDF pp. 3–4). This is a more highly processed biochar composite using nickel/iron salts, concentrated ammonia, autoclave heating and repeated solvent washing.

### Characterisation and batch performance
BET surface areas were 422.5 m²/g for BC, 111.6 m²/g for NFO and 356.1 m²/g for NFO/BC; corresponding average pore sizes were 3.16, 7.95 and 3.88 nm, and pore volumes 0.33, 0.21 and 0.27 cm³/g (PDF p. 5, Table 1). The composite saturation magnetization was 15.2 emu/g, which the authors describe as sufficient for magnetic separation.
At initial TC concentration 20 mg/L, increasing dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g/L raised NFO/BC removal from 37% to 93.9%; the authors selected 1.0 g/L as the optimal dose. Maximum reported removal was 92.4% at pH 7; at pH 3 it was 75.5%. Langmuir fitting gave a reported maximum adsorption capacity of 420.41 mg/g (PDF pp. 7–9; Table S1 is in the article's supplementary material, not the uploaded PDF). Isotherms were tested at 5–200 mg/L using 20 mL solution and 20 mg sorbent for 24 h; the kinetic test used 20 mg sorbent with 20 mL of 20 mg/L TC at pH 6 (PDF pp. 3–4, 8–9). The reported capacity is a model-derived qmax, not an observed removal percentage.
### Matrix sensitivity, regeneration and mechanisms
Humic acid substantially reduced removal as its concentration increased. NaCl had little reported effect, but at 0.5% Mg2+ and Ca2+ salt conditions, adsorption capacity decreased to 59.8% and 63.8% of the original condition, respectively (PDF p. 8). After four adsorption–desorption cycles using deionized water for desorption, NFO/BC removal remained about 72% (PDF p. 8). This is a meaningful decline after a short number of cycles, notwithstanding magnetic recoverability.
The authors report a positive ΔH of 31.384 kJ/mol for NFO/BC and negative ΔG values, interpreting the process as endothermic and spontaneous (PDF pp. 9–10; exact temperature-specific values are in supplementary Table S4). Their proposed mechanisms include electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions, metal–TC complexation and pore filling (PDF pp. 10–11). These are the authors' interpretations, not proof that one mechanism dominates in all matrices.
### Limitations relevant to the review
The experiments use prepared/simulated aqueous TC solutions; no real wastewater or continuous fixed-bed test is reported in the uploaded article. The study does not provide a full process cost, life-cycle inventory, nickel/iron leaching assessment or treatment plan for regeneration effluent. Magnetic separation is a handling advantage but does not itself demonstrate lower life-cycle impact.

## B. Pinheiro et al. — amoxicillin adsorption by chitosan and alginate beads
Research Square preprint DOI: https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-4331760/v1, posted 31 May 2024. Existing screening candidate **CORP-023** identifies a later *Environmental Science and Pollution Research* version (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-024-35293-4). The uploaded file is the preprint PDF; the published version has not been compared here.

### Bead preparation and operating window
Chitosan (at least 75% deacetylated) was dissolved in 1% acetic acid at 1 g per 50 mL; alginate was dissolved in ultrapure water at 1 g per 50 mL. Solutions were dripped into separate coagulation baths: 1 M NaOH for chitosan and 20% CaCl2 for alginate. Beads rested for 48 h and were washed to neutral pH (PDF pp. 4–5). These are separate chitosan and alginate bead adsorbents, not a single mixed chitosan–alginate composite, despite the wording of the title.
The amoxicillin concentration used for pH optimisation was 300 mg/L with 1 g adsorbent, with pH adjusted using McIlvaine buffer. Optimum pH was 4 for both bead types. At pH 4, observed capacity was 80.4±0.2 mg/g for chitosan and 74.2±0.3 mg/g for alginate. Alginate beads reportedly dissolved above pH 6, limiting their usable pH range (PDF pp. 8–9, Table 1).
### Kinetics and isotherms
The reported kinetic uptake was 78.6 mg/g for chitosan after 200 min and 71.3 mg/g for alginate after 250 min (PDF pp. 9–10). The paper interprets Boyd's external-diffusion model as best for chitosan and Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion as better for alginate. Table 2 also reports a Weber–Morris fitted q∞ of 136.75±8.2 mg/g for alginate, much higher than the 71.3 mg/g kinetic uptake stated in the text/conclusion; record this as an unresolved model-versus-observation discrepancy.
Isotherms used 50–300 mg/L amoxicillin at 278, 293 and 308 K (5, 20 and 35 °C) (PDF pp. 5–6). Langmuir fitted qmax values for chitosan were 98.4±0.8, 102.2±0.9 and 94.4±0.7 mg/g at 278, 293 and 308 K; for alginate they were 60.8±1.2, 104.3±0.8 and 99.0±0.7 mg/g (PDF p. 12, Table 3). However, the authors state that Freundlich generally fits better, with R² values around 0.990–0.996 compared with the weaker Langmuir fits in some conditions. Therefore, Langmuir qmax should remain explicitly labelled as a fitted estimate and not be treated as the preferred model-supported capacity without checking the data and the published version.
Thermodynamic values in Table 4 are negative ΔG at all three temperatures, and positive ΔH (20.6±0.8 kJ/mol for chitosan, 13.8±0.4 kJ/mol for alginate), interpreted by the authors as spontaneous and endothermic adsorption (PDF pp. 13–14). The experiments use high mg/L amoxicillin solutions rather than trace concentrations or actual wastewater; the stock was prepared from a commercial capsule described as containing 574 mg amoxicillin trihydrate (PDF p. 4), so the analytical impact of formulation excipients is not clearly addressed in the inspected methods.
### Processing–cost–performance implications
The authors emphasize that making separate beads with simple acid/base or CaCl2 coagulation uses fewer synthesis steps than some highly engineered comparators cited in their discussion. However, no quantified cost, energy, regeneration-cycle or real-water study is reported in this preprint. Their claims of comparative simplicity should be treated qualitatively.

## C. Sousa et al. (2025) — chitosan-based materials and antibiotic interactions
*International Journal of Molecular Sciences* 26, 11070. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms262211070. Existing candidate **CORP-028**. Supporting review.

The review focuses on chitosan-based adsorption materials for four antibiotic groups/examples: tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, cephalexin and azithromycin. It covers chemical modification, polymer composites, magnetic/porous composites, solution chemistry and computational studies. It is an overview, not a primary experiment; the inspected article does not present a PRISMA-style reproducible search flow. Its Tables 2–3 compile capacities from separate papers under different conditions, so those numbers are discovery aids rather than harmonized rankings (PDF pp. 8–12).
The review explains that chitosan's amino and hydroxyl groups support electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, while its poor mechanical properties and acid instability can motivate crosslinking or composite formation. However, crosslinkers may consume amino active sites, and some chemical crosslinkers (e.g., aldehydes/epoxides) carry toxicity or environmental concerns; less harmful options such as genipin and natural acids are discussed (PDF pp. 6–8).
Across the compiled studies, adsorption varies with antibiotic speciation, pH, functional groups, porosity and material composition. Proposed mechanisms include electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, hydrophobic interactions and host–guest inclusion. A review-table maximum is not evidence of a uniquely established mechanism, and kinetic/isotherm fits should not be interpreted alone as mechanistic proof.
The computational section (PDF pp. 17–19) describes DFT and docking studies as simplified molecular models that can provide qualitative insights. For example, one cited tetracycline/chitosan–ZIF-8 modelling study found binding to be stronger at the ZIF-8 component than the chitosan component. These calculations help interpret experiments but do not substitute for real-water adsorption tests.
The review is useful for classification and citation discovery around chitosan modifications, antibiotic-specific interactions, and experimental/computational evidence. It does not supply a standardized processing-cost-performance comparison, a cross-study life-cycle analysis, or new primary measurements.
